Xu
- Notes that the Jiajing gazetteer had 17 categories, while the Longqing edition had 8; proposes to follow the latter with some slight adjustments, as the smaller number of categories is easier to consult. Notes that the Longqing gazetteer broke with the tradition, followed since Ban Gu, of including a 食貨志, folding that information into the 版籍志; the editors approve on the basis that population is the source of wealth (有人斯有土...) and so follow this example.
- The Longqing gazetteer contained detailed information on the lands seized from Buddhist monasteries, which the Wanli edition cut to avoid disputes now that these lands had been properly surveyed: "隆慶志於各廢寺糧米載雜志中特詳此未丈量以前事也。寺田與民田均隸於官應附版籍志別作一款而其不載於郡志久以。冊籍不具查覆實難（黨？）覆之不詳漫爾記之徒啓爭端契竇至於廢寺之田已經伐買丈量以後既屬民業若存其額且生一事也故今削之."
- Huang Fengxiang's intro: There were Quanzhou gazetteers in the Song Jiading and Chengyou reigns, and in the Jiajing and Longqing reigns; affirms the need for new editions to replace one another 如火之傳薪，車之接軌. The Longqing edition was published 45 years previously. Credits the new edition to the request of 楚梅城楊公, the then-xunfu. Huang Fengxiang was asked to contribute despite his old age. 

規制志
- In the section for Nan’an xian, notes that the wall around the county seat was only built following a devastating wokou raid in Jiajing 37. The work was completed in JJ 43, with a stone postal road through the main gate. 
- The Tong’an city wall was first built in the Song, but was rebuilt and reinforced repeatedly over the years, particularly in the Ming when the city seems to have been repeatedly troubled by bandits. One rebuilding happened in JJ 37, but the wokou are not mentioned to have actually taken the town at that time. In JJ 38, while the walls were damaged from heavy rain, the city was attacked by “賊,” but the local officials (including 同知 李時芳) managed to stop up the gaps and keep them from entering the walls. In Wanli 33 and 35 the wall was damaged by first an earthquake and then a severe storm, and repaired quickly both times. 
- Anping is mentioned as the first among the 鎮城. It is located south of metropolitan Jinjiang (the 八都), and is currently thriving (煙火湊集). The fortification was built after the wokou raids of JJ 37, on the advice of the zhixian 盧仲佃, in order to give the people a place to gather for protection. Over time more soldiers and officials were stationed there, until in Wanli 35 a proposal was even made to establish an Anping xian from southern Jinjiang with this fort as county seat. 
- The 城池 section ends with a short essay bemoaning that people’s moral decay has nullified the benefit of fortifications: 城池為保障所從來矣。往四郊多壘，城中危急，家自為守，人有固志，卒底與平，豈一墉一沼之力哉？無亦世風未漓，人心猶樸，上下相安，大小相恤，故足賴也。今侈競成俗，強能侵弱，賤亦妨貴，閭里之間無賴成群，小奸大奸之狀，萬一有他，誰非亂萌？故夫禁奢止競，辨分定志，非宜城急務乎？城垣雖固，總之以人心風俗為本。
- In the 行署 section, notes that most Quanzhou counties did not have their own 布政司, but that one was built for Tong’an in the Zhengtong reign and repaired in the Longqing era. 
- The 書院 section records a number of academies associated with Zhu Xi, including one in Anping (said to be founded by Zhu Xi’s father and used in the present for lectures to the local garrison and officials) and one in Tong’an, which was rebuilt and equipped with a statue of Zhu Xi in Chenghua 12. 
- The section on markets notes only two 市 in Jinjiang removed from the county seat: 安平市 at 八都 outside Anping Zhen, also known as Anhai (即今安海), and an 吳店市 located at 27 都.

- Hukou section begins with a history of tax systems and censuses of Quanzhou before the Ming. Upon reaching the Ming, it gives the basics of Hongwu’s population registration system without giving actual census numbers. The next institutional arrangement described is the 八分法 introduced by 沈灼 in Zhengde 14.
	- Description of 八分法: “每一丁歲徵銀八分以充歲辯等料惟差役仍舊十年一事云郡.” 
	- Following the wokou raids of the Jiajing era, the hukou records dropped by 6 or 7 tenths. “自嘉靖以來煦育日久黎庶蕃殷逮季年倭夷入寇兵火癘疲之餘戶口十損六七有例並戶除丁晉江南安惠安德化四縣戶丁雖稍得除建減而戶以花分開折友增於舊. 至於同安安溪永春三縣則有司漫不加意,里書得肆為奸,戶口皆增,非惟上方德意抑亦下重民害焉.” 
		- “有例並戶除丁” = Regulations were passed to combine households and remove Ding. 
		- In the first four mentioned counties, the number of ding was reduced in this process while the number of households slightly increased. In the latter four, due to uncontrolled corruption among village clerks, the household and population numbers were raised, causing harm to the people.

- 田地: Note that one category of 官田 is 廢寺田: 廢寺田者，寺額廢而入官者也。
	- The introduction explains that under the Min Kingdom most of the land in Quanzhou was owned by the temples, with common people working as tenants. (My copy is too illegible on these pages to finish the narrative.)
	- Explains that since the taxes on guantian are less onerous, the “豪家” find ways to move their land into that category, and then take advantage of poorer neighbors burdened by taxes to expand their lands: 故豪家操鬻田者之急或強冒官產或減畝（冷?）券迺鬻田者亦久操之懸產不推迄于死徙其子孫。稚弱淪亡竟無從究詰間或有水漂沙壓田去產有饔飧無資而追呼日逼凡此二者不得不號籲于官。官司憐之浮糧之名所由？矣… This situation has also been exploited by corrupt officials, with the result that neighbors fight for profits while marginal land goes out of cultivation and even good land is left unworked.
		- Claude’s semi-questionable translation of the above: “"Thus when powerful families control those who are desperate to sell land, they either forcibly claim government land or reduce the mu [illegible], and then those selling land are also long controlled by them, with property rights left unsettled, until death displaces their descendants. The young and weak sink into ruin, and in the end there is no way to investigate and question. Sometimes there are cases where water floods or sand covers and takes away fields, where one has property but no resources for food, while collection demands press day by day. These two types of cases cannot but cry out to the officials. The officials pity them, and this is how the term 'floating grain tax' came to be [illegible]." 
	- In Wanli 10 the court ordered Quanzhou to perform a land survey “方田.” In Hui’an and Anxi this was difficult, and they merely redistributed “extra” tax burdens (the 浮糧) evenly among all tax holders: “惠安安溪二邑官民皆難與慮始第均攤浮糧於業戶而已.” In Jinjiang the official 彭國光 carefully surveyed land and divided it into higher, middle, and lower categories, with the result that large quantities of new land were enrolled and the total tax grain for the county was increased by 2/5ths beyond the 浮糧/均攤 payments. Seeing this other counties decided to follow Anxi and Hui’an; the Dehua gazetteer supposedly comments on this. Further discusses the fiscal uses made of monastery lands; **during the wokou crisis of the Jiajing reign 6/10ths of proceeds from 寺田 were expropriated for military expenses** (“嘉靖中有大工之役費無所出令民間找賣寺田逮季年閩中中倭兵餉匱乏撫按復議寺在僧存者內將四分給僧焚修六分抽餉行之已父。”) 
	- Surveys of land follow on 472-474. Jinjiang counted 4252 qing of taxable guan and min land in Jiajing 41 and 5733 qing in Wanli 10; Nan’an went from 3609 to 3615; Tong’an went from 2596 to 2624. In other words, only Jinjiang actually registered substantially more land during this “resurvey.” (Hui’an, Anxi, Yongchun, and Dehua also registered mere tens of additional qing).

- 賦役: Starts from Yang Yan’s double-tax system. Praises the Song for “equalizing” the excessive taxes of the Min kingdom. Considers the Ming system largely a continuation of the Song methods, even the lijia: “坊里長即宋之督賦稅者也。” Explains that the practice of commuting grain rice to silver began in the Xuande reign and was extended in Zhengde 14 by [Shen Zhuo 沈灼], who implemented a system by which 官米 was commuted to silver and shipped to Beijing to cover the original tax assessments while 民米 was collected in kind and kept at home. After this system was adopted Fujian’s rice tax could be kept in local military granaries rather than being shipped north. This worked as designed in Jinjiang and Tong’an, where there was plenty of rice, but the other counties did not produce enough on their guantian to fully cover their tax requirements, and so half of their 民米 was commuted and earmarked for the capital at the rate of 5 *qian* silver per *shi.* The new assessments of the 浮糧 were entirely paid on silver from the start, at a half rate (2 *qian* 5 *fen* per *shi.*) The 耗 fees were also added to the official tax rate at some point. The editor claims that this made the people’s lives easier and they thereafter only found the corvée burdensome (獨里甲之役有甚為民苦者). In Jiajing 16 the Yushi [李元陽] suggested levying a fixed silver surtax to cover official expenses in order to save the li heads from being forced to shoulder those burdens irregularly. 
	- 482 is a bit illegible, but appears to talk about how various lijia services were piecemeal converted to silver. When [Qi Jiguang] fought against the wokou, another silver surcharge was levied to pay for his troops. In the first year of Wanli the Du Yushi [Pang Shangpeng ](…) 一條鞭法通府州縣。十歲中夏稅秋糧存留？運額若干綱徭兵站加銀額若干通為一條其曰綱銀則院寺峻邑之供應祭祀之費用生員之試賞舉貢進士之盤纏牌坊皆統焉。其曰均徭銀. A list of the government functions which switched over to silver payments at this time. This was experienced as a great relief to the jia members. 
	- Final comment before going over to numbers: “賦役之法，始也煩，繼也簡，始則賦役分為二。今則合而為一矣。舊制賦稅法皆以鈔，今鈔法之廢已久，惟據見徵糧差銀.”
