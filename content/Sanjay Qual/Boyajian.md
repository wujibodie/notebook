Chapter 3: Carreira and Casado Trade, 1580-1598
- "Portuguese Asian trade comprised much more than the Cape traffic. It also was the enterprise of many casados and soldados residing in Portuguese-controlled communities and lancados residing in native-controlled communities throughout the western Indian Ocean, the Bay of Bengal, and the China Sea. Generations of students of Portuguese Asia focused on the Estado da India's tribute and customs regime (Niels Steensgaard's redistributive enterprise). Recent scholarship pays homage to Portuguese merchants and private trade in Asia and fundamentally alters the perception of Asian trade. Casado trade now emerges as the Estado da India's principal feature. In this chapter I continue the revision. I take the measure of the intra-Asian trade, especially in the light of New Christian participation in it, and explore the relationship between the Cape trade and its merchants and the country trade and traders. That relationship is central to understanding the evolution of the Cape trade and the meaning of the Asian overseas enterprise to Portuguese economy and society in the sixteenth and centuries. It is also of significance to a growing debate over the degree to which the Portuguese enterprise in the precompany era (i.e., prior to 1600) changed Asian trading." 53
- Concludes that New Christians of Goa and Manila invested around 1.5 million and 450,000 cruzados annually on the carreiras, much of which went into inter-Asian trading; this combined figure meant the New Christians accounted for about 44% of total circulating Portuguese capital in Asia. 81

Chapter 6: The Zenith of the Carreira Trade, 1599-1619
- "About 1618 the general expansion of the Atlantic economy and the trade with Asia came to an abrupt halt. American and European markets ceased absorbing the flow of goods from Asia. A precipitous decline of prices and trade in 1618 signalled the saturation of European and American markets... Unable to sell their goods, merchants sought remedies and relief from taxing authorities. And this recession was global; the Manila galleon trade suffered too..."
	- Duarte Fernandes complained in 1618 that the glut of Asian goods in Lisbon had driven prices in some cases below the levels of previous years.  "Prices and trade recovered within a very few years, but a gradual long-term decline of profit margins followed over the next several decades." Result is the "most competitive phase yet of European trade with Asia." 144-5
