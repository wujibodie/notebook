
- Borschberg's preface singles out Thomaz as the preeminent scholar of Portuguese Melaka and sees Pinto as building on his accomplishments with a more focused study of a less familiar period (1575-1619). 

Introduction
- Two major causes of lacunae in studies of Portuguese in Asia: first, an overemphasis on Goa and the West Indian Ocean or on Macao, with Southeast Asia being left out until the rediscovery of the Suma Oriental and the work of Thomaz; second, "the concept of the decadence of the Estado da Índia" led to disinterest in the supposedly stagnant late 16th century and beyond. This is especially true for the Hapsburg period, seen as a "dark age" for Portugal. Pinto attempts to fill in these gaps, "gathering together in a single work data and approaches which have been dealt with by various authors in a fragmentary manner." xxii
- Prefers the concept of "exhaustion" to that of "decadence" for Melaka in this period. The steady exhaustion of Melaka's economic centrality, which began from 1511, was exacerbated and then "irreversibly hastened" by the changes of the latter part of the century, especially the arrival of the English and Dutch. It was only the Dutch assault that finally dislodged Melaka as the "heart of the Malay World." xxv
- Periodization: 1575 marks the failure of the last major siege of Melaka by Aceh, as well as the loss of Ternate, while 1619 was the year of the VOC's conquest of Jakarta and founding of Batavia. "The Portuguese lost Melaka in 1641, but it was in 1619 that Melaka 'fell' into the hands of the Dutch, not by conquest but owing to a gradual subordination and suffocation by a hostile rising power." xxvi 

Chapter One
- Melaka was far from the resources of the Estado, and was both more vulnerable to local conditions and gave more power to the Captain. The Captains tended to monopolize trade; in the 1570s Aires de Saldanha controlled over half of the city's spice imports. The captains' control over the customs house made it nearly pointless to investigate them. 
- The voyage to the Moluccas was one of a few formal royal monopolies, although private capital was allowed to be invested in the yearly galleon, and after 1610 freedom of trade was conceded to that route as well, although the risks involved made it unpopular. 11
- In 1594 an "exit tax" of 6 percent was levied on exports to Coromandel, Pegu, or Java, as these places lacked Portuguese customs houses, but this amounted to a double charge and harmed Melaka's competitive position. 13-14
- During the 1570s, the captains at Melaka made an agreement with the sultans of Johor, whereby spices would be imported to Johor and then purchased duty-free by the captains. This ended when a new captain (João da Silva Pereira) was ordered to end the situation, and ultimately destroyed Johor Lama in 1587. 18
- In the latter half of the century Aceh achieved near-monopoly over Sumatra's pepper and gold supplies, largely cutting out private Minangkabau traders who had previously carried these goods to Melaka. 
	- Aceh's main trading partner in India was Masulipatnam, the port of Golconda. 
- Banten in west Java largely dominated Palembang's pepper supplies and was the "main supplier of pepper to China" via visiting merchants. 21
- "The years 1605–06 marked a fundamental change: it was at this point that it became evident that the Dutch naval power no longer limited itself to attacking ships, but was also a direct threat to Melaka, Tidore, Solor and Ambon." 26
- The basic reason the English failed to establish themselves in Southeast Asia was that they lacked a commercial center on the Coromandel coast; their factory at Surat bought textiles which were in little demand in the region. The hostility of the Dutch prevented them from finding some other niche to fill. 26
- The immediate effect of Portuguese defeat was to hand primacy in combatting the new European enemies to Spain. Luso-Spanish maritime warfare was coordinated from Manila, and the division between two spheres of influence was eroded. 28
- According to Bocarro's *Decada* 13, 1615 was the last year in which nutmeg and mace traded widely at Melaka. 29
- Frequent Dutch attacks on Portuguese shipping forced Melaka's attention away from the main routes (to Macao and Coromandel) and towards less risky ones: "In this scenario, Melaka’s trade, apart from declining considerably in terms of importance and volume, became fragmented into less profitable trade routes, trafficking in numerous smaller trading circuits that they carried out throughout Southeast Asia as well as to the Coromandel coast and the Far East. From then on these voyages were made in small ships that were quicker and more maneuverable so as to be able to slip through Dutch vigilance." 32
	- Economic factors also were involved in the slackening of long distance trade: Notes that by 1616 the trade of Chinese silk to Japan was bringing just 25% profit, down from 100% previously. fn 104
	- One of the most important of these smaller trade routes was the trade in Perak tin, which came to be disputed between the residents and captains. 
- Melaka's fate was finally sealed by the Dutch foundation of Batavia in 1619, after which the enemy was at the doorstep and a new emporium for Indonesian trade developed. 


Chapter 2: Political and Military Framework
- "From a political and military point of view, it was an age of stabilisation and decline. However, the accumulated experience of the Portuguese enabled the establishment of a debate about ways of regeneration in administrative, economic and political terms, the containment of problems and deficiencies, and the charting of new courses. Let us take a brief look at two of the most interesting elements in this debate: first, the proposals for creating an autonomous government in Melaka, which came about after the reflexive analysis on a vast structure that extended from the Cape of Good Hope to Japan, and secondly, the projects for territorial conquest, a kind of regenerating project inspired by successful Spanish experiments in America and in the neighbouring Philippine islands." Dynamism came from the peripheries, not Goa itself. 39 
- When in 1596, in response to manpower shortages, the king pardoned renegades in Bengal and Pegu, the only crimes outside the pardon were lese-majeste, sodomy, treason, and counterfeiting. 41
- Though documents of the period were pessimistic about the state of the Estado, they also showed numerous ideas for reform and progress. 60 
- Francisco Rodrigues Silveira's *Reformaçao* proposed a new set of policies to discourage desertion, improve discipline, clearly separate military and commercial functions, and increase salaries to avoid corruption. 63-64
- One of the main reform proposals of the period was to grant Melaka an autonomous government, empowered to undertake its own territorial expansion. 
	- Dom João Ribeiro Gaio proposed the conquest of Aceh and the Malay Peninsula, to be followed by invasions of Cambodia, Siam, Cochinchina, and then China and Japan!
	- However, the only attack on Aceh came in 1606, and it was less aimed at conquest than forcing the sultan to accept a Portuguese fortress in north Sumatra. 
Chapter Three: Geopolitics of the Straits
- "Melaka, while a fundamental hub of the Portuguese maritime empire, was tied to the vast network that was the Estado da Índia; Aceh, vanguard of Islam in Southeast Asia, embodied the opposing structures that, via the trade in Sumatran pepper, extended to Gujarat and the Red Sea, and from there on to Cairo, Venice and Constantinople. Johor personified the legacy of the sultanate of Melaka and extended to the Eastern Archipelago, exercising a strong attraction over diverse mercantile communities, namely Chinese and Javanese merchants." 80
	- The triangular balance of power was destroyed by the arrival of the English and Dutch, with Melaka suffering most. 
- Portugal and Johor entered a period of detente in the 1560s, under Sultan Muzaffar Syah, first because his father had been captured by Aceh, and secondly because he was of the generation after 1511 and was thus more willing to cooperate with the Portuguese against more immediate rivals. In the three sieges of 1573-5, Johor remained neutral due to its fear of Aceh. 83
- When the Dutch began to appear in the Straits, the Portuguese exerted themselves to maintain good relations with Aceh in opposition to the interlopers. However, the new sultan Iskandar Muda was hostile to the Portuguese and willing to explore cooperation with the Dutch and English. 
- Emphasizes the limited ability of Melaka to supply large armadas in view of its own lack of resources. The first attempt to expel the Dutch from Sunda in 1598 failed because the Dutch fleet had already left and the captain was uninterested in seriously pursuing them. 103
	- The second planned armada was prevented from leaving Lisbon by an English blockade, while the expedition of André Furtado de Mendonça suffered storm damage on its way to Melaka, failed to draw the fleet of Banten out for an engagement, and then fought an inconclusive campaign against the Dutch around Ambon, before returning to Melaka as Captain in 1603. 
- In 1603 the *Santa Catarina* was captured with the assistance of locals from Johor, the first sign of Johor's sympathies towards the Dutch. A Portuguese attack on Johor in response failed to capture the capital. 
- Finally, the greatest armada of all and the "greatest military failure of the age" was the fleet captained by Viceroy Dom Martim Afonso de Castro. When this fleet arrived at Melaka in 1606 it found the city besieged by the Dutch, and while it succeeded in saving the city its losses were so heavy that hopes of an offensive campaign vanished. de Castro died the following year. 111

Chapter 4: Portuguese and Malays
- Iskandar Muda received an Ottoman embassy in 1612. "Aceh’s relentless march forward to conquer a substantial part of Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula during the 1610s and 1620s was inextricably linked with the supply of Ottoman arms, munitions and military advisers." 161
- The first known case of Acehnese control on the other side of the straits was Sultan Alauddin's 1584 erection of a fortress in Perak, where he had been born (see Andaya). 163

Conclusion
- "Despite the Portuguese
policy of maintaining pre-existing practices and regulations, so as to ensure
the continuity of trade and avoid any reduction in Melaka’s capacity to
attract mercantile communities, Melaka never again regained its erstwhile
prominence. Part of this commerce accompanied the deposed sultan and
his court who, after a period of some turbulence, established on the banks
of the Johor River a new sultanate which maintained the dynastic line and
inherited the prestige it had enjoyed in the past. Part of it disappeared
with the withdrawal of the Gujarati mercantile community, bitter rivals of
the Portuguese throughout the Indian Ocean, who would later settle in the
up-and-coming sultanate of Aceh, located on the northern tip of the island
of Sumatra. This is why it is said in the course of this study that Malay
Melaka broke into three pieces, each one of which claimed the ancient
legacy of the sultanate." 234
