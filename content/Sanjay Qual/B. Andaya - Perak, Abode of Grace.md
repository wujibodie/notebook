Chapter One
- Tome Pires referred to Perak as a small village of some 200 households in an important tin-producing area. It was a relatively minor vassal of the sultans of Melaka, who continued to exert authority over it after his exile to Bentan. It first came to importance when one of Sultan Mahmud's sons, Raja Muzafar Syah, left his court after being passed over as heir and chose Perak as site of his own state. Muzafar Syah asserted control over the entire Perak River basin and began to tax exports of tin, a commodity in increasing demand at this time, both in India and China. 
- Consistent problems for Peraka as a state: low population subject to quick shifts, dichotomy between easily-attacked but also more controllable *hilir* lowlands and the defensible but hard-to-govern, tin-mining uplands (*ulu*), and vulnerability of charismatic model of kingship to break down. 
	- Frequent problem: the *anak raja,* princes of the royal house not in line for the throne, often had no outlet for their ambitions in such a small and weak state, and so caused problems by breaking off their own fiefdoms or making bids for the throne. 32
	- The Raja Muda was second-in-command and often designated heir, but also made the most frequent attempts to seize the throne directly. 
- "Realization of their own weakness forced successive Perak rulers to search for allies who would guarantee peace and security in return for concessions in the tin trade. This search, and the often heavy cost of obtaining the protection of a powerful friend, become basic themes in Perak history from its very inception." 35

Chapter Two 
- In 1575 Perak was attacked by Aceh, but the result was that the widow of Perak's former sultan was brought as a guest, and soon her eldest son married the Queen and became ruler of Aceh; this ruler installed his younger son as Sultan of Perak, and from then on Perak benefitted from its privileged place as a valued vassal of Aceh. However, in 1620, Aceh's Sultan Iskandar Muda conquered Perak and seized almost the whole population as captives. One of these, Sultan Muhammad Syah, was installed as puppet ruler. Perak was trapped between alternate Acehnese and Portuguese demands and was unable to satisfy either; in this context it saw the newcomer of the VOC as a potential ally, and allowed a Dutch factory to be built. However, conflict ensued over Dutch demands to monopolize the tin trade, and a group of disgruntled nobles orchestrated the massacre of the Dutch residents in 1651 (46). In 1670 Sultan Mahmud allowed another Dutch factory to be built out of fear of Siam, but this too was destroyed by a hostile Bendahara in 1685.
	- "The Bendahara's position was rivalled only by the royal merchant (*saudagar raja*), an Indian named Sedelebe. The latter had managed to survive various fluctuations in his fortunes and by 1686 was in charge of virtually the entire tin trade in Perak. Buying tin in the ruler's name, he ordered all the miners in the *ulu* to deliver their tin to him and even refused to grant a Chinese merchant a pass to trade tin with the Dutch."  50
- In the 18th century two new powers emerged in the Malay Peninsula: wandering groups of Bugis (from Sulawesi) began settling in large numbers, mixing trade with piracy and forming multiple small statelets. Bugis came to be a politically dominant group in Johor, and were viewed with suspicion by many Malays. Further, migrations of Minangkabau (Sumatran Malays) had settled across coastal Malaysia, including Perak, and these became another separate group (normally hostile to the Dutch) which could take sides in factional struggles. In this context it was relatively easy for wandering *anak raja* to gather a mixed force around him to seek his fortune as a pirate or settler, and this contributed to a general atmosphere of insecurity in the Straits of Malacca throughout the 18th C. In this climate the rulers of Perak decided to turn to the VOC once more, signing a new treaty in 1745. 

Chapter Three
- Dutch interest in Perak was mostly due to a need to find some financial basis to support Melaka, which was decaying commercially but too important strategically to give up. At this time Batavia approved raising purchase prices on tin to meet high demand, especially in China, where it was used to purchase tea. 
- In 1743 a major attack by Bugis adventurers (retaliating for the murder of a Bugis captain previously) defeated Perak's ruler, who was caught in civil dispute with many of the nobles supporting the invaders. Sultan Muzafar was forced to flee upstream, while his younger brother was installed in the old capital until Muzafar reunited Perak in 1746. However, dissensions lingered, and Muzafar sent the "Indian Captain" of Perak on an embassy to Dutch Melaka. 
- European attempts to establish a tin monopoly at Perak floundered on Perak's insistence that the elephant trade be included, since this was their other major product; since previous Dutch elephant-trading in India had been disastrous, they were hesitant to accept this condition. 77
- Despite some disagreement on the specific terms, the Dutch were granted the island Tanjung Putus for their garrison, and agreed to buy the entire Perak tin production at a fixed price. 
- The development of diplomatic ties with the Dutch increased the importance at Perak's court of the *jurubahasa,* the linguistic expert. Honor and honesty were crucial prerequisites along with knowledge of various languages. Many were Indian Muslims, who settled there as artisans or traders. Mention of Tamils and Bengalis in particular. 84-86
	- The Dutch residents at Tanjung Putus were also important intermediaries who had some standing at the Perak court, and were required to know Malay, but since they were based miles away from the capital they were themselves dependent on local informants. 

- The end of the Dutch-Perak alliance came in 1781, when the VOC fort was seized by an English ship (Andaya considers this probably unnecessary, and the surrendering Dutch commander comes off as a coward and drunk who was happy to leave Perak). 345
- A Dutch factor returned to Perak, but the VOC was expelled for good in another English expedition of 1795. 379
- Conclusion: Although the Dutch alliance did not permanently resolve the basic problems facing Perak, it allowed for a period of relative tranquility (*sentosa*) which was remembered as a golden age, particularly during the reign of Sultan Iskandar. Court ritual and control of outlying territories were extended, and tin production increased under the certainty of steady sales. After 1791, the security of shipping and trade decreased and the state had to focus more of its efforts on maintaining basic control of the most important routes, and also came under the domination of Kedah and Siam. Emphasizes the role of that period's security in strengthening the position of the rulers and contributing to a cohesive identity of Perak as a kingdom (*kerajaan*), rather than just a collection of isolated settlements. 
	- During this time Chinese settlers began arriving to mine tin; in 1786 it was already noted in Batavia that they had brought improved mining techniques, "and thus set the stage for the great development of the tin industry which was to come late in the following century."
