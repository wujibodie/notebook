
Introduction
- Begins with an extended discussion of van Leur's work: his contributions, his predecessors and sources, limitations.
	- Already criticizes van Leur's overemphasis on the "peddling character of Asian shipping trade," which "brings van Leur into conflict with one of his own most important theses, namely, that up to the beginning of the 19th century, Asian and European trade were on an equal footing in Asia." 5
- "Asian trade was not predominantly a peddling trade, but neither was it, like the western European trade of the time, a shipping trade carried on mainly by independent citizens." Besides in Malacca, sees merchants as generally exerting little political influence. 
- Arguments for Portuguese influence on the Asian trading system before the Dutch arrival on 9-10: introduction of new technology, stimulation of production, changes in routes and rise of new cities, and integration of Portuguese private traders into numerous locales.
- Sees the VOC as disturbing or destroying important aspects of the native economy in Indonesia in the first half of the 17th century, and that thus by this time "European ascendancy was beginning to manifest itself," as against Van Leur's view that Europe only outstripped Asia after about 1800 (!)
	- Though note that Van Leur seems to have included India and China (?) in this judgement, while MR is focused on Indonesia. 10-11
Chapter One
- In the 11th century Chinese ships were supposed to have frequented Malabar for pepper, but whether they carried goods from Srivijaya to trade there is unclear. The Moluccas and Bandas were already producing spices traded abroad, but it is unclear who was shipping them. 16
- After the collapse of Srivijaya (for reasons in dispute) the former capital became the pirate center of Palembang, with a substantial Chinese component; Marco Polo encountered a parallel town on the Malayan coast called Tumasik. Mentions that piracy in the Straits made shipping through them unattractive in the 13th century. Around this time the Northern Sumatran port of Sumadra-Pase rises to importance, apparently stimulated by the beginnings of pepper production on the mountain slopes of Sumatra. 
- Notes that despite importance of Gujarati traders in Islamization of Indonesia, with the rise of Malacca they largely stopped going beyond the Straits, though individual traders probably did so in Malay or Javan ships. 24
	- Similarly, the Chinese mostly restricted their trade to Malacca and stopped visiting Java directly, though Pires' mention that the Chinese had not been seen in Java for 100 years would also have to do with the sea ban. 26
- Majapahit was in collapse by the early 15th century, and the Islamized port towns of northern Java began expanding to Sumatra and Borneo; signs of Javanese influence in Amboina. 25

Chapter 2: The Rise of Malacca
- In Ma Huan's time Malacca was still a relatively small town devoted to fishing and the export of local tin. Over time Malacca conquered or subjugated a series of Malay towns which paid it gold or tin in tribute. More importantly, Malacca dominated the "Celates," the fisher-pirates of the straits around Singapore and Riau, and so managed to reduce piracy there; however, the pirate kingdom of Aru managed to resist through the 16th century. 
Chapter 3: Malacca at the end of the 15th Century
- Basic structure of trade in Malacca was determined by the monsoon schedules: Indian and Chinese traders arrived and left between March and June, while Javanese traders arrived in summer and left in winter. Thus merchandise had to be stored for part of the year, and agents of the main traders had to reside in Malacca year-round. 
- Four *shahbandar* heads of the foreign merchant communities: one for Gujaratis; for Bengalis, Peguans, and Paseans; for people from Java, Moluccas, Luzon, Palembang, Borneo, Banda; and one for the Chinese and Ryukyuans (possibly including Japanese). 42
- When a ship arrived at Malacca its cargo was assessed by a group of 10 merchants of mixed nationality, then charged 6% as customs (if from the West) or were simply asked for "gifts" of about the same value (if from the East). 44
- Prices were also arranged in a consultation between large groups of buyers and sellers, to avoid price-fixing. 45
	- This system worked to keep prices at fairly stable known levels and also to ensure that goods would be sold quickly; it was a priority that business be finished as quickly as possible. 
- One of the most powerful figures in Malacca at the time of Portuguese contact was the "Utimutu raja," the head of the Javanese community, who lived in a fortified compound with numerous slaves and who lorded over his own community using his control of the rice trade as leverage to protect his interests. 56
