Introduction 
- Basic lens of Java as "carrefour" is based on the idea of an accumulation of "strata" from different civilizations (Indic, Islamic, Chinese, European; relatively little emphasis on any "indigenous" element). Notion that a "geological" survey of historical "mentalities" will reveal these different strata and make possible a general economic and social history. 
Chapter One: Geography
- Already uses the term "Chinese Mediterranean" for the Nanyang. By way of comparison, points out that Canton to Guangzhou is roughly the same distance (~3000 km) as Gibraltar to Istanbul, while Aceh is about halfway by sea between Jakarta and Madras (2000 km either way). 13
- Separates "Insulindia" into a number of zones marked more by common seas than landmasses; map on 18. Of these, central Java gives the impression of being a "coeur meme d'un carrefour."
- 23: Divides Java into four agricultural systems: *tegal/tegalan*: dry, plowed fields (distinguished from the slash-and-burn of Sumatra); *sawah,* wet rice fields; *kebun*, orchards and gardens, usually mixed with and inseparable from *sawah*; and the "grand plantations," which in their present form trace back to the Dutch coffee plantations, though they have precedents in the pepper plantations of Banten or the Chinese cinnamon and sugar fields around 17th C. Batavia.

Part One: The Limits of Occidentalization 
- Western observers have created a dichotomy between an inert, ornamental traditional culture and a Western modernity, where one one hand the "Orient" has curative powers for the "illnesses" of the "giant with feet of clay," but on the other "development" is set to deracinate and assimilate tradition and difference. Argues against this that the "Western impact" "si important qu'il puisse paraitre, n'a concerne en fair qu'une part infime de la societe." 40
- Most of the first chapter is dedicated to a survey of European "exoticizing" literature on Indonesia, with one theme being the steady development of the myth of Bali in particular. 
- Marco Polo never visited Java, but was impressed with stories he heard of it while in north Sumatra; Odoric of Pordenone did visit Java, but wrote relatively little about it except his impressions of the grandeur of Majapahit. 
- The Dutch conquered the village of Jakarta and built their first fortress of Batavia in 1619, on the direction of Jan Pietersz. Coen. 
- Emphasizes the early difficulties of Batavia's settlers in replicating their home society: the highly literate Dutch were unable to keep a primary school running until the end of the 18th century, and had trouble recruiting sufficient pastors to even keep the church school open. On occasion small literary or intellectual circles would form around individual figures, such as Governor-General Johannes Camphuys (1684-91).
