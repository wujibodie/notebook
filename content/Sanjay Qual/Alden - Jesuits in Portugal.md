Chapter 3: Foundations
- Francis Xavier toured the Portuguese Asian colonies from 1542-1552. With his approval Jesuits took control of a Christian seminary at Goa in 1550.
- In the 1540s 26 Jesuits arrived in Asia, but numbers increased each decade, until by 1602 62 recruits arrived in a single year. 47
- Alessandro Valignano arrived in Goa in 1574 and proved one of the ablest administrators and chief scholars of the Jesuits in the East. His "Indian Summary" of 1583 was a full report on the state of the Indian Ocean missions. He blamed the poor results of these missions not so much on a lack of resources as on their misapplication: efforts were wasted on Africans and Indians who lacked the intelligence to grasp Christian principles. Valignano thus recommended a shift towards the "so-called white races" of China and Japan. 56
	- Valignano pushed through the foundation of a Jesuit college in Malacca despite its expense, as a place to prepare for missions further afield. 
- The Spice Islands had few Jesuits in part because of a high death rate: of 38 who worked there between 1546 and 1588, 13 died of disease or violence. 59
- During his two years in Japan Xavier already met with and debated *bonzes*, "who became the Jesuits' principal intellectual adversaries in the archipelago." 59
	- Xavier on the Japanese: "The people with whom we have thus far conversed are the best that we have yet discovered; and it seems ... that, among pagan nations, there will not be another to surpass the Japanese."
	- Three reasons Japan was especially promising: first, the Buddhists were splintered and did not command total respect; second, the recent closure of the Chinese coast provided the Portuguese with an obvious economic niche that the Jesuits could serve as "interpreters and arbitrators;" third, the whole region had a common language and thus did not present the same challenges as India or Africa. 60
- The late 1570s and early 80s saw the "take-off" of the Japan mission, as the daimyos of Bungo, Omura, and Arima in north Kyushu all converted, and over 50 Jesuits ministered to 100,000 converts. 61
	- Valignano was outraged to find that European Jesuits held convert "acolytes" in contempt, and argued both for Jesuit accomodation to local customs and for the admission of Japanese into the Society. 62
- By 1587, when Toyotomi Hideyoshi ordered the expulsion of all Jesuits, there were 113 fathers and 200,000 Christians in Japan. However, within a few years Hideyoshi relaxed his vigilance and permitted the return of most priests to their old stations, though with restrictions on their political activity. 64

Chapter 4: Perils of Propinquity 
- The Jesuits have frequently been blamed for urging Dom Sebastian to his death in Morroco. Sebastian's mother deserted him soon after his birth to become the only female member of the Society (!). 
	- Critics in Sebastian's own lifetime thought his Jesuit education had molded him into a fanatical crusader rather than pragmatic statesman. He also valued chastity and showed no interest in women. 83

Chapter Five: Mandarins and Martyrs 
- "From the perspective of the Society, however, the third apostate, Crist6vao Ferreira (b. 1580-d. 16so), was far more devastating to its image.
Ferreira, born in Torres Vedras, north of Lisbon, entered the Society at sixteen. After
completing studies in philosophy and theology, he was assigned to the Japan station. By 1603 he was studying Japanese in Macao and became sufficiently proficient that he subsequently preached in that language. He went to the Japanese isles around 1610 and remained underground there after the general expulsion. In 1617 he took his final vows and traveled surreptitiously in the central archipelago, regularly sending out reports concerning the status of the Church and its notable martyrdoms. By 1633 Ferreira was vice-provincial, the most senior Jesuit remaining in the islands. That year he was captured and subjected to a newly devised form of torture by which the victim was bound, inverted, and suspended over a pit of excreta. Some lingered for days before expiring, though the torture's primary intent was to exact renunciations of the faith rather than to cause death. In Ferreira's case, that technique proved successful. He became a convert to Shinto, wrote tracts condemning Christianity, and served as a merciless interrogator of missionaries captured almost as soon as they landed on Japanese soil. The objective of some of those missions was to save the soul of this major delinquent, but Jesuit reports that Crist6vao Ferreira finally came to his senses and died in the pit seem to have no factual basis. 23 Ferreira's apostasy was far from unique. Two of five European Jesuits who arrived in Japan in 1642 also renounced their faith, as did nine of those who came the following year. Among them were another vice-provincial and the last Jesuits to set foot on Japanese soil for centuries to come." 136
