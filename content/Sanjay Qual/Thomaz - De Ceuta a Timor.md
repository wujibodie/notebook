One: Portuguese Expansion and European Expansion
- Basic question: Was it the Portuguese who initiated the period of "European expansion?" Did they simply begin their own period of expansion, imitated by Castille and others?
- Problem of breaking up the overall phenomenon of "European" expansion into individual national expansions is that it may be impossible to bring them together into a synthesis that explains each case. Basic question of whether this expansion was basically multiple in its character and origins or if it stemmed from some single root. 3
	- An attempt to explain overseas expansion as a product of an overall European wave flounders on the fact that Portugal "from practically no point of view could be considered as at the vanguard of Europe." 3
	- Also doubts whether Portugal's poverty and remoteness were really the engine of its expansion, since the major early movements came from the heights of power. 
- Four rubrics to understand the conditions of Portuguese expansion: 
	- *Instrumental causes*, namely the happy juncture of Mediterranean and Northern shipbuilding and navigation techniques, along with the possession of codified astronomical and seafaring knowledge via the Romans and Arabs; but these conditions can only explain why Portugal was able to succeed in exploring the ocean, not why it chose to do so. 
		- A structural orientation towards the Atlantic due to geography along with a centralized monarchy and a degree of "national consciousness." Sees a degree of political coherence as required for state-backed explorations in all major cases (opposed to e.g. the Italian city states), but again only as a precondition. 
	- *Goals of expansion*: counter-pressure vs. Turkish expansion, or search for Prester John, or Sudanese gold to solve Europe's bullion famine, or to preclude a Castilian conquest of Morocco (and encirclement of Portugal).  However many of these explain only one part of Portuguese expansion, and many were common across Europe. 
	- *Formative causes:* Ideological factors like the crusade mentality, Franciscan evangelism, Aristotelianism, etc., all shaped the form expansion took. Particularly the reconquista mentality along with the "frontier" pragmatism and permeability conditioned Portuguese attitudes to foreign peoples and distinguished Portugal somewhat from its European neighbors. Still, while these factors might explain certain successes of Portuguese expansion, they were not its motive cause.
	- *Material causes*: Maritime expansion as outgrowth of the demographic and commercial expansion of the previous centuries. In Portugal economic growth shifted the center of gravity towards the coasts and especially Lisbon as the center for trade with both Flanders and Italy. However, disputes the idea that maritime commerce was a dominant factor in medieval Portugal, not least because the "burguesia" never imposed its own values on state or society. 
- Sees the colonization of the Azores and Madeira as largely continuous with the process of internal expansion and not as the start of something new, even despite Madeira's commercialized export industry in sugar; the proper start of Portugal's maritime expansion is dated to the conquest of Ceuta in 1415. 
	- Even if its conquest was not originally undertaken for commercial purposes, the conquest of Ceuta did help protect Christian navigation through the Strait of Gibraltar, and the merchants of Lisbon and Porto contributed ships to protect Ceuta and for the failed attack on Tangier in 1437. Even so, debates the notion that the bourgeoisie actually brought about the Moroccan conquests.
- Narrative of the explorations under Prince Henry focuses on their "Moroccocentric" character, with Henry heavily motivated by the search for an advantage against the Mouros. The earliest surveying voyages in 1434-6, which found the passage beyond Cape Bojador, were interrupted by Henry's involvement in the war on Tangier; 1441-48, years in which Henry was barred from further attacks on Morocco, were a "crucial phase in the evolution of the expansionist phenomenon," in which a rapid progress south occurred and new harbors were sought out. 32
	- In 1443 a request was granted for a private voyage to the Saharan coast: a "corsair" party of squires which went purely for the purpose of slave-raiding. 
	- In 1444 the first contact was made with Guinea, with a squadron of 26 ships visiting the "terra dos Negros." By 1448 expeditions to Guinea were undertaken on a purely commercial basis, and so this is the year which for Thomaz demarcates the separation of the Guinea voyages from the Moroccan conquests. 34
- Final explanations of the Portuguese expansion: 1. Need felt by the nobility for a reorientation in the face of its own overpopulation and squeezes from the bourgeoisie and the Crown; 2. Need for new commercial routes to invest growing profits, pioneered by the merchants but imitated by the king and aristocracy; 3. An affirmative political plan by the national state seeking to avoid absorption by its powerful neighbor, as well as maintaining internal order by draining out the conflicts inherent to an era of socioeconomic change. 38-9
- The maritime voyages, beginning from the particular concerns of Portugal in the 1430s, ultimately pointed the way for a Western Europe that was searching a new political, social, economic, and mental equilibrium. "Em todo este processo o papel dos descobrimentos quatrocentistas pode definir-se essencialmente como desbloqueante, talvez sem querer nem dar por isso. Se a Europa em fermentaçao se assemelhava então a gruto de Eolo, repleta de ventos prontos a soprar sobre os oceanos, os descobrimentos foram o cabo da lança, que, arredando a mole que lhes barrava a saída, lhes permitiu ter livre curso." 41

II: The Evolution of the Portuguese Expansionist Policy in the First Half of the 15th C.
- Criticizes two dominant streams in Portuguese historiography: first, the heroic-voluntarist stream, for failing to give room to those projects which did not succeed and to the motivations for exploration preceding the final discovery of the route to India; second, the economic-socializing stream for its insufficient ability to explain phenomena of a "conjunctural rather than structural" nature. 44
	- Thomaz' goal: To clarify the poorly-studied structures and events which combined to produce the early phase of Portuguese expansion. 
- Fernão Gomes, discoverer of the coast of Guinea, was ennobled with the name da Mina and made a councilor of the King. "So, just as the nobles, involving themselves in commerce, *burgeoisified* themselves (se aburguesiavam) as we saw above, the burgeoisie ennobled themselves; from then on the two classes interpenetrated more and more, for which reason the clash between their interests weakened bit by bit." 138 
- After the death of Henry in 1460 the voyages of exploration were paused until 1469. His adoptive son, Dom Fernando, focused his energies on populating Cabo Verde. Since the islands were dry and unattractive to farmer-settlers, but important as a naval and trading base, residents were offered concessions for trade with Guinea. 
- It's not clear why D. Afonso V reopened the maritime explorations, although he may have been motivated by the search for Prester John due to resumed war with the Marinids; he probably also recognized the possible economic gains, and may have already been dreaming of a path to India. There are a few references that his goal may have been an "India" identified with Ethiopia; the first indisputable reference to Marco Polo's India is in a proposal by a Florentine to visit that region going *west*, without reference to the African route. 143-145
	- In any case Afonso became distracted by his intervention in the Castilian succession war, and exploration was halted until the accession of Joao II in 1481. 

III: The Joannine Imperial Project
- Joao II, while not making a clean break from the past, did pursue a coherent and unified maritime expansion policy bearing clear signs of "modernity;" among the most important of these was the direct participation of the court and Crown in planning and executing the voyages.
	- "tudo o que concerne a expansao maritima passa a ser directamente gerido pela Coroa." 152
	- M-G saw reign of Joao II as the time when the "cavaleiro-burgues" synthesis fully developed. 
- "Perhaps the most original and salient characteristic of the Johannine program was the simultaneity with which seven distinct fronts were developed, which on its own reveals a degree of planning previously impossible thanks to the centralization of the expansionist enterprise in the hands of the king, and which contrasts powerfully with the tactile empiricism of previous periods." 160-161
- The seven fronts:
	- Technical preparations for future voyages, including astronomical-cartographic studies and "prospecting" of winds and currents
	- Organization and exploration of commerce with the African coasts, particularly marked by the construction of S. Jorge da Mina. 
	- Continuation of the voyages along the west coast of Africa, with the decided goal of rounding the Cape and reaching the Indian Ocean. 
	- Collection of information about the East, including through overland journeys to India. 
	- Attempts to penetrate the African interior, either to find Prester John or to traffic with the gold-producing states. 
	- Early nurturing of African Christian groups, most spectacularly with the Christianization of the Congo in 1491. 
	- Diplomatic activity intended to reserve rights over the discovered territories for Portugal, culminating in the Treaty of Tordesillas. 164

IX: The Portuguese in the Bay of Bengal
- Conclusion: "In any case the history of the events of 1521-22 has a demonstrative value, as it shows us in action all the elements which will be present thereafter: merchants, corsairs, ambassadors of the King, local *caudilhos*, Portuguese mercenaries more or less in their pay. The organization of these disparate forces retains, the whole century long, a so to say meteorological character: here a political high pressure front, there a storm, ceaselessly changing positions, unmaking and remaking themselves by turns.  A disconcerting history, truthfully, in which seemingly nothing moves and everything moves, as things change incessantly without altering their deeper nature. The great transformations only arrived later, in the 17th century, with the entry on the scene of the great commercial companies. The Portuguese, who unintentionally opened the way for them, became the victims, rather than the authors, of the new state of affairs." 486

X: Nina Chatu 
- Emphasizes the hostility between the Muslim and "Gentile" residents of Malacca; Nina Chatu gained great wealth as leader of the "Cefir" community (both shabandar of the Kelings and bendahara) in the aftermath of the flight of most of the Gujaratis and Islamized *orang kaya*; his downfall and suicide followed the Portuguese selection of Raja Abdala, a relative of the Malaccan Sultan, as bendahara and "viceroy," in order to shore up support among Muslim merchants.
- There were two routes to the Moluccas: the direct way only took 40 days, but the more profitable route took 11 months and involved buying cotton textiles in Malacca to sell for copper coins in north Java, which would be exchanged for rice and coarse textiles in Bima (Sumbawa), which would then be traded for cloves and nutmeg.500
