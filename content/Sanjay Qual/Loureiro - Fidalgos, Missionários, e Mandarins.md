
Introduction
- The basic rubric of the book is an exhaustive study of the *noticias, imagens,* and *vivencias* of China in Portugal during the 16th Century, during which Loureiro asserts there was a distinct *cultura da expansao,* consisting of "the compound of texts produced by Portuguese authors as a consequence of the links established with extra-European regions." 21 
	- Particularly relevant authors from the culture of expansion include the chronicles by Joao de Barros, Fernao Lopes de Castanheda, Damiao de Góis, and Gaspar Correia; the reports of contact by Duarte Barbosa, Tomé Pires, Afonso de Albuquerque, António Galvao, Garcia da Orta, and Fernao Mendes Pinto; Jesuit histories, especialy those of Joao de Lucena and Sebastiao Goncalves; but most crucially the first hand experiences of Galiote Pereira, Amaro Pereira, and Gaspar da Cruz. 
	- Periodization: begins from the first actual contact of Portuguese navigators with China in 1513, and ends in 1583, the year in which the Jesuits were given permission to enter Guangdong proper and proselytize, as well as when the news of Portugal's union with Spain reached Macau; after this point the news about China would reach a new level of concreteness, while the Portuguese activities in the South China Sea entered a distinctive phase. L. calls the period 1513-1583 an "independent cycle" dominated by a set of "identical processes" including exclusivity of Portuguese access to China. 37

Chapter Two: 15th Century Journeys and Navigations
- Emphasizes the Portuguese court's curiosity about and focus on a route to "Cathay" as well as "India;" Henry the Navigator and Joao II made multiple requests for information from Italians who had visited the East or had information about it. The voyages down the African coast were envisioned as leading to the East even before the rounding of the Cape, as evidenced by Joao's rejection of schemes to reach China sailing West. The delay between Dias' return from the Cape (88) and the da Gama voyage is partially explained by the discombobulation of the court at the news of Columbus' discoveries; a brief period of planning for American expeditions was closed by the Treaty of Tordesillas in which Portugal definitively settled on an Eastern route to Cathay/India. (84)
	- Romantic imagery of Portuguese explorers sailing down Guinea Coast while Zheng He's voyages reach the shores of Somalia and Kenya: "expansionistas independentes, que, apesar e sua singular coincidencia cronológica, se ignoraram mutamente."
	- In 1486 Joao II sent two explorers overland to India, citing "the great desire the King has for the discovery of India." One of them actually reached it, but without ever returning. 80

Chapter Three: First News About China
- The return of Da Gama's first voyage, with "Gaspar da Gama" in tow, brought the first news of China (though still not identified as such) to Europe. 
- The da Gama voyage brought porcelains, which were known to have originated outside India, but without being linked to any concrete place. 92
- A letter by Girolamo Sernigi, a Florentine resident in Lisbon, sent a few days after da Gama's return, mentions news of large fleets of long-haired ("come Allemani") and shaven men which had visited Calicut some 80 years previous, in wooden ships with cannon, and who Sernigi speculates may be Russians; L takes this as "one of the earliest European references to the Chinese presence on the Indian littoral." 93
- Gaspar Correia, years after the fact, claimed that the first Portuguese factory at Calicut was located in the former residence "dos Chyns." 95
- The earliest reports from the Manueline period associated "Cathay" or "Malchina," "Mahacina," with a large kingdom of white-skinned and long-haired men, which produced silk and porcelains. 
- Long study of the *Marco Paulo* of Valentim Fernandes, printed in Lisbon in 1502, and which combined all available European travel accounts of the Far East,  in which Marco Polo remained the "supreme authority," although the book acknowledged the need for revisions to be made by new explorers in the Kingdom of the Great Khan (98-104).
	- "This was an era of movement, in which the traditional knowledge began to be called into question by the Lusitanian navigations." 104
- The 1502 Cantino Planisphere, created in 1502 in Lisbon, contains the first Western reference to the "*terra dos chins*," somewhere beyond Malacca. 106-107

Chapter 3: Portuguese and Chinese in Malacca

- Dom Manuel gave the first order to reach and fortify Malacca in 1505, as part of a "race" with Castille, which at this time was also interested in reaching the city. 
- The Portuguese expedition to Malacca left Lisbon in 1508, with explicit instructions to "ask about the chiins," in terms of their commerce, products, military capacities, physical appearance, culture and religious customs, and political organization and territorial limits. 139
- The early Portuguese understanding of the Malaccan sultanate was that it had previously been linked to Siam, but at some previous time had broken free while affirming its "vassalagem" to China. 121.
- At their anchorage outside Malacca, the Portuguese under de Sequeira encountered 3-4 Chinese ships. At once the Portuguese considered the Chinese potential allies against the Muslim Gujaratis and Malays, and were hopeful that the Chinese saw them the same way; however this encounter was not treated by the chroniclers as especially monumental, since at the time China was "*mais um* povo asiático." 
- Lopes de Sequeira's voyage to Malacca lasted several months, but he was ultimately forced to leave due to the hostility of the "mouros" of the city. 
- Major consequences of the 1511 conquest of Malacca: the Portuguese installed themselves in the center of a gigantic commercial web; they gained access to a base for the gathering of geographic knowledge; and it gave them an advantage in the Iberian race to the Spice Islands and allowed them to "discuss on a secure basis the *questao das Molucas*;" and it opened the route to China. 135

	- Albuquerque also sent various exotic goods, including a printed Chinese book; Dom Manuel sent this book further to Pope Leo X in 1514.

	- However, Chinese merchants were present at the start of hostilities between the Portuguese and Sultan Muhammad, and might have elected to stay away out of caution despite not knowing the outcome.
	- As a result, it was decided to undertake an expedition to Canton guided by a "Mouro" who had traded there before. However, this Mouro pulled out of the venture and went to China without any Portuguese goods or passengers. 
- Tome Pires was sent to Malacca in late 1512 by Albuquerque to serve as factor for spices and investigate infractions among the local Portuguese. 148-9
- In 1513 a fleet of four junks arrived at Malacca led by "Cheilata," a Chinese merchant who had dealt with Diogo Lopes de Sequiera previously. The fleet carried little merchandise and largely intended to survey conditions after the war in 1511; they were said to have returned to China satisfied that trade could continue. Two Portuguese followed them, the first to actually reach China.151
- The factor sent on this expedition was Jorge Álvares, while the junks were loaded with merchandise both from the Portuguese and from Nina Chatu. This ship was able to anchor at "pulo tumon," an island off the coast of Guangdong. Jorge Alvares would have been the chief informant on China for Tome Pires
- The next expedition to China was that of Rafael Perestrelo in 1515-1516, and proved that this route was potentially extremely profitable. 159
- The Alvares and Perestrelo expeditions provided the bases for the early accounts of Duarte Barbosa and Tomé Pires, who jointly "were responsible for forming the first images of China in the Portuguese culture of the early 1500s." 160

Chapter 6: The birth of the image of China
- Chapter focused entirely on the works of Barbosa and Pires, who produced the earliest systematic account of China as a real place rather than a vague name associated with exotic goods. 
- Pires clearly consulted Portuguese returnees from the first expeditions to China; but Barbosa (based in Cannanore) curiously claims to have no new European informants. Possibility of Malay informants?
- Barbosa particularly notes that the Chinese had no ritual restrictions on any meat (important for placing them as non-Muslim?) and ate with sticks. He notes with great surprise that some Chinese live their whole lives on boats, bringing their women with them, before moving on to their main products. 

Chapter 7: The Expedition of Fernao Peres de Andrade
- In 1516 Dom Manuel send Fernao Peres de Andrade on an expedition to "discover the Bay of Bengal and China," with a partial goal of definitively establish a Portuguese presence in those regions which the Spanish might dispute as falling on their side of the Tordesillas line. The new governor of India, Lopo Soares de Albergaria, travelled to Goa with this armada. 192
	- Gaspar Correia is the major source for this expedition
- Once Andrade had reached Goa, he was appointed with the duty to bring an embassy to the Chinese emperor, led by Tomé Pires (supposedly a friend of Albergaria)
- The armada stopped in Pacem to stock up on pepper, and met their Florentine factor Giovanni da Empoli. 198
- After a series of delays and disasters (including the accidental burning of the flagship Belem at Pacem) the Andrade fleet departed Malacca in June of 1517. With 8 ships, this was by far the largest Portuguese armada sent to any far Eastern kingdom. After arriving in August, the fleet remained in Chinese coastal waters for over a year, trading, exploring, and noting as much information as possible about the reason. During this time negotiations were made for Tomé Pires to disembark and take his embassy to the "King." 205

8: The Portuguese in Canton, 1517-1518
- The Fernao Peres de Andrade expedition was intercepted by the Chinese navy, but managed to peacefully anchor at Tuen Muen 屯门. There the Portuguese attempted to petition to enter Canton as emissaries, but were delayed for over a month by the garrison commander of Nantou. They finally reached Canton in late September. Peres ordered a volley of cannonfire as salutation, which the Chinese saw as a severe breach of protocol. 
- After leaving Tome Pires and making a quick survey of the outskirts of Canton, Fernao Peres returned quickly to Tuen Muen due to 1. news of a pirate attack on the ships which had remained there, and 2. health problems in the unsavory environment of the Pearl River mouth. 
- While anchored at Tuen Muen, the secondary Portuguese ships encountered a fleet from "Léquios," the traders of which paid for their goods with an "inexhaustible supply of gold," and Jorge de Mascarenhas was sent with one ship to discover these islands. His Chinese pilots told him the winds were not right for that journey, and instead conducted him to Chincheo in the first instance of Portuguese contact with Fujian. 232
	- Can it be assumed that these "pilots" encountered in the islands off Guangdong were natives of Quanzhou?
	- "It is not perfectly established whether Jorge de Mascarenhas in fact visited any city of the Fujian coast. It is probably likelier that he carried out his commercial dealings on the islands of the Bay of Amoy, by identical processes as those used in the Pearl River estuary. On the other hand, the Portuguese chroniclers included detailed descriptions of this city, just like they did for Canton. But, in reality, they only describe a few generalities about the inhabitants of those regions: they are gentiles, white, very clean and handsome, and men of "much friendliness and good conversation"." 233
	- Mascarenhas went further to Fuzhou, but was prevented from actually having any relations with this city. In the understanding of the Portuguese at this time, the two great cities of Chincheo and Foquiem were synonymous with the two adjacent but distinct regions or provinces around them. 

Chapter 9: The failed settlement in the Celestial Empire 
- Duarte Coelho returned to Malacca in March, 1518, with the earliest news of the Fernao Peres expedition. Malacca then decided to recall Peres de Andrade due to the city being under threat from hostile Malay powers. After himself recalling Mascarenhas (using the Chinese postal system!), Peres left for Malacca in September. 
- After Fernao  Peres' return to Goa in February 1519, Governor Diogo Lopes de Sequeira dispatched a second expedition to China under Simao de Andrade (younger brother of Fernao). de Andrade was accused of being "very pompous" and temperamentally unfit for diplomatic responsibilities. His first mistake was building a fortress on Tuen Muen, and unloading artillery there, both of which were taken as signs of hostile intent. Next, his choice to publically execute a Portuguese criminal was similarly seen as an affront to the sole judicial authority of the emperor.  Finally, he prevented the junks of other nations from disembarking at Tuen Muen until the Portuguese were finished selling their merchandise. 
	- Loureiro's evaluation: Andrade was probably proceeding from the precedent of Portuguese actions in other Asian ports, where they succeeded in dictating their terms through shows of force, as well as from a misunderstanding of his older brother's experiences. Even with his mistakes, his expedition was allowed to remain on Tuen Muen for a full year and departed peacefully (after hearing that Tome Pires had been allowed to visit the emperor in Nanjing). Finally, there is some reason to think that the decision to fortify an island on the Chinese coast was a direct royal order. 251-252. 
- After 1520, following the reception of concrete news and experiences via the de Andrade missions, the Manueline court developed a concrete political plan to "assure a permanent Portuguese presence in the Chinese littoral in a similar mold to what had been adopted in other Asian regions: a factory, granted its respective technical and administrative personnel, protected by a fortress and permanent navy, led by experience *fidalgos*. But the Manuelin project, although founded on trustworthy news gathered firsthand by Portuguese observers was condemned to failure, as we shall see." 260 

Chapter 10: The Embassy of Tomé Pires
- The embassy consisted of some two dozen Europeans, with a few biographies on 266-269.
- Tomé Pires and co. left Canton on January 23, 1520, almost three years after disembarking; their activities during this time are largely unknown. They reached Nanjing in May; the Zhengde Emperor was there to "coordinate" the war against the Prince of Ning. Tuan Mahamed, ambassador of the previous Sultan of Malacca, brought his sovereign's complaints against the Portuguese to Nanjing just before Pires arrived. The Portuguese were ordered to proceed to Beijing, which they reached in August, being quartered in the ambassador's mansions there and instructed in protocol; but Zhengde never returned to Beijing. 270-72.
- The letter the Portuguese brought to Beijing was rewritten by its translators to include language about accepting the vassalage of and paying tribute to the emperor, an improvisation was later to be "catastrophic," as the official interpreters who read both the early translation and the Portuguese original found the latter to diverge unacceptably from proper procedure. 273-4
	- As a result, the Portuguese were moved to new, suburban dwellings and held under strict vigilance, while their initial interpreters (hired in Malacca) were decapitated. 
- While Pires' embassy was on its way back to Canton, the news of Zhengde's death reached that city, just as a third Portuguese expedition arrived in the PRD. When they were told to leave at once, they resisted (probably not realizing the severity of the situation); as a result the members of Pires' embassy were arrested immediately upon reaching Canton, while most of the Portuguese ships were captured and their European members executed. 3 ships managed to escape to Malacca with the survivors. 277
- Once the Jiajing emperor ascended the throne, it was quickly decided to burn the Portuguese letters, refuse their gifts, and reject any diplomatic relations. Dom Manuel was instructed to return Malacca to its proper ruler before the prisoners would be released. 

Chapter 11: Confrontations on the Chinese Coast
- The next expedition to China consisted of four ships captained by Martim Afonso de Melo Coutinho, drawn both by the enormous profits possible and by a misunderstanding of the "gravity and irrevocability" of the imperial ban on the Portuguese. 290
	- The fleet stayed in Tuen Muen attempting to gain permission to trade and negotiate a settlement, but retreated after two weeks; as it sailed away two of its ships were captured and the crews executed. 
- The next few years saw difficulties in Malacca due to war with Bintao (Bintan), a sultanate in the strait of Singapore that hosted the former royal family of Malacca. This state was conquered by Péro de Mascarenhas in 1526, shortly before he left for Goa to take up the governorship (which he was prevented from doing), thus making conditions right for another attempt to reestablish links with China. 301
- In 1527, the factor Pero Barriga wrote to the king that three junks from Chincheo had arrived in Malacca, carrying "much porcelain many damasks and silks of many sorts". 303 
	- These "chincheos" were distinguished from the "Chinese," since the same factor wrote that other junks were still awaited from China. Sign that China was being equated with Canton?
	- In the same year Jorge Cabral mentioned two junks from Chincheo, "a subject kingdom of the King of China." 304
	- The Portuguese were also encouraged by the news which these ships brought that Tomé Pires was still alive and being treated well.
- By 1527, the question of whether to attempt a return to China was seen differently in the different centers of power: from the point of view of Lisbon, China was generally seen as one among many potential Asian markets, which was worth capturing but not at any great expense; to Goa, military exigencies "contributed to a devaluing of all peripheral Asian regions, that is, those which were not essential to the regular functioning of the spice trade on the *rota do Cabo*." In Malacca, China was seen as a crucial commercial outlet which could significantly contribute to the prosperity of the city, its officials, and its resident merchants. 307

Chapter 12: Reestablishment of relations with China
- In 1528, after confirming that Chinese junks were ready to return to Malacca, Jorge Cabral decided to send a ship to Canton. "The presence of Chinese in Malacca... would inevitably attract our ships to the coast of the Celestial Empire." Further, because the visitors to Malacca in these years from from "Chincheo," they attracted Portuguese navigators "to zones of the Chinese coast previously contacted little or not at all." 316
- In 1529 the Liangguang zongdu Lin Fu 林富 petitioned to reopen limited maritime trade in Guangdong; the court reopened Guangdong's ports to foreign trade, except that of the Portuguese, but local officials were ready to ignore this exception. 
- The first step on the Portuguese side towards reopening relations was to make peace in 1533 with Pao and Pattani, which were important sources of the pepper which the Portuguese needed for trade with China. 
- Strangely, there are few contemporary references to a successful Portuguese voyage to Canton in 1533, but it seems one did take place without incident. 320
- After 1533, normal traffic to China was resumed, but with the distinguishing feature that these voyages were largely private and took place outside ordinary legal structures: "after this we discovered more than fifty ports better than those of Cantam." (Quoting the Historia of Castanheda.) 320
	- Unfortunately the voyages of this period, being largely taken on by "fugitives" in cooperation with Chinese smugglers and wokou, left little impression in the official documentary record. 
	- The only European source confirming a Portuguese presence in Chinese waters in 1534 is a letter from Cristóvão Vieira to some Portuguese anchored in the islands off Guangdong. 321
- Loureiro lists three factors for the shift of Portuguese activity to Fujian and Zhejiang: 1. The continued hostility of the Guangdong military administration to the Portuguese, in part due to their memory of previous events as well as the greater attention of the court on that province's foreign relations; 2. The influence of mariners from "Chincheo," who guided the Portuguese to ports where the local population was open to mutually beneficial relations; 3. the letters of the Portuguese prisoners in Canton specifically praised the commercial potential of those regions. 
	- Cites a letter of Vasco Calvo's which describes the "governanças" of Foquiem and Chaqueam as semi-autonomous and rich in silk and silver (!). 323
	- Ningbo was called "Liampó"
	- Gaspar da Cruz: the Chinese smugglers "começaram a encaminhar os Portugueses a que fossem a Liampoo fazer fazenda, porque nam ha naquellas partes cidades nem villas cercadas senam muitas e grandes aldeas ao longo da costa." Praise of the opportunities for smuggling enabled by the thickly-settled but little-garrisoned coastal regions which were far from the walled cities of the major military-administrative centers. 
- During the captaincy of Pêro de Faria the traffic between Malacca and China seems to have developed regular rhythms and regulations, to the point that a 17th century observer in Lisbon saw a porcelain platter inscribed "En Tiempo de Pedro de Faria. Año 1541." Evidence of a custom order for a Portuguese or Castilian inscription on a Chinese porcelain! 329
- Introduction to Fr. Gaspar da Cruz as a main source for Portuguese activities in this period, 329-333.
- On Portuguese trade in South China 1530-40: "A situation of this kind could not exist on the basis of Portuguese will alone. Any commercial relation presupposes more or less convenient and willing partners. Thus, our presence in the Celestial Empire is also explained by the existence of Chinese economic interests and by the complicity or tolerance of certain local authorities." 332

Chapter 13: The Letters of the Canton Captives, 1534-36
- The Portuguese who survived their imprisonment in 1522 until Portuguese links with Canton were reestablished became the most experienced witnesses to the realities of Chinese life at the time. Specifically, Cristóvão Vieira and Vasco Calvo both wrote long letters between 1534 and 1536 which made their ways into Portuguese hands, and were rapidly brought back to Portugal and widely circulated. Both letters focused on relating the story of Tomé Pires' embassy, providing a synthetic overview of Chinese society (as it appeared from their cells), and proposing a Portuguese conquest of China. 351
	- The idea of conquering China had no impact whatsoever in Portugal, where such plans were consistently rejected by the Crown, but some of the concrete details included in the prison letters made their way into the growing European imagination about China. 355 

Chapter 14: The discovery of Japan and Luso-Chinese commerce
- Begins with a brief overview of Chinese bans on Japanese shipping and the wokou starting from the 1523 Ningbo incident. Covers the Portuguese introduction as a consequence of their activities around Fujian. 
	- What a coincidence that the Portuguese and Japanese relations with China broke down near-simultaneously in the very first years of the Jiajing reign! Loureiro connects these incidents by arguing that the Chinese coast was experiencing "a conjuncture of *armed commerce*." (Citing Roland Higgins) 365 
- Diogo do Couto describes a 1542 voyage to Chincheo by three Portuguese called Antonio da Mota, Francisco Zeimoto, and Antonio Feixoto. They were blown off course by a typhoon and landed on a mysterious island which proved to be part of Japan. 366-7
- The first Governor of India to really interest himself in China was Martim Afonso de Sousa, who was also the governor who received Francis Xavier. Afonso de Sousa began to officially authorize private voyages to China. 375 
	- 1544 exploratory voyage of one Pero Diez, who reached as far as Nanjing, described on 377
- By contrast, the governor João de Castro was more hostile to private trade and thus disinterested in the China route. He opened an inquisition in 1545 into the realities of the China trade, which found that it was mainly a secondary market for pepper but that it was highly important to the city of Malacca. It was decided to restrict private trade and attempt to bring the China route under the control of the Estado. In general, the Crown was fully disinterested in prospects of trade with China, and only two royal licenses for the voyage were granted between 1523 and 1564. 

Chapter 15: Jesuit missionaries and the route to China
- The basic situation of royal indifference to China mixed with local functionaries approving the journeys of adventurers was altered by the introduction of two new independent factors in the 1540s: first, the discovery of Japan, which intensified the Portuguese navigations in the East China Sea; and the arrival of the first Jesuit missionaries, whose interest in the cultures of Asia "marked the beginning of a new phase of accumulation of news about China." 397
	- Conclusion: Before the shifts of the 1540s, the Portuguese imagined the legitimate sphere of their Asian activities as extending from Hormuz to the Moluccas, and the China Seas were thus mainly attractive to adventurers who wanted to work out of sight from the Estado. This perspective was changed by the new perspective of the Japan convoys and the Jesuit contact with China. 416

Chapter 16: Portuguese Prisoners and Chinese Justice
- João de Castro gave permission for Diego Pereira to travel to China due to Francis Xavier's intercession. 421
- Points to economic crisis in Guangdong, the relative lightness of the wokou problem there, and the renewed attractiveness of Portuguese trade bringing Japanese goods such as silver as reasons for Guangdong officials to reevaluate trade with the folangji. 439

Chapter 17: Images of China in the culture of Expansion, 1549-1553
- Largely focuses on the *História* of Fernão Lopes de Castanheda, which transmitted a great deal of new information to European audiences, and which took a generally admiring tone about China, contributing to heightening expectations about trade with China, soon to be realized. 459

Chapter 18: The Spiritual Conquest of the Middle Kingdom
- Focuses on Francis Xavier and the Jesuit propaganda network which developed around the potential of the East Asian kingdoms as fields for conversion. 

Chapter 19: From Sanchoão to Lampacau: The First Luso-Chinese Treaty
- In 1549, multiple Portuguese ships anchored at Sanchoão (Shangchuan), where their presence and trade was tolerated by local authorities. One reason the Portuguese shifted their attentions there was the increasing vigilance of the Chinese navy in the waters of Zhejiang and Fujian, where a Portuguese ship had been seized just previously. 
- 493-502: The negotiations led by Leonel de Sousa with ended with official permission for the Portuguese to trade in the islands south of the Pearl River Delta after 1554. In particular, they were granted the right to build residences on the deserted islands of Lumpaquão and Quoay; there are questions about the latter name, but it may have referred to the peninsula which the Portuguese came to call (already by 1555) "Macau." Fernão Mendes Pinto was among the first Portuguese to use this name, while he anchored there en route to bringing several Jesuits to Japan.
- In the course of negotiations the Portuguese were repeatedly permitted to enter Canton for several weeks at a time, and the Jesuits who accompanied them prepared their first sets of observations about Chinese character and customs at this time.
	- Father Melchior wrote a *Enformaçao de algumas cousas acerca dos costumes e leis do reino da China*, which was sent to Portugal in 1554. 508
	- Melchior was highly pessimistic about the prospect of converting the Chinese, and joked that it would only be possible "if God makes new ones, because it would be too much to talk about it with  the ones presently existing on earth." 509 
- A letter from Father Melchior in late 1554 referred to "thirty-some thousand quintas of pepper and a hundred thousand cruzados in silver" stockpiled at Lampacau; Loureiro notes that this was the equivalent of the entire pepper cargo of the *carrera da India*. 509-510

Chapter 20
- Looks at the earliest Portuguese accounts of China following the 1554 treaty: an anonymous Enformaçao, the Jesuit accounts, and Galiote Pereira's *Tratado*. 
- Summary: The later diffusion of the *tratado*, working above all through the information channels of the Jesuits, permitted the definition of China as an exemplary society, where the Portuguese could gain inspiration for the perfection of their own institutions. Further, the awareness of the defensive capacities proven by the Portuguese captives helped marginalize the idea that China would be easy to conquer. China's image became more positive, and also more rigorous and realistic. 536

Chapter 21: Origins of Macau - Myths and Reality, 1557-1563
- The anchorage called Macau began to develop vertiginously after 1557 for reasons that are still imperfectly understood. 
- On conflict between fidalgos and the Estado at this time: "Porém, de uma forma geral, os primeiros anos da presença portuguesa em Lampacau, e depois em Macau, foram vividos quase totalmente a margem do Estado da India, demasiado longínquino para poder exercer alguma espécie de controlo. A iniciativa nos mares da China pertencia sobretudo aos mercadores e aos aventureiros. 549
- Chinese accounts claim that the Portuguese were first permitted to anchor at Macau in 1553 on the pretext of having been shipwrecked. They began to habitually anchor there each season until, after 1557, the local authorities simply ceased obliging them to leave between seasons. Part of the reason longer stays became necessary was because of the long delays involved in catching the monsoons from Malacca to Japan, since the monsoon season for both voyages was in the late spring to summer. 550 
- Loureiro understands the decision of the mandarins to allow Portuguese to reside at Macau in almost entirely economic terms, though also makes reference to maritime security. 
- In 1563 the Portuguese petitioned to send an embassy to Beijing, with the goal of requesting free movement for the Jesuits, and a mandarin came to Macau to inspect their preparations. Their gifts were deemed insufficient, and they were ordered to provide two elephants to be allowed to proceed. Some of the Jesuit fathers blamed the greed of the Portuguese merchants, who were thought to have sabotaged the mission out of fear that the embassy would dominate the year's trade. 

Chapter 22: Consolidation of Porto do Nome de Deus
- By 1563, Macau had grown to a settlement of 900 Portuguese and 1500 slaves and servants, living in permanent wooden houses. 
- Pokes holes in the historiographical tradition which attributes the foundation of Macau to Portuguese help against wokou, finding that the crucial move to Lampacau, and thereafter the beginnings of regular anchorage at Macau, occurred before the battle against pirates which took place in 1564. The incident began when a naval force returning from a battle against the wokou mutinied due to arrears in their salaries (!). These troops began looting the coasts of the Pearl River region, but the Portuguese were consistently able to hold them at bay from their own possessions, and lent help to the local mandarins; Portuguese success in a quick series of battles favorably impressed Cantonese authorities, helping to distinguish the *folangji* from the *wokou*. 567 
- A note that an original yearly bribe to the zongdu was eventually normalized as a "rent" for Macau itself. 584
