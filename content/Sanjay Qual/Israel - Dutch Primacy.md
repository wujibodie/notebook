
- Begins from the understanding that a European "world economy" was created after 1500 or so by the economic domination of European powers even where they did not rule territorially. 
	- Critiques Braudel's schema of successive centers to the European world economy: it "implies a greater degree of continuity in the form and functions of these world economic empires than is really warranted by the context. It misses the crucial shift both in scope and degree of concentration of economic power." Everything before Amsterdam belongs to a "late medieval poly-nuclear phase of expansion." 4
	- Antwerp's dominance of warehousing for almost all Western European international trade was a "transitional phenomenon, sandwiched between the characteristically regional emporia of the Middle Ages and the fully fledged world emporium of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries." Concentration in a single place only truly began after Antwerp's decline; also critiques Braudel's shift from the Mediterranean to the Protestant North, arguing that "economic vitality" shifted to the North Netherlands (to a lesser extent England) alone after 1585. 5
	- Distinction between a mere "depot," like Antwerp was earlier on, and an "active entrepot, with a dominating merchant elite, such as Antwerp was tending to become during the later phase of its world supremacy." 7
	- "The questions when, how, and in what sense the Dutch entrepot supplanted Antwerp and the regional emporia of the past to become the first true world entrepot revolve at bottom around two issues-the relationship between bulk carrying and the rich trades, on the one hand, and, on the other, that between a passive and active entrepot." Braudel argued that around 1590 bulk carrying became the key to the "rich trades," and this was an area the Dutch dominated due to foregoing preponderance in shipping grain, salt, fish, timber, etc.; shifts in European consumption patterns, esp. heightened food demand in the Mediterranean, gave the keys to silver-rich Iberia and Italy to Holland. 
		- Israel thinks attributing Dutch primacy to their bulk shipping is a "profound misconception," because bulk shipping did not necessarily bring mastery of more valuable trades; even in the early decades of Dutch shipping to the Med. the cargoes were financed by Italians. Instead of Braudel's secular rise and fall determined by the Baltic bulk freighting (1590-1660), Israel sees 7 phases of Dutch trade decided above all by "shifts in the Dutch share of the high-value trades." 10-11

Chapter Two: Origins of Dutch Hegemony
- "if for us today it constitutes a historical question of the first order that this tiny entity should, for a century and a half, have functioned as the hub of world commerce, the universal intermediary of trade, possessing, at its height, roughly half of the world's total stock of seagoing ships, this phenomenon struck contemporaries not just as prodigious but also as profoundly disturbing." 12
- van der Kooy in the 1931 explained Dutch primacy as resulting from the early modern world having a "particular, structural need of a central reservoir of goods," to ameliorate the price inconsistencies of the new worldwide trade; Holland was ideally situated to serve this role. J.I. thinks this fails to explain why there should be one such depot and not many equal ones, and also doesn't distinguish active control of commerce (including monopolistic raising of prices) from a passive entrepot. "The core of the problem posed by van der Kooy's theory for the historian is that it does not explain why, despite the devices of engrossment and monopolistic practices adopted by the Dutch entrepot, competition from potential rivals, even English trade rivalry, remained relatively weak" 16
	- Only way to understand this problem is to accept the "harnessing of the Dutch entrepot to the machinery of the Dutch state." 16
- Sees the initial build-up of the Dutch fleet as being a consequence both of new techniques and of a lack of dominant merchants: not only did the divided commercial world require more total ships, but they needed new ways of financing shipbuilding, including sales of 16th, 32nd, and even 64th shares in ships. (German and Flemish shipping tended to be broken into just three or four shares at most.) 21
- Major goods shipped east to the Baltic were Iberian salt, French wine, and herring. As early as 1523 Middelburg was recognized as official staple for French wine for the entire Hapsburg Netherlands. Emphasizes the broad social basis of involvement in the herring fisheries, with thousands of investors and tens of thousands of laborers, and with heavy participation by small towns as well as great cities. Sees the need for provincial collaboration, rather than Amsterdam dominance, as an "abiding pillar of strength." 24-25
- Another important point was the increasing specialization of Dutch agriculture due to rising imports of grain, leading to growing exports of dairy and releasing labor from farming to shipping and manufactures. 
- The 1560s and 70s were not promising for Dutch trade, as the Flemish merchant elite fled "the Spanish rage" not to Holland but to England or Germany, and internal conflict split Amsterdam (Catholic until 78) from the rest of Holland. Antwerp began to revive until its siege and capture in 1585. 29-30
	- After 1585 about 100,000 Flemish Protestants fled, mostly to the North Netherlands. However, the United Provinces at this point was also in recession due to the Spanish embargo and occupations. 30-31
	- Underlines the crucial role of the thousands of textile workers who settled in Leiden and Haarlem (and in smaller numbers elsewhere).

Chapter 3: Breakthrough to World Primacy
- "The year 1590 was a decisive turning-point for the hitherto floundering United Provinces." The key moment was Philip II's decision to halt his Dutch offensives to turn the Army of Flanders towards France (where Henri de Navarre was still a Protestant), along with lifting the embargo on the UP (but not England). The early 1590s then emerged as the general entrepot for Iberian and Iberian overseas commodities. 
	- The final subjection of Flanders to the North was effected by the Dutch combined blockade of the Scheldt Estuary and the Flemish sea-coast, which effectively forced both bulk and high-value trade to shift north, along with their attendant industries. 
- One example of a rapid Dutch "conquest" of a high-value trade was their displacing of the English Muscovy Company from the pepper and cloth sales to Archangelsk between 1600-1609. Two factors: the arrival of capital and expertise from Antwerp, Hamburg, Cologne, and better access than England to costly wines, cloths, spices, sugar, and silver. 
	- Calls this route a "deficit trade" because 20-30% of the value of exports was paid in Spanish silver. 
	- The great early consortia which organized the Archangel trade from Amsterdam were run by Antwerp exiles, like Marcus de Vogelaer, "one of the greatest Amsterdam merchants of the early seventeenth century."
	- Balthasar de Moucheron of Middelburg gained state backing for a series of voyages in search of a north-east passage past Nova Zemblaya, "an early and highly significant indication of the Dutch merchant elite's capacity to harness the resources of the Dutch state." 48
- Although the Dutch were the major bulk shippers in the Baltic overall, they had little sway in the northern areas which did not export grain. The "second Dutch conquest of the Baltic" followed the burgeoning after 1590 of imports of southern luxuries (pepper, sugar, dried fruits). The Dutch came to replace the Hansa cities as main importers of spices to the Baltic after 1597. 51
	- Even then relatively few of the merchant elite, whether foreign or native, were very involved in the Baltic trade. 
- In the Mediterranean, Israel argues that the Dutch grain trade after 1590 was not so dominant as Braudel supposed, and that progress in the "rich trades" was slow in any case and largely based on the development of Dutch textile industries and Russian luxuries (Italians bought "39/40ths" of caviar according to one merchant). The resumption of Spanish embargoes, now against Dutch shipping at Iberian ports, also had a devastating effect. 56
- Dutch traffic to the Caribbean was spearheaded by the Hoorn salt fleets, in search of new sources after the closure of Spanish ports, but a lively "rich trade" developed in smuggling with the Spanish colonies as well as in tobacco from Venezuela and Guyana. This trade was largely shut down by Spanish countermeasures after 1603. 
- The first Dutch East India Company was the Compagnie van Verre, founded in Amsterdam in 1594. Further companies were founded in Middelburg, Rotterdam and Amsterdam again. By 1601 14 fleets had visited the Far East, and annual shipping on this route exceeded that of the Portuguese. At that time competition was driving up prices in Indonesia and bringing them down in Holland, such that the directors of the various companies petitioned Oldenbarnevelt to arrange what became the VOC. The Heren XVII were appointed partially on the basis of capital invested and partially out of political balancing between the regions involved, such that Amsterdam had 8 seats (just under half despite contributing half the capital), South Holland and West Friesland had two each, and Zeeland had 4, with the 17th seat rotating between the last three. 
- The Frisians were disgruntled with their lack of representation and proposed to found a Frisian East India Company in 1604, but were blocked from doing this by Holland, while other discontents proposed a joint Franco-Dutch East India Company. 
- In 1605, after major investments in warships and troops, the Dutch conquered Amboina, Tidore, and Ternate and thus gained a monopoly on nutmeg, mace, and cloves. 73 
- Finance: "Just as tenuous, irregular communications created the conditions for a central store of commodities, so the same slowness and unpredictability of communication favoured the concentration of financial services at a a single point." Dutch financial dominance as largely a consequence of the Dutch staple market, although attractive institutions (including a low interest rate) helped. 73
	- The immigration of specialized outsiders facilitated the development of brokerages (since brokers need experience but were not allowed to trade themselves), with Sephardic Jews specialized in the Levant and American trades especially important. 
	- Importance of back rooms as well as the Bourse: "Joseph Penso de la Vega, whose Confusion de Confusiones ( 1688) is the world's first account of a stock exchange, describes how the dealers in VOC and WIG shares in the 1680s generally met in coffee-shops, where they could lounge interminably over their tobacco and cups of coffee and tea, contemplating trends and the latest news and doing deals."
	- The Amsterdam Wisselbank was founded in 1609, in response to years of chaos in local exchange rates. 
	- One benefit of persistently low interest rates was that it helped Dutch factors get an advantage in making bulk advance purchases, which helped lower prices. 79
Chapter Four: The Twelve Years' Truce, 1609-1621
- The second phase of Dutch primacy starts in 1609, when the lifting of the Spanish embargo makes further dominance of the Mediterranean possible. This phase was characterized by consistently lower Dutch freight charges during the Truce, giving them an advantage over their commercial rivals. 89
	- The truce also saw the highest proportion of Dutch bulk shipping moving directly between the Mediterranean and Baltic, without other stops. 91
	- This period also saw the brief eclipse of English shipping to the Levant, as a result of better Dutch shipping rates and access to Spanish silver, but this advantage disappeared abruptly in 1621. 101
- The negotiations of 1606-09 turned on Lerma's offer to recognize Dutch independence in exchange for the dissolution of the VOC and retreat from American and Asian waters. This was a tempting offer to many, especially as the Spanish embargoes and privateering were seen as handing the European commercial advantage to England. However, Amsterdam, with its city council led by Renier Pauw, was adamant on protecting the VOC. Zeeland also worried that an end to the blockade of Flanders would divert their trade to the south. 
	- In the final peace Oldebarnevelt kept the VOC (which promised to refrain from attacking Iberian possessions), but did block the formation of a West Indian Company as a concession to Spain. 
	- The Heren XVII complained that the VOC was prevented from seizing Macao and Manila, and thus taking the Chinese silk trade, which would "break Italy's ago-old hold over the European silk market and would thereby divert immense profit and employment to itself."
- Christian IV raised tolls on the Sound and blocked Dutch shipping to Sweden in 1611, but was forced to back down in the face of threats from a Dutch-Swedish-Lubeck alliance in 1614; thereafter a "*pax neerlandica*" prevailed in the Baltic, with an enforced policy of mare liberum which stood in contrast to Dutch actions elsewhere. 95
- In the 1610s the Dutch position in the spice trades weakened due to the resumption of Portuguese shipping and the appearance of English factories in the Bandas, and the Dutch engaged in a "mini-war" to eject English settlements ion Java and the Bandas. The English helped the sultan of Bantam besiege the newly-founded Batavia. To avoid a two-front war with the end of the truce, the Heren XVII agreed to split the fine spice trade on a 1/3-2/3 basis with the EIC, in exchange for cooperation against the Iberians. 106
- Dutch trade to the Americas nearly ceased altogether during the truce, being replaced with transshipment via Lisbon, and so many recognized the need for a West India Company. Prince Maurice prevented this, however, until the formal resumption of hostilities. 
- Finds that there was a tight link between Dutch industry and the Dutch preeminence in world trade, with the manufactures "gaining greatly in variety and importance" and further contributing to commerce. However, JI thinks it is excessive to say (contra Wallerstein) the United Provinces were the "leading producer of industrial products" in their time; they never produced more textiles than England and France, let alone reaching the dominance of Britain in the late 18th century. However there were a few high-value industries, intimately tied with foreign trade, where the Dutch did surpass their rivals, in some cases becoming the majority producer in industries like cloth bleaching and dying, lens grinding, whale-oil refining, diamond cutting, etc., where access to rare materials and high-quality techniques mattered.
	- The "New Draperies" of Leiden succeeded in growing during the Truce even under competition from the South due to the improvement of techniques and opening of new (Iberian) markets. 
	- Dutch wages (and living expenses) were as much as twice those of their competitors in most industries, further reinforcing the need to specialize in high-value manufactures. 
	- English cloth manufacturers suffered a major defeat when the 1614 "Cockayne Project," which banned the export of unfinished cloths to the UP, was met with a Dutch ban on imports of *finished* cloth; deprived of their biggest market, English textiles entered a recession, and James I was forced to rescind his ban even while the Dutch kept theirs in place, locking the English into supplying raw cloth to be processed and sold by the Dutch. 119-120

Chapter Five: The Dutch and the Crisis of the World Economy, 1621-1647
- After 1620 the Dutch (and world) economy entered a period of stagnation and "profound restructuring." On explanations for the crisis, quickly dispatches Chaunu's thesis about lower silver imports and then critiques Braudel and De Vries' emphasis on demand-side, demographic factors as failing to fit the timeline of Dutch shipping. Israel wants to give a larger role to warfare, particularly the 30 Years' War, which extended further and was more broadly destructive than other periods of fighting. 
- First sign of crisis after 1621: Dutch shipping of Iberian salt was never fully replaced by English, Hanseatic, and Danish freighters, with the result that salt became chronically scarce and expensive in the north. 129
- Spanish silver also became more expensive as it had to be routed to Amsterdam via intermediaries. The main routes involved Calais or Bayonne, and were often managed via Amsterdam Sephardim working with French and Iberian New Christians. 
	- To block smuggling to the Dutch, in 1624 Philip III set up the Almirantazgo, first only in Andalucia, to inspect all outside shipping. 132
		- Through diplomatic treaties, the Almirantazgo was extended to check shipping out of Britain, Germany, and Denmark as well, with licenses from specific ports required for shipping to Iberia. 
		- Along with intensive privateering from Dunkirk, Spanish policy caused a drastic rise in Dutch freight rates. 134
- Christian IV cooperated with the Spanish to exclude Dutch shipping to the Baltic, and the Dutch were unable to fully respond due to the war in the South Netherlands. In 1643, however, the Swedish invaded Jutland, and in 1645 a Dutch fleet finally arrived in Helsingor and forced the Danes to lower the Sound toll and cease inspections of Dutch shipping. 149
- When the West India Company was set up, it was particularly successful at raising funds from inland town councils, which were often eager to profit from war with Spain; Amsterdam only contributed 20% of the starting capital for this venture. 
	- Most of the non-Amsterdam and Zeeland directors in the company were burgomasters or other office-holders. "The company was intimately intertwined with the ruling Dutch regent oligarchy." 160
	- For its first decade, despite massive outlays, the WIC largely failed to gain a foothold in the Americas. Finally major conquests in Brazil and Curacao were completed in 1634 and shipments of sugar, tobacco, and brazilwood began to make the WIC profitable. They also temporarily overran Portuguese positions in West Africa and began large-scale slave transfers at this time. After the American colonies were opened to trade from outside the company, the Brazil business came into the hands of the Amsterdam Sephardim, many of whom moved to Brazil, by virtue of their familiarity with and contacts in Portugal. 165
	- Truce with Portugal in 1641 meant a collapse in sugar prices followed by a revolt of Portuguese planters in Dutch Brazil. The collapse of the WIC's position in Brazil led to a drastic fall in share prices followed by a reevaluation of its business: after 1646 the WIC began to advocate for peace with Spain in order to refocus on peaceful commerce in the Americas rather than grand colonial conquests. 170
- The loss of Spanish silver sources made the Japan trade vitally important to Batavia, and Coen made diplomatic inroads there at the expense of the English, Spanish, and Portuguese. However, they needed Chinese silks for the trade to be meaningful, and so began a campaign of belligerence against Chinese shipping in 1621, starting with the capture of junks on the way to Manila. The VOC also blockaded Portuguese shipping from Goa. 
	- JI has a rather confused account of the events that led to the foundation of their Taiwan factory in 1624. 173
	- By 1623 the VOC had 90 ships and 2,000 troops posted to 20 forts, and could easily bully the English factors despite agreements to cooperate.
	- In Gujarat the Dutch were stymied by formidable local trade protected by the Mughals; "there seems little justification for categorizing Gujarati enterprise as a "pedlar traffic" which was in some way innately backward." Their only advantage in West India was better access to spices, especially cloves. 179-80
	- The new Governor-General van Diemen (1636-1645) used increased reserves of Japanese silver to expand commercially and militarily, culminating in the conquest of Galle and Malacca (1640, 1641). The 1641 truce caused the VOC to redirect its attention to Manila and Ternate. 
- "To view the United Provinces as essentially an extension of the needs and interests of Amsterdam is to miss totally the special character of the Dutch state and the precise nature of its contribution to the making of Dutch world-trade hegemony." It was the province of Holland as a whole, with at least ten sizeable towns, that dominated the Dutch state and the world economy. 
- Major internal change in the Dutch economy during this period was the further expansion and specialization of the Leiden textile industry, which by the 1640s was producing many times its 1630 volume of high-quality *Lakens* and camlets, while its "coarser" textile production (says and fustians) declined. This new focus would go on to contribute to Dutch penetration of overseas markets after 1648. 

Chapter 6: The Zenith, 1647-72
- With the end of the 80 Years' War, the Dutch gained access to all European markets as well as annexing North Brabant, confirming its colonial conquests, and accepted the permanent closure of the Scheldt to maritime traffic. The most immediate victim of the peace was England, which saw its trade to Iberia and the Baltic collapse. Many Italian merchants also switched their business to Amsterdam over London, while Dutch access to Cadiz silver helped them retake market share in the Levant trade. From London's perspective the "Dutch world entrepot was simply too successful for its own good," and the result was the First Anglo-Dutch War. 207
	- Starting point for the war was the 1651 Navigation Act, which stipulated that all imports had to be shipped either by English ships or those of the country of export, cutting out the Dutch middlemen. The English also began intercepting Dutch shipping wherever possible. The war which followed in 1652-3 saw English victories around the home islands, with the capture of around 1200 merchant ships, but in the final peace Parliament failed to win any commercial concessions, in part because the Dutch managed to block English trade everywhere except the Channel and North Sea. JI sees the war as proof of "the underlying strength of the Dutch world-trade system." 210
- Dutch trade to the Baltic stagnated and was put under severe threat by the victories of Charles X; the Dutch navy had to intervene to relieve the siege of Copenhagen and reopen the Sound in 1659. However, various English and French schemes (together with the Danes and Swedes) to break the Dutch hold on Baltic shipping failed and were abandoned by the 1670s. 
- With the end of war in Europe, the VOC was able to recruit some 10,000 veteran soldiers for its garrisons in Asia. However, the profitability of the company's main trades was floundering, especially due to interruptions in the flow of Japanese silver after 1650. 245
	- Mentions the Dutch factory in Perak as one attempt to secure the economic foundations of Malacca. 
	- After 1660 the VOC responded by remitting its highest ever levels of Spanish bullion to Batavia from Amsterdam, so that the VOC could shift away from the Chinese silk trade disrupted by war. 
	- In the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the VOC took over multiple English outposts and also subjugated Makassar, which was forced to exclude other Europeans from its territory. The peace with England in 1674 permanently excluded the EIC from the Bandas. 
	- Utterly vague description of the loss of Taiwan to a "Chinese force" and of the Qing refusal to grant the Dutch trading privileges. 254
- The final conquest of Ceylon and resulting cinnamon imports led VOC shares to reach their highest level of the 17th century in 1671. 256
- "The four great rich trades fed on and reinforced each other. On less than a hundred ships, the Dutch brought in each year some fifty million guilders' worth of riches from Batavia, Cadiz, Smyrna, and Archangel. After 1647 the four were in a crucial sense a collectivity." 258
- After 1650 the Dutch began to specialize in rolling "blended" tobacco, which combined home-grown with higher quality foreign tobacco. 
- Basic question: why did Dutch dominance survive its naval defeats to the English? JI's basic answer is that the Dutch were still strategically dominant everywhere but the North Sea, and so were able to put commercial pressure on the English that outweighed Dutch defeats in the marquee naval encounters. In this the Dutch were aided by a general fear that English hegemony would be even more unsupportable than Dutch hegemony, such that the Danes and Swedes supported the United Provinces. 275
- Dutch customs returns "confirm that the real rhythm in Dutch overseas enterprise was not up until 1650 and then down, as Braudel and the Braudelians supposed, but up (1609-21), then down (1621-47), then again up (1647-71 ) , and then only finally down from 1672." 280
- The real blow to Dutch trade came not from England but from France, which was better positioned to harm the Republic's commercial interests and less vulnerable to economic pressure in turn. Louis XIV and Colbert ramped up mercantilist pressure after the end of the Anglo-Dutch War in 1667, raising tariffs on most major Dutch exports. 

Chapter 7: Beyond the Zenith, 1672-1700. 
- "Phase Five" began with the crash that accompanied Louis XIV's declaration of war on April 6 1672, in alliance with England. Although a joint Anglo-French naval invasion was blocked, the French quickly occupied the eastern half of the Republic, and merchants and capital fled, while all Dutch shipping was cut off for 2 years. In the end, though, Holland's defenses held, while privateering caused England to panic and the Spanish-Austrian entry into the war diverted France's attentions. 
- Colbert's primary goal in invading Holland was to capture (some of) three of the four Dutch "rich trades," namely those of the Levant, the East Indies, and the Caribbean. 296
- After peace was made with England in 1674, the Dutch reentered and even strengthened their position in the Baltic based on improved relations with Sweden. Danish tariffs on Dutch goods were suspended in 1688 following a Dutch counter-embargo, but the Dutch hold on Norwegian traffic in particular was slowly eroded by the expansion of the Dano-Norwegian merchant marine. 
- The Levant was the one major success of the French commercial offensive, and the most serious setback to Dutch primacy in this phase, as French traders came to hold the dominant position from Egypt to Constantinople. 
- France's wars with Spain in the 1670s and 90s provided opportunities for the Dutch to further dominate Iberian and American commerce. Guyana and Suriname also developed lucrative sugar plantations at this time under direct Dutch management, though their production did not replace the need for sugar purchases at Lisbon. 326
- The Dutch also lost their hegemony along the Guinea Coast, especially following the foundation of several French forts in the region. 
- The VOC successfully defended itself in India and Ceylon against French attacks, and it remained the dominant commercial power in South and East Asia. However, the English, French, and Danish all succeeded in inserting themselves in coastal Indian trade at the expense of the Dutch. This period also saw a restructuring of Asian trade away from spices and towards Indian silks and cottons along with tea, coffee, and porcelain. 336
	- The shift in trading patterns worked against the VOC, since Indian textiles largely came from Gujarat and Bengal, where the Dutch lacked political power. Thus "by 1700, the VOC's naval and military establishment in Asia was an inherently less efficient instrument for promoting the Company's trade, and obstructing its rivals, than it had been in the past." 336
- "In the closing decades of the century, the growing reliance of the Dutch entrepot on the rich trades, and therefore on export industries, was turning Holland into the technical research laboratory of the western world." 357
	- Inventions included the street lamp and the fire-engine, both of which produced minor export industries. 

Chapter 8: The War of the Spanish Succession, 1700-1713
- At the start of the 18th century the Dutch position had visibly passed its peak. 1701 was the first peacetime year when the Dutch share of Danish Sound shipping was under 50%. 
- Philip V of Anjou proved hostile to Dutch trading interests as soon as he took the Spanish throne, and the Dutch were particularly incensed by the erection of tariff barriers in Flanders and the reopening of the Scheldt. 
- When England broke ranks to negotiate a separate peace, the Dutch and Austrians were forced to agree to rather poor terms in the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht. Philip V remained on the throne of Spain, with promises to keep separate from France, while the Dutch received no redress for their original commercial complaints, although the assignment of the South Netherlands to Austria was preferable to its going over to the Bourbons. This peace prepared the way for the seventh stage, "in which the basic mechanisms of Dutch world-trade primacy were largely destroyed." 376

Chapter 9: Decline Relative and Absolute, 1713-1740
- The question of the extent of Dutch decline in the 18th century is the most debated topic in Dutch economic history. Johan de Vries famously argued that there was no "absolute decline," only a relative failure to keep up with expansion elsewhere. Jan de Vries argues that the Dutch economy did decline after 1670 but recovered after 1750, while still others see no decline of any kind (looking especially at per-capita income). 
	- JI's view, in agreement with 18th century Dutch writers Luzac and de Pinto: "the Dutch economy suffered fundamental decay during the eighteenth century," especially in industry, with only the East India trade and fisheries retaining some vitality. 379
	- Sees the root cause of the dispute as a misunderstanding of Dutch shipping, which gives too much importance to the relatively stable bulk shipping and not enough to the rich trades, which suffered "catastrophic collapse" in the 1720s and 30s. Where bulk trading grew, as in French wines and colonial goods, it often bypassed the Dutch entrepots. 
	- One reason for the collapse of the rich trades was the Dutch displacement from the Mediterranean, but even more important was the "industrial mercantilism" which swept Europe after 1720 (connected to "absolutism"?). 383
		- In many cases the growth of manufacturing elsewhere was assisted by the importation of Dutch techniques along with strong tariff barriers. 
		- In particular Russia, Prussia, and Sweden-Finland all shut out Dutch finished cloths, refined sugar, and tobacco to stimulate their own industries, and Denmark followed along in 1632. Holland increasingly transshipped raw materials rather than finished goods. 
- The end result of these changes was that after 1740 the Republic reverted to a "passive staple or storehouse" mainly engaged in reshipping colonial goods, having lost its "active" financial and manufacturing base. 

Chapter 11: Conclusion
- "It was only with the rise of large fleets of full-rigged ships, the penetration of the Baltic by French and Iberian bulky goods such as wine and salt, and the oceanic discoveries, all in the fifteenth century, that there arose a world economy in the sense of a network of closely and regularly interacting markets spanning most of the globe." 405
- Again affirms that Braudel's list of dominant trading cities should be pared down to Antwerp-Amsterdam-London for the sole period when world trade really was concentrated in a single point, from the late 16th to late 19th centuries, Antwerp being a transitional stage only. 
	- "Whereas Antwerp acquired a powerful merchant elite only towards the end of its era of primacy, and never fully completed the transition from passive staple to active, controlling entrepot, the Dutch Republic functioned as an active, controlling force for the whole of the century and a half from 1590 down to 1740." 408
- The advance of the United Provinces at the expense of Antwerp and England should be attributed in large measure to the strong federal state, which forcibly maintained favorable conditions for Dutch traders across the world, both through wars and treaties in Europe and through the organization of the VOC and WIC, while also fostering an internal culture conducive to cheap and widespread investment in commerce. 
- Closing remarks: "The notion that the United Provinces was, in any sense, political or economic, the city-state of Amsterdam, implying that Amsterdam ruled the rest as Venice and Genoa ruled their subject territories, is a total misconception. Nowhere else in the early modern world was the close economic collaboration of a network of maritime towns, inland manufacturing towns, fishing ports, and inland specialized agriculture anything like so intricately organized and federated as in the Dutch Republic during the seventeenth century." 415
