
A new pattern of (especially Persophone) elite circulation after 1550 or so: “In contrast to the bulk of these medieval migrations, the period from the latter half of the fifteenth century, and especially after 1550, marks something of a departure from the pattern. Now one has the circulation of elites proceeding even in the absence of conquest, elites who – moreover – are noted for their ability to combine commercial activities with the more conventional skills noted above.” 23

Rise in migration of Persian courtiers to India in particular after 1600. Example of Muhammad Said Ardistani of Golconda, about whom a British observer noted “Concerning forran navigation, he hath trade to Pegue, Tenasaree, Acheen, Rackan, Persia, Ben- galla, Moka, Peruk, Maldeevaes and Macassar. He hath ten vessels of his owne, and intends to augument them, makeing much preparatyon for building of more.” 25

“An alternative way of posing these phenomena might be to argue that if the two categories with which we began, namely agrarian-based, prebendally organized states, and trade-oriented ones, can be treated as two ends of a spectrum or distribution, what the period witnesses is a growing attraction towards the center, as opposed to a polarization. In part, this change is mediated through the growth of a sort of absolutism and it is of some interest that both Hideyoshi and Shah ‘Abbas have been characterized as “absolutist” in their own historiographies. To elaborate, the circulation of elites, the circulation of money-media, as well as certain other unifying phenomena, caused the direction taken by different Asian states in, the period to appear similar. However, we may still speak in terms of differing degrees of interest and involvement in trade, of monetization, of measurement and enumeration of resources and the tax-base; from a choice between two bald categories, we emerge with a spectrum of possibilities, within which we can place Aceh, Arakan, Iran, Japan, and the Ottoman empire.” 29-30

Conflict from 1383-85 between Joao of Avis (supported by the south, the urban artisans, and merchants) vs. the “Castilian party” (more support in the north, the interior, and among nobles and clergymen) won by Avis, resulting in further “meridianalizaçāo” and more maritime occupation alongside greater assertion of monarchic absolutism. 

In the 1470s-80s Portuguese nobles petitioned the crown to restrict Jewish traders on the basis that they encouraged Castilian corsairs (who considered Jews fair game), but instead Dom Joao II negotiated safe passage for Portuguese Jews with Castile. 

Understanding of Portuguese “mercantilism” as a form of “monarchical capitalism” (Manuel Nunes Dias) by contrast to the Company model of English and Dutch Indies trade. 48

Portuguese and Castilian monarchies were similar in their attempts to chart a course between the towns and the rural nobility, or between the interior and maritime worlds, but differed in their precise involvement in overseas trade. SS sees this difference as being expressed most clearly in the feitoria system, in which the Portuguese crown placed “factors” abroad along the same line as the Italian fondachi, as salaried employees of “royal” economic interests which were only vaguely distinguished from “state” concerns. The feitoria system can be traced to late medieval Bruges, where the Portuguese quarter gained legal status and eventually 2 consuls. In addition to these the Portuguese crown eventually deputed feitors from ~1400 to handle its own commercial interests (particularly purchases for the royal household), but these did not replace the consuls or have responsibilities over the Portuguese community as such. This office was permanently established by the 1460s and moved to Antwerp in 1498. 

- Thus, the epithet used by the French King François to describe Dom Manuel in the early sixteenth century – the “grocer king” (le roi épicier) – had roots going back into the early fifteenth century, and can perhaps even be traced as far back as Dom Fernando (r. 1367–83), who some evidence suggests – operated a mercantile fleet of around a dozen vessels 51
    
- The first feitoria in India was set up at Cochin in 1502, on de Gama’s second expedition, following hostilities with Calicut. From the start this feitoria was marked by quarrels between two captain-majors, Francisco de Albuquerque (a fidalgo from a proud family) and Afonso de Albuquerque (a bastard of the same family, without noble title, a “creature” of Dom Manuel). 
    
- Importance of East Africa to the Lisbon-Malabar trade: need for gold to make headway in Indian trading world. 
    

The “first pattern” of Portuguese activity: active confrontation with Muslim powers and creation of a centralized string of fortresses, epitomized by Afonso de Albuquerque’s conquests of Goa, Melaka, Kilwa, and Hormuz between 1510-15. Albuquerque came under intense criticism from, especially, the middle nobility, which resented his suppression and cooptation of private trade and preferred a strategy of free corsairing to hierarchical campaigning. 

Second pattern: Extension of fixed carreiras from the Portuguese forts to other port cities, and development of diplomatic ties with these, with private traders and renegades playing a larger role. More typical of the East Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal. Example of 1521 Brito-Pereira embassy to Bengal, which encountered a rival Portuguese embassy of renegade converts to Islam! 

- “By the early 1520s, the official settlements in the western Indian Ocean (which numbered perhaps a dozen in all) had been organized more or less in the fashion of the North African praças. They had a garrison, a certain number of officials – a captain, a factor, a scrivener and so on – but also a group of private merchants, now termed casados. These casados, whose status was analogous to that of the fronteiros in North Africa, and juridically related in turn to the category of homens-bons in Portuguese urban society, represented the acceptable face of private mercantile activity.” 76
    

The patterns which had stabilized by the 1520s began to be broken up by 3 major external shifts: 1. The rise of Brazil as a competitor for Portuguese resources; 2. Suleiman’s first Red Sea fleet and its attack on Diu in 1535; 3. The collapse of the Bengali sultanate and rise of two “aggressively mercantilist” states on the Burma coast, Arakan and Taungu, with hundreds of Portuguese settling as petty traders and mercenaries. 84 

Chapter 3: The mid 16th century Crisis. 

- By the late 1530s, “the Portuguese Asian enterprise appears to have exhausted the energy provided by the dialectical interaction between what we have called, as a shorthand, the Albuquerque and Albergaria tendencies.”
    
- Shift in the reign of Joao III (1521-1557) from an early “liberal” “Renaissance humanism” to a later “Conservative” “counter-Reformation” marked by the introduction of the Inquisition and the Jesuits.  
    
- Godinho noted multiple economic crises in the 16th century, but the most serious was a “structural break” c. 1545: “He terms it a “structural break” (viragem estrutural), which is marked by three features: first, the recovery of the Levant route and the pepper and spice trade of Venice; second, the beginnings of stagnation in the Portuguese Asian economy; and third, the period when monarchic capitalism (and its allies – the middle bourgeoisie) give way to a new set-up, dominated by powerful transnational banker-merchants, allied to the Portuguese nobility.” 92; Godinho 1968
    
- Reign of Martim Afonso de Sousa (1542-5) as Viceroy of Goa was marked by a heavy focus on eastwards rather than westwards trade, particularly with the Malay ports and China. During de Sousas tenure, the old hostility to foreign Muslims and cooperations with local ones was reversed: the Portuguese now warred openly with the “Mappilas” of Malabar while allowing privileged trade with the Red Sea to certain prominent Persians. 98-100
    
- The sudden expansion in the China-Japan trade after 1555 provided a new breath of life to the established ports, as bullion flowed to the Goa mint and customs duties from the Far Eastern trade kept Goa and Melaka afloat, as well as by “diverting the attention of private entrepreneurs to a new field in which they did not compete with the Crown.” 112
    

Chapter V: “Thus, rather than being a phase of generalized decline, the argument of this chapter is that the years from 1570 to 1610 form a phase of reorientation in the history of Portuguese Asia, a period when two tendencies vie for dominance. The residual momentum of the system (or perhaps its inertial tendency) still lay in the direction of controlling maritime trade, in one fashion or the other. But increasingly, a new tendency emerges: in places as far distant from one another as mainland Southeast Asia, Sri Lanka, and East Africa, the Portuguese enterprise shows a far greater predilection for territorial adventurism than in any earlier period.” 116

- Basic contrast between the Spanish model, which emphasized control over territory and labor in the encomiendas, and in which the cargoes to Seville were entirely private (though taxed and regulated by the Crown), with the Portuguese model which emphasized the king’s own commercial interests in foreign voyages. Argues that by the 1570s, under influence from a nobility inclined towards the Spaniards, the Portuguese royals’ self-image had shifted more in the direction of the Hapsburg conception. 120
    
- Example from 1590s of a business alliance of Novos Cristianos across the Iberian world: “Anto ́nio Dias de Casseres at Macau with Diogo Fernandes Vito ́ ria at Manila, Henrique Dias at Lisbon, and Diogo Dias Neto and Bernardo de Luna at Mexico.” 
    
- Collapse of the New Christian networks in 1630s+40s: “Persecution was only one reason for the decline; the decline of the trans-Pacific trade, the attacks by the Dutch on Portuguese- owned shipping, and the tendency for prosperous New Christians to buy into titles and move out of business also are likely to have played a part in the change that occurred.” 130
    

“The Bishop of Melaka from 1581 to 1601, Dom João Ribeiro Gaio, had a number of schemes that speak of substantial territorial conquest, which he put up for the Crown’s con- sideration. Many of his schemes, which were conceived in the Habsburg period, required Spanish-Portuguese cooperation, with Manila to provide one of the bases from which operations would be conducted, together with Melaka. One of these concerned the possibility of conquering the Thai kingdom of Ayuthia, as well as its ports such as Patani; the Bishop argued that Thailand would provide agricultural supplies and cattle, besides being a rich center of trade that could be taxed, and among the products he mentions are indigo, benzoin, sappan-wood, and timber for ship-building. This notion, far from being regarded as an eccentric pipe-dream, was seconded by Luis Pe ́rez Dasmarin ̃as, interim Governor of the Philippines in 1593–6. Besides the fact that the conquest of Ayuthia, Patani, and territories around would make Philip II the “richest and most powerful ruler in the world,” legal-ethical considerations were brought into play, in a manner parallel to what had occurred in respect of East Africa in 1569 (Boxer 1969b). The anti-Christian conduct of the Ayuthia ruler Phra Nareth – evocatively described as the “Black King” – was dilated on at some length by Dasmarin ̃ as; it was also argued that the Thais themselves wished, almost to a man, to be rid of their ruler, and enjoy peace and security, “something which everyone desires, and which now they lack completely.”” 133

- 16th century Sri Lanka was split into three competing states, and Portugal emerged by the 1520s-40s as the partner and then protector of the southernmost of these, Kotte, which was the major cinnamon producer. From the 40s onwards the Portuguese “mercilessly exploited” Kotte even as its territories were conquered by the rival state of Sitavaka, until in the late 1570s the dying king of Kotte willed his kingdom to Dom Sebastiao. The Portuguese thereafter conquered their way north, vassalized several thousand villages, and installed a mediator class of peninsular settler-landlords (which SS relates to the encomenderos of Spanish America). In the 1620s and 30s, after Sri Lanka had been fully conquered, village revenues exceeded customs. 140
    
- Portuguese control over the clove trade began to be challenged around 1570, when a rival network linked to Aceh began to expand production south of the original centers to Ambon and Halmahera, and the loss of Ternate following the Portuguese assassination of Sultan Hairun in 1570 (the “Aerio” of Argensola).
    
- By the 1580s Portuguese Asia had mostly transitioned from royal carreiras to a system of “concessions voyages” viagens de lugares, where private merchants won the rights to manage particular voyages alongside appointment as “Captain-Major.” “The Macau–Japan concession represented one pattern, that of the post of Captain-Major. In fact, until 1623, Macau had no permanent resident Captain, so that the Captain-Major of the concession-voyage also served as the town’s Captain-Major during the trading season;” but concessions were also sold for older and more established carreiras. 147
    
- Since the confessions conferred a monopoly on given routes, private traders began to avoid Portuguese ports in order to escape these monopolies, to the benefit of rivals like Aceh. 
    
- The most lucrative and expensive concession by far was the Macau-Nagasaki line, which brought in an estimated average profit of 35,000 cruzados.

- Overall assessment of “decline” from 1570-1610: there was a decrease in royal involvement in Asian trade and in the total number of ships returning annually to Lisbon, but private trade and overall opportunities for profit clearly increased, and overall tonnage (rather than ships) arriving at Lisbon remained stable. 
    
- Disagrees with Lane, Godinho, Braudel, and Steensgaard on a shift back to overland spice trade after 1570, arguing that the Portuguese were still meeting roughly the entire European demand for pepper; but with the caveat that trade was increasingly managed by “international merchant-capitalists,” in the relevant period mostly New Christians, while the Crown focused more on territorial conquests. 
    

Chapter Six: Retreat (1610-65)

- Emphasizes that the Dutch were not the only competitor to the Portuguese, and that a number of indigenous third parties mediated the Luso-Dutch conflict, often with decisive impacts. The Dutch also were not the cause of a number of major Portuguese losses, such as the end of the Japan trade. 
    
- The major geopolitical shift of the late 16th century was the rise of the Mughals, and particularly their conquest of Gujarat (1572), Bengal (1576), and Sind (1592), which gave them an important maritime presence across a wide variety of routes. 
    
- In 1612 the resurgent Taungu empire (reconsolidated by the upland Ava) conquered the private Portuguese fort of Syriam in the Irrawaddy delta: “Anauk-hpet-lun, allegedly aided by “cap-wearing Moors” (possibly from Masulipatnam), attacked Syriam by land and water in 1612; the fort surrendered, and Brito was impaled on an iron stake, while many of his companions were carried off as prisoners to Ava and tied to the Crown on a hereditary basis as military specialists.” 162
    
- Hormuz was lost to the combined forces of Shah Abbas and the English EIC in 1622; SS thinks that the importance of this loss has been exaggerated (by e.g. Steensgaard), as the Portuguese still had access to the Red Sea trade via Muscat, but it did allow for the rise of Safavid trade via Bandar Abbas. 
    
- The Portuguese began exploring the creation of a Company on the Dutch model as early as 1620, when Duarte Gomes Solis proposed the creation of a company to trade in silk and porcelain, which was of course opposed by the Casados of Macau. Olivares explored the idea of a “Hanseatic-Hapsburg Company” to trade between the Baltic and Iberia, which might be further expanded to Africa and Asia; the idea was for the municipal chambers and bourgeoisie to put up capital that would serve military purposes, but when a Portuguese Company was actually founded it found few backers and had to be funded 80% by the Crown. SS sees this failure as largely due to the suspicion of the New Christians that any investments would be lost if they were persecuted for heresy, and in any case the Company was shuttered after just 5 years in 1633. 
- The 1630s were the worst period of decline for the Portuguese in Asia; although the decade began with the defeat of an Acehnese siege of Melaka, the Portuguese lost Hughli to the Mughals in 1632; large parts of Sri Lanka across the decade (to Kandy, allied with the VOC); and the Japan trade in 1639, while the Dutch took to blockading Goa itself intermittently from 1635 on. All this contributed to the Restoration in 1640, one goal of which was to staunch the bleeding in the colonial wars, and the new Portuguese king ratified a treaty with the Dutch in 1642 — just after the Dutch had finally seized Melaka, in 1641.     
- Points out that a crucial factor in the Dutch conquest of Melaka was the support of the Sultan of Johor, ‘Abdul Jalil, who had assisted the Portuguese against Aceh in 1629; after 1641 the VOC supported an advance of Johor’s trade as against Aceh. 184
- Expectations that peace would bring “free trade” were dashed by the Dutch imposition of harsh cartazes until the last quarter of the century, and in any case the most profitable portion of the Portuguese network had been shattered. The Portuguese exit from the Manila trade also impoverished that city, though other factors certainly played a part there… 185
- A final blow was Oman’s conquest of Muscat in 1650.
- “The years between 1641 and about 1680 represent the height of Dutch maritime influence in Asian waters. This is a fact not generally recognized in the histo- riography, in which the fall of Banten to the Dutch in 1682 is given excessive importance.” 186 
- Most important part of the Dutch position was its dominance over the export of Japanese bullion, which gave it an enormous advantage in the intra-Asian trade (cites Glamann for figures)
- The end of the 10 year’s truce with Holland meant the steady Dutch conquest of Sri Lanka and various factories in Malabar and Coromandel. A final loss in India was the transfer to England of Bombai island as a dowry in 1665, over the strenuous objections of the Viceroy of Goa.
- While VOC victories harmed the Portuguese, it is less clear that they always benefitted the Company, which seemed at this time focused on extending its sovereignty beyond what a rational cost-benefit calculation would suggest… 

- Conclusion: “The retreat of the Estado da India in the years from 1610 to 1665 was not merely a question of a rivalry with the “enemy from Europe,” as the Dutch were then termed by the Portuguese. As our analysis has shown, the Dutch were a catalyst in Portuguese retreat in Asia, and also moved in the closing stages (the 1650s and 1660s) to pick up the spoils. But much of the damage was done by “the natives themselves” (as Ribeiro put it): Syriam, in part Hurmuz, Hughli, Nagasaki, Masqat, the Kanara ports, and Sa ̃o Tome ́ are the clearest examples, but the taking of the coastal forts in Sri Lanka, Melaka or even Cochin could not have been accomplished by the Dutch save in a propitious local political context. It was the change in this context, as much as rivalry with the English and other Europeans, which caused the Dutch in tum to lose their dominant place in Asian trade in the eighteenth century.” 189

Chapter Seven: Staying on, 1665-1700

- With the “disarticulation” of the trans-Pacific network, and continued growth in Brazil, the carreira da India begin to call at Bahia, where Indian textiles would be traded for sugar; this link became of vital importance after the rise in gold mining at Minas Gerais after 1692. The trade with Bahia (which came to include the slave trade from Mozambique) began to even eclipse the direct Lisbon-Goa route in the early 18th century. The tobacco monopoly of Goa, dependent on Brazilian imports, began to become a major source of revenue. 195

- In the 1690s two Companies on the Dutch/English model were founded, one with primarily Goan shareholders, which was noted as trading in Surat, but which was folded into the other, Lisbon-based company due to complaints that it neglected the security of Portuguese possessions following the loss of Mombasa. The reorganized “joint” Company itself lapsed after its first 12-year term. 

- Macau’s trade increasingly shifted to the Sundas and Timor, the sandalwood trade of which brought in 150-200% profits, but this trade was disrupted by the Dutch, and Timor developed a hybrid community of “Larantuquieros” (mixing Dutch, Portuguese, and Timorese). The community at Larantuka in turn was relatively independent of any oversight from Goa. 220-221

- “Thus, searching constantly for outlets which the Dutch might permit them, the Macau-based traders of the last third of the seventeenth century are a pale shadow of the prosperous mercantile community of the early years of the same century. Yet survival alone ensured that their day would come once again. As the Dutch began in the early eigh- teenth century to exercise a less and less dominant presence in intra-Asian trade, the residents of Macau were among those who benefited. In the 1720s and 1730s, their trade shifted to the ports of the Malabar and Coromandel coasts, and at the same time their link with the ports of Vietnam became a major strength (Moura ̃ o 2005). Later, after the 1760s, when the opium trade between Bengal and China became an important growth area, the traders of Macau were once again there to take advantage of the possibilities.” 222
- Argues against the victory of the Companies over the Estado as representing the superiority of a particular mode of “rational” organization, pointing out that the VOC relied at least as much as the Portuguese had on brute force, establishment of monopolies, and politically-negotiated privileges (their monopoly on Nagasaki trade). 

- “In 1700, the Portuguese in Asia survived in two forms: as private traders staving off a stifling embrace from Goa (as in Macau and Porto Novo), or as sub-imperialists, carving out territorial dominions (as in southeast Africa or Timor).” Argues that this was not particularly different from the English dichotomy between the Company’s steady advancement as a quasi-state power and the “country traders” making their fortunes in partial defiance of it. 225

Chapter 8: The Official Realm

- Since the Portuguese state in Asia was not largely based on mass Iberian settlement, instead being concentrated in small urban nuclei, its institutions were largely borrowed from the Portuguese municipalities, unlike in Brazil. However, the municipal chambers evolved differently in the different centers, and by 1700 played very different roles in Macau vs. Goa. 
- The Portuguese Casados included both “white” and “black” (native Christian) members, though even the “whites” were largely married to local women. Estimate of around 5000 men of the former category and 7500 of the latter in 1635. 233
- After the introduction of the concession system in the 1570s, there was a systematic rivalry between the official captains of Goa, Melaka, Macau etc, and the Casados over control of maritime commerce. The older military towns (Goa, Hormuz, Melaka), with strong strata of and large garrisons, tended to be more amenable to the captains, while more purely commercial centers like Macau and Hughli were effectively run by the Casados. 
- In the case of Goa, the Casados tended to be enlisted into fidalgo factions which often clashed with one another and with incoming viceroys, on certain occasions even mutinying and forcing an appointed official back to Lisbon. 249-50
- Early Portuguese ventures in Asia included conspicuous numbers of Florentines and South Germans (especially Augsburgers), drawing on these communities’ presence in Portugal. Mention of one Florentine (Corbinelli) who first set up shop in Lisbon to participate in the Atlantic sugar trade, then ended up as the first official factor in Goa. One Ferdinand Cron was in Goa for almost 4 decades as an agent of the Walsers and Fuggers, and as a result also worked for numerous important Iberians, including Francisco Da Gama and Dona Margarida of Spain. 
- Many of these foreigners, including Cron and the de Coutres brothers, were expelled after 1620. 
- The prominent solteiro trader Francisco Vieira de Figueiredo, though he had been a Casado of Nagapattinam made his fortune in trade with Melaka and Manila, and served as ambassador of Manila to Cambodia in the early 1640s, then moved to Makassar, where, with the title of envoy for the sultan, he was able to trade even with the Dutch territories. 
	- “In 1647 and again in 1648, he was accredited envoy-at-large of the Sultan of Makassar, but in the latter year, when he put in an appearance at Banten and Batavia, he was also envoy from the viceroy at Goa. Some years later, in 1652, he returned to Makassar from Coromandel, claiming to be the envoy of Mir Muhammad Sa‘id Ardistani, Mir Jumla and minister of the Sultanate of Golkonda.” 
- After the Dutch began attacking his shipping following 1652, Vieira tried to foment troubles for them from Makassar, ending ultimately in a Dutch siege of the town followed by the expulsion of all Portuguese. 
- “From the viewpoint of Portuguese society therefore, the Asian empire was a stabilizing factor, permitting a certain limited upward mobility to a few, but for the most part shoring up the economic and social power of the fidalguia, while at the same time providing a safety valve, to limit conflicts that might other- wise have taken place within that class. To Marxist-influenced writers, this has suggested that expansion into Asia stunted Portuguese development, by rein- forcing seigneurial institutions rather than the power of the urban bourgeoisie.” However, SS points out that the same trends did not prevent England from developing, so the problem of relative underdevelopment must be more complex than foreign remittances shoring up seignorialism. 259-260
- Once source claims that there were 5000 Portuguese mercenaries employed in Southeast Asia in the 1620s, a roughly equivalent number to all the “Casados brancos.

Conclusion

- JC van Leur argued that the Portuguese had a basically medieval mentality, and that their “non-economic” motives and organization meant that their encounter with Asia was not particularly transformative; this idea was critiqued by Meilink-Roelofsz but carried on by Steensgaard, who saw the Portuguese as an old-school “redistributive enterprise” as opposed to the “productive enterprise” of the VOC.
- “Besides, a serious problem appears to be that the Dutch in their trade of the period were never too keen on the notion of improving efficiency through competition, but preferred instead to use legislation, state power, and force to promote trade. This is one of the major reasons why the timing of expansion and contraction in the Dutch international trading economy coincides so closely with political shifts (Schama 1987: 339–43; Israel 1982, 1989). Further, where once historians had fetishized the “chartered trading Company” and its rationality, it is now increasingly clear that behind such institutions in the Netherlands lay persistent networks of family and patronage, quite comparable both to those of the English East India Company and the Iberians." 288
