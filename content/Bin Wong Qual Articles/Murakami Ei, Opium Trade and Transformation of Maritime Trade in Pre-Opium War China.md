
- Starting point: the major questions of Qing history are not about comparisons to Europe, but comparisons with East Asia, particularly between periods and regions in China: "not why an industrial revolution did not take place... but rather why Chinese institutions, which had worked well in the 18th century, failed in the 19th and were, therefore, ineffectual in preventing the crisis." A focus on the role of the opium trade (as symbol for maritime trade in general) in contributing to the breakdown of the institutional order.
- Three primary research goals: 1. Circulation of opium around coastal China beyond Guangzhou, with the reasons for the rise in use; 2. Transformation of the maritime trade system in China before the rise of the opium trade, and reasons for that transformation; 3. Role playing by Chinese officials in the changes of the maritime system, including the institutional background for the "corruption" often attributed to maritime officials.
- The maritime trade controls put in place in 1684 worked well for all parties through the 18th century, with the brokers (yahang) in Canton and Amoy presiding over a steady increase in the volume of both foreign and domestic trade while generally ensuring that state regulations were abided by. At a certain point, however, volume had increased to the point that institutional changes were needed. 
- First sign of change was the collapse of trade in Amoy, as first the yanghang and then the shanghang brokers went bankrupt due to their tax collection duties outstripping the available maritime revenue; Amoy's trade at this time was partially siphoned to the less regulated smaller outlying ports nearby. A number of prominent Fujianese merchants moved to Guangzhou (later Shanghai) to deal in the booming trade with Westerners. The increase in maritime customs out of Guangzhou meant that the collapse in Amoy's foreign trade was "fiscally inconsequential for the Qing government." It was the growth of the coastal trade, of which opium was the most important part, which destroyed the government's ability to manage maritime trade via Canton. 43
- The early 19th century opium trade was mostly carried out via *yaokou*, small groups pooling capital from a number of investors; Fujianese and Cantonese were involved in transacting the coastal trade, often as partners, but the investing groups behind the yaokou were often tied together by personal and/or lineage connections; at least in Fujian lineages were often the largest-scale investors in the trade due to their ability to gather capital. 
- Fujianese and Cantonese traders spread the opium trade to the north via the pre-existing domestic trade in beancakes, oil, etc., hence Tianjin and Manchuria were the major initial destinations; institutions developed for the earlier trade, such as the Fujian huiguan, played an important role in brokering for and sheltering opium traders. Yanghuopu 洋货铺 transitioned early on to providing opium, and managers of these shops were often among those arrested for opium offenses from the 1830s on. 
- The basic problem facing Qing anti-opium measures was that the old brokerage system, which focused on managing entry and re-departure, was inadequate to deal with widespread coastal trading, where gaps between entry and formal departure became longer and much illegal activity was undertaken precisely during those gaps. Attempts to force brokers to control opium only pushed coastal traders to circumvent the brokers entirely. The only mechanism the Qing government had for the control of trade, brokerage, was inadequate to manage trade in an illegal substance, since traders in opium would simply avoid registration with brokers entirely. 
- Conclusion: "The reason for the failure of the Qing government's ban on opium was not administrative corruption, but rather the contradictions that developed in its trade control institutions." 51
