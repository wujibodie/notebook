
Qing China has traditionally been seen as a landlocked empire focused entirely on its agrarian heartland to the exclusion of any maritime ventures. Besides concern with the prestige play of the “tributary system,” authors from the Fairbank school have tended to believe, Qing rulers gave little thought to crafting policy about their maritime borders. This understanding has been substantially revised over the past decades, and it is now possible to speak with some certainty about a dynamic and flexible Qing orientation towards the sea – one in which maritime trade never overshadowed agriculture as a source of state revenues, but rather presented a problem of local control to which the Qing was surprisingly attentive and creative, crafting policies in response to specific conditions throughout the course of the dynasty. The question now is how to periodize and categorize these shifts in policy. I will argue below that, rather than seeing a concrete “reorientation towards the sea” at any given point, core imperial attitudes towards the maritime frontier were fairly consistent throughout the Qing, and that the major turning points of policy were adjustments based on the resolution or appearance of particular problems.

The Ming Dynasty did witness extraordinary contrasts in maritime policy, between the attempts at outright closure of maritime trade in the Hongwu or Jiajing reigns, to the spectacular Zheng He voyages of Yongle, and the flourishing private sea trade of the Wanli reign. The extreme ups-and-downs of that dynasty reflected the fundamental incompatibility between the anti-commercial vision of its founding emperor and the actually-existing habits and demands of the maritime East Asian world. By comparison, the most substantial changes in Qing maritime policy largely reflected temporary responses to immediate conditions rather than radical revisions to underlying policy preferences, which remained quite stable throughout the dynasty. Neither as committed to suppression of merchant power as Zhu Yuanzhang, nor as willing to directly sponsor foreign enterprises as the Yuan or Southern Song rulers, the Qing consistently attempted to strike a balance between controlled entry of foreigners into the empire and reasonable allowance for private pursuit of profits from foreign trade. This balance was achieved through a combination of government-mandated brokers, division of labor between officially-registered port cities, and broad acceptance of Chinese voyages abroad. The cardinal point distinguishing Qing maritime policy from that of its predecessors and contemporaries was not a hostility to maritime trade, but a consistent preference that foreign trade be handled in Chinese ships and if possible outside imperial borders.

Iwai Seiichi has argued that the roots of Qing maritime policy should be sought in the career of the prime dynastic ancestor, Nurhaci. Nurhaci came of age as a servant of the Ming general Li Chengliang, operating in the newly-established frontier markets along the Liaodong border which that general helped establish. The unified Manchu culture which Nurhaci began to establish was thus a product of the hybrid culture of the frontier trading settlements, and the earliest Aisin Gioro patriarchs had a keen appreciation for the value of foreign trade, including from their ports on the Liaoning peninsula. As a combined trader-raider, Nurhaci was in much the same mold as a figure like Zheng Chenggong, and his successor Hong Taiji branched into maritime plundering with a series of raids across the Bohai to Shandong. When the Manchus entered Beijing, therefore, they were already used to frontier commerce and saw it as a useful tool for the development of easily administered nodes of settlement. This set of points helps clarify Ronald Po’s contention that the Qing viewed the coasts largely as a “frontier,” analogous to its frontiers in the north, west, and southwest. With the model of “frontier markets” foremost in their mind, the early Qing emperors evinced a greater understanding of the administrative, rather than fiscal, potential of commercial centers.

The emergence of Zheng Chenggong as perhaps the most dangerous Ming loyalist after 1644 also confirmed for the Qing that market networks outside their control were dangerous centers of potential resistance. Their draconian policy of coastal resettlement is often cited as the single most extreme case of maritime restriction by any Chinese dynasty. However, it would be a mistake to view this policy as in any way typifying “landlocked” hostility towards coastal life as such. It should be remembered that it was during this period of sea “closure” that the Qing navy was engaged in one of its most logistically difficult feats: the war against the Zheng regime and eventual conquest of Taiwan. In the course of this long war, the Qing demonstrated the capacity for impressive coordination of large-scale naval operations, the integration of “local” forces from the maritime south (the Qing admiral, Shi Lang, was a Fujianese turncoat from the Zhengs), and even of cooperation with European naval powers, as the VOC provided logistical support for the operation.  While the “burning coast” policy was undoubtedly destructive for civilian life in affected areas, it can hardly be said that the Qing court at this time was inattentive or incapable with regard to maritime affairs. On the contrary, even as it was engaged in intense battles with the Zhengs at sea, the Qing court opened its “safe” ports to foreign trade and actively solicited cooperation from the Chinese merchant diasporas in cities like Nagasaki and Manila. Guangzhou, just beyond the boundary of the resettlement zone and under the control of the nominally loyal Shang warlord family, was at this time chosen as the center for official trade with the South China Sea, a position it retained even after the defeat of the Three Feudatories and the Zhengs. 

While Guangzhou was famously appointed the sole port of entry for southern missions to the Qing empire, the image of a single aperture on an otherwise sealed maritime border is not entirely accurate. While foreign ships were forbidden from docking in most of the empire, domestic coastal trade boomed, and with it came an expansion in tacitly accepted foreign ventures. The center for such trade remained, as it had long been, centered in south Fujian, with the island of Xiamen now developing into the region’s single largest entrepot for commerce with Southeast Asia. Xiamen, which had risen to prominence under Zheng Chenggong, was garrisoned by a strong naval force tasked with suppressing piracy and collecting customs. As sketched by Ng Chin-keong, Xiamen developed into an impressive city largely on the strength of its official monopoly on trade with Taiwan, at the time developing into a major source of rice and sugar for the mainland. The presence of a customs office on Xiamen both sanctioned that city’s maritime activity and contributed to the dispersion of that activity across the southeast coast, as numerous port towns and fishing villages began to specialize in smuggling of foreign goods to avoid customs. This situation in turn set the stage for the Chaozhou-Zhangzhou-Quanzhou region to become a center of coastal smuggling upon the entry into Chinese economic life of the opium clippers, with the result that Xiamen (as shown in detail by Murakami Ei) actually declined in the 19th century despite the increase in foreign trade generally and its being designated a treaty port. 

The introduction of treaty ports and destruction of the “Canton System” following the Opium Wars has often been taken as the decisive turning point after which China’s gaze was forcibly turned towards the sea. While the immediate fiscal and political consequences of the wars on the Qing state were severe, the establishment of treaty ports in itself contributed to, but did not entirely cause, the collapse of the old Qing system of regulated markets. As in Fujian, Guangzhou had begun to suffer economic troubles as a consequence of the opium trade even before 1840, with a number of the officially-licensed brokers of the “13 hongs” being bankrupted by an increase in illegal competition. The appearance of foreign concessions in major cities and the visible circulation of foreign manufactured goods, especially after the 1860s, sparked intense reactions and calls for reform, including the first major imperial investments in a modern navy and merchant marine, as well as the shift to ocean shipping of tribute grain to Beijing. While the reforms of the late 19th century are rightly famous, it is worth keeping in mind that versions of such reforms were already being called for by men like Wei Yuan before the first Opium War, in response to the growing foreign presence within and around the old “Canton System.” It is also important to consider that the influx of new imports into the treaty ports was probably at least matched in scale by the expansion in Chinese maritime trade, if remittances from Chinese laborers abroad are included. While the scale and reach of foreign trade expanded vertiginously in the 19th century, it did so by growing within a matrix of limited foreign market cities and bustling semi-legal, small-scale coastal trade which had its similarities to the Great Wall trading towns which had nurtured the fundamental outlook of the Qing emperors.
