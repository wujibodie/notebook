Introduction
- Suggests that Japan and the "eastern" Mongols after Altan Khan were exceptional in viewing tributary missions through a mainly commercial lens; smaller neighbors were more appreciative of official imperial protection.
	- The phrase haijin first appeared in official writing in the 1587 大明会典; it was first widely used in the Jiajing era with reference to anti-smuggling policies.
- 互市 first took on the meaning of regulated commerce (usually on international frontiers) in the Song Dynasty (e.g. the horse-tea trade with the Xi Xia). Ma Duanlin thought the system originated in Han Dynasty trade with Nanyue and the Xiongnu. 
- Broadly accepts and adopts Fairbank's claim that before 1842, all commerce was conceived within the "tributary system" with its attendant assumptions of sinocentric world order etc. 
	- But disagrees with the idea that Chinese viewed the tributary system as basically "approving" of mercantile activity 53
	- Focuses on steady trend from early Ming to open up the initially 一元的 tributary system, with its focus on ritual submission, to create spaces of depoliticized trade - 互市. Russian tea trade as one early Qing example. 
- Follows Banno Masataka 坂野正高 in viewing the tributary system as an "operational concept" (操作性概念) with serious problems, but one that needs to be used as a starting point. 
- Picks up from 5 questions of Hamashita Takeshi's:
	- Why was Asia's international system constituted via the tributary system, and why did it persist?
	- How to explain the 15th-16th century explosion in the private junk trade?
	- Need to consider the China-centered tributary order as giving "Asia" its particular character as a region
	- Transformation of Japan after the mid-Ming from a tributary state to a 互市 one
	- Understanding the different institutions regulating "tributary" and "trading" relations

Chapter One - Ming Dynasty Expansion of Tributary Policy and Institutional Hegemony
- Attempts to understand ideological underpinnings of Ming 朝贡. General conclusion is that the performance of hegemony masked relations that were considerably more favorable to the lesser partners than supposed. There was also room for rank competition among China's "subjects," as in Japan's assertions of ritual primacy over Korea. 
- Case of Altan Khan shows that Ming internal presentations of foreign relations could differ from the actual (more equal) events on the ground, as understood by the Mongols.

Chapter Two - Commercial Monopoly and the Ming Sea Ban
- Looks at the development of Ming maritime restrictions and their steady unification with the tributary policy in a cohesive system of maritime defense and trade regulation.
- In Hongwu 14-16, policies strengthening restrictions on foreign trade without official licenses were enacted; increasingly tributary missions became the only legal avenue for foreigners to trade with China, even as Chinese "smugglers" were increasingly suppressed.
- The Yuan at first carried over Song maritime regulations, but in 1284 all maritime customs stations were unified with the salt monopoly stations, and Fujian's customs were put under the salt bureaucracy of Jiang-Huai. Quanzhou's customs revenue was transferred to Yangzhou salt administrator Manggutai 忙古台, who appointed Pu Shougeng as his agent there. 118
	- At this time the Yuan banned private maritime trade by "powerful families," giving Pu Shougeng a monopoly on Quanzhou's overseas trade; however this policy lasted only two years. 
- The enormous revenues of the Yuan maritime customs produced a situation of rivalry between the central court and provincial officials. 126
- Argues against the idea that the Ming was ideologically anti-commerce from the start, and points to encouragement of maritime customs in Hongwu's first years. 141-2
	- Private circulation of foreign goods was banned in Hongwu 27, but there were still regulations for government use of such products. 
- In other words there was no necessary ideological or practical reason why the Ming tributary system had to ban private maritime trade; when it did so it was as a policy response to specific problems, paricularly the troubled relationship with Japan.
- Hongwu ordered tributary states to only send one mission per emperor, with the exception of Korea.
- Hongwu was offended on learning that the representatives of the 日本国王 to the court were actually sent by the shogun rather than the emperor, and interpreted this as a scheming official's attempt to seek private gain. 
	- The original reason for the worsening of Ming relations w/ Japan was not wokou raids, but Hongwu's suspicions about the chaotic internal politics of Japan as displayed in the confused and complicated tributary missions. A HW13 reiteration of the ban on trade with overseas countries was likely aimed at Japan
	- There were also real concerns about a Japanese invasion. Japan's success against the Mongols gave it a reputation as a military power.
- Sees Hongwu 14-17 as the period when the haijin was strengthened by banning *internal* circulation of foreign goods as an anti-smuggling measure. This strengthening of restrictions continued throuhg the HW20s. 
- The Hongwu era Libu noticed that stricter Haijin seemed to lower the number and splendor of tributary missions. Letters were sent to some vassals (Majapahit?) demanding explanations. 
	- Sign that the strict maritime ban was actually harmful to the tributary system and that its real target was the perceived threat from Japan.
- Summarizes Zheng He voyages as having first sought to expand tributary ritual to bolster Yongle's legitimacy, but thereafter to have become a profit-seeking enterprise.
- During a period of about 30 years in the Yongle and Xuande reigns, Japan ceased sending tribute. After Ming diplomatic missions to Japan were rebuffed, the Xuande court hit on the idea of ordering Ryukyu to buy Japanese goods for trade, with cash provided by the court. 
	- From here it was during the reign that the tributary system turned into a system of "mutual monopoly" with the court encouraging frequent contact while strictly forbidding private overseas commerce to maximize profits. 

Ch. 3 - Frontier Society and the Commercial Tide
- 皇明四夷考 - 郑晓, Jiajing 43 account of foreign relations
	- 过去是外夷进入中华，今日却是华人进入外夷。
	- Cites cases like Wu Dengying's usurpation in Annam and the contemporary 板升 and wokou phenomena
- Records of 板升 appearing over the wall in Liaodong, possibly under Jurchen auspices. 212
- Ultimately argues that Nurhaci's rise was based on his origins in the frontier markets opened by Wang Chonggui and Li Chengliang, and that the Qing conquest was thus conditioned on the 1570s and later frontier culture of Liaodong hushi towns. 
	- Nurhaci as same archetype as Wang Zhi and Zheng Chenggong

Chapter 4 - 16th Century China's Groping towards a Commercial System and the Hushi
- From around 1508-1522 Guangdong experimented (on local initiative) with allowing and taxing maritime trade before being shut down following Jiajing's decrees and naval victories over the Portuguese 269
	- With their trade in Guangdong banned, the Portuguese look for new smuggling centers in Shuangyu and Jinmen - Lin Renchuan connects this directly to the closure of Guangdong
- Following protests from Cantonese gentry, a government-sponsored "market" for foreign traders, with official 牙行 selling their goods on to the private market, was in place again by 1530. The Portuguese were banned, but were known to trade under the name of tributaries. 
- The first proposal to officially set up foreign markets outside the tributary system came from the Guangdong Xun'an Yushi 王绍元 in c. 1550.
- In 1552, a set of regulations for visitng merchants in Guangdong was drawn up (with the help of Minnan and Huizhou merchants), the 客纲 and 客纪; the Portuguese were included in these regulations, “regularizing" their presence, and they shortly moved to Macao
	- Pang Shangpeng wrote about the Portuguese presence on Macao （as well as about Chinese mediators who learned their language and customs)
- Wang Zhi's letter of surrender requested the opening of markets in Zhejiang on the Guangdong model --  Iwai thinks he was aware of the recently implemented kegang
- Discussion of 客纲 system 298-99
	- Conclusion: While the late Ming 客纲 was not identical to the Qing 13 hongs, there was probably a genealogical relationship between these.
