- Sees the Zigong salt works as "China's first privately owned high-capital, high-throughput industrial enterprises," the most successful of which were "horizontally and vertically integrated companies that... made their shareholders among the richest men in China."
- Two goals: "to better understand the institutions that structured China’s indigenous economic development and to try to resituate the history of Chinese business within the larger history of the firm in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries." xiv
- Follows Alfred Chandler's history of Standard Oil in attributing the rise of vertical integration to new technologies which "made possible high capacity or "throughput" production;" combination of a larger market and high throughput incentivize producers to maximize the use of their large capital investments by attacking both forward (marketing) and backward (raw materials) bottlenecks. 
	- Integration also led to necessary changes in management structure to become more specialized and hierarchical. 
Chapter One: Administration and Technology
- In 1877, Sichuan Governor Ding Baozhen established a partial salt monopoly under the *guanyun shangxiao* system; in response employees of a firm run by Wang Langyun demolished the guanyun office in Zigong. 
- Centralized salt certificates were abolished in Sichuan and Shaanxi following the devastation of the Ming-Qing transition. Only merchants trading for than 40 jin of salt at a time needed to purchase government tickets. It was first in 1729 that marketing boundaries were set and quotas were drawn up for sale of Sichuan salt. 
- As population grew over the course of the Qing, the old production quotas grew out of date; meanwhile many old wells became defunct, and their quotas were shifted to still-productive wells, especially Furong and Jianwei, the production of which increased steadily. 
- Deep-well brine tapping occurred only in the West, and became more lucrative as the technology of drilling improved after its invention in the Song. After 1830, miners around Gongjing began to reach "black brine" deposits some 3,000 feet deep, with higher salinity than "yellow brine" sources. 15-17
- Natural gas had been known to exist in Sichuan for centuries, but it was only the large volume of brine being pumped near some of the largest deposits which made economical its use as fuel before the gas itself was easily transportable. "Symbiotic" relationship between Gongjing black brine wells and Ziliujing gas furnaces. 20-23
	- Gas was piped directly into a furnace, which fired until exhausted; the salt pans were located inside the furnace. 
	- Coal-fired furnaces existed, but were expensive and produced tainted salt only fit for the poorest markets. 

Chapter Two: Structure of Investment 
- Shaanxi merchants were among the most important groups in the Sichuan salt trade, and built a huiguan at Ziliujing in 1752; Shaanxi firms were the dominant wholesalers in the Ziliujing markets. However, their profits were mostly repatriated, and they were not significant investors in salt production itself. 
- While early mines leased land using rental contracts identical to those of ordinary agricultural land, over time the mines or wells became separate from the land itself. From there it was only a short distance to "share rents," in which the right to a portion of mine profits was contractually based on input of capital. "By the turn of the nineteenth century it became possible for the possessors of land, capital, drilling equipment, technical expertise, and managerial talent to each partake of rights to a well in proportion to the value of their particular contributions." 31

Chapter 3: Fragmentation as Business Strategy
- "Along with the partnerships formed to invest in well digging, salt production partnerships served in part to overcome the problem of limited household wealth relative to the capital requirements of salt production. Reducing various elements within the production process to discrete firms also served to minimize risk in an extractive industry subject to political as well as economic and technical pressures, as demonstrated by the participation in such partnerships by even the most successful salt entrepreneurs." 50
- Before the company law of 1904, Furong salt firms signed "partnership agreements," documenting the names of partners and their capital contributions by the firm, and were protected by Qing courts. 
- In the early 20th century management groups known as daguan developed. Some of these existed to ensure consistent management of the wells and their immediate infrastructure despite a diversity of shareholder viewpoints; others managed whole portfolios of wells, mediating between partners and individual well managements. Daguan served as "impartial arbiter of competing and intersecting interests, *productivity* being the main determinant of its decisions." 53
- Drilling and distribution of brine and gas were the first steps in salt production, and represented the phase with the largest number of investors; entrepreneurs successful in this stage might then go on to develop furnaces, the ownership of which was more concentrated, and which further expanded into large integrated enterprises. 68-69
- Estimates the average profit per salt pan of ~330-650 taels per year before tax. 71
- Conclusions: the commoditization of well shares developed out of a situation where limited household capital formation facing a capital-intensive industry combined with a robust contract culture; points out that the long-term nature of these investments challenges "assumptions about Chinese preferences for quick returns through usury and pawnbroking." 

Four: Organization and Entrepreneurship
- Largest lineages involved in the Furong salt yards were the Lis (Siyou), Wangs (Sanwei), Hus (Yuanhai), and Yans (Guixin). 
- Hu Yuanhai was originally a Jiangxi cloth merchant who serially invested the profits from his early salt wells into new drilling on land he owned; when he died he had five brine wells as well as gas, land, and tens of thousands in silver. 77
- The Yans avoided partnerships and so drilled only on their own land in Gongjing, specializing in "black brine."
- Shaanxi merchants began heavily investing in Furong salt in the mid-19th century, which Z credits for "the first phase of the takeoff of the salt industry." 79
- Wang Langyun belonged to a minor family of farmer-drillers, and reorganized around deep mining for black brine with the help of tenancy agreements with Shaanxi investors in the late 1830s. One old well called Tianyi turned out to be among the most productive black brine wells of all, helping make the family's fortune. 80
- The Li family had a handful of old brine wells, which were expanded and developed through tenancy agreements with mostly Shaanxi merchants. They also became involved in smuggling their own salt through Chongqing, and via a marriage alliance, marketed for the Wang Sanwei trust as well before the Wangs began marketing alone.  
- The Wang Sanwei lineage met once a year to settle accounts and arrange for the reinvestment of profits. After a period of mismanagement and indebtedness, managerial control was given over to a central accounting office run by a specialized non-relative *zhanggui*, assisted by two accountants. This trio supervised a set of specialist foremen in charge of particular furnace tasks. Most furnaces also hired "masters," educated men who mediated between the managers, lineage heads, and outside gentry connections. 
	- Good managers could be paid 100 taels per month, on par with high officials; average workers earned about 1 tael per month. 
- At least half of all brine wells were independent and small-scale operations lacking the hierarchical management of the major lineage enterprises; these were incapable of managing their own marketing, hence why the four great trusts were able to monopolize long-distance wholesale, and thus control overall price levels. 
- Most smaller well owners also did not want to invest in the dozens of buffalo required for pumping, and so relied on "buffalo contractors," who formed a middling class in Furong of about 200 people; a small number of these worked their way up to very large herds and from there to investing in the wells themselves. 94-95
- Vertical integration: With the profitability advantages of their larger size, the main "salt barons" were able to steadily improve their efficiency by investing in stockpiling of raw materials, wholesaling, piping (of brine to gas furnaces), and credit, such that "by the end of the dynasty... they could virtually conduct the business of salt without ever making a purchase from an outsider." 96
- The Wang Sanwei lineage was probably the largest property owner in Sichuan and controlled land worth 3,000 *shi* in rents (after a major sale in the late 19th C.); numerous brine wells at Shanzi ba and over 10 gas wells including some of the largest in the region, producing altogether maybe 11% of the region's salt; the Guangshengtong salt trading company; and traded in cotton yarn at Chongqing using their silver revenues. 
- The brine pipes connecting drill wells to furnaces were operated as independent enterprises, though largely owned and funded by the major "salt baron" lineages. 
- Most historians have seen the "bureaucratic" nature of salt merchants' wealth and their "familism" as meaning that they provided "no foundation for structural change." 113
	- Z. ofc disagrees: the lineage structure was an important source of funds in a capital-poor environment and also provide a good organizational framework. 
- "It is one of history’s great coincidences that civil war visited both China and the United States at mid-century. However, it is no coincidence that war provided the shock that opened new markets on both continents and gave many fledging industrialists the profits on which their future business fortunes were based."
