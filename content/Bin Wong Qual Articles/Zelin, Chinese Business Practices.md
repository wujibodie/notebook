- In 1901 Liu Kunyi and Zhang Zhidong submitted a memorial in which they suggested that Westerners were dominating China because they had clear commercial law and large joint-stock corporations, which China lacked. Zelin sees this as reflective of a general scholar-official ignorance of the actual conditions of mercantile life in late imperial China. 
- Starting from the basic fact of increased mercantile activity in all sectors from the mid-Ming to late Qing, Zelin asks: "How did these small merchants, not to mention their larger counterparts discussed below, manage money and investment, structure their businesses, minimize risk, obtain information, and assure transactional compliance"?
	- First answer: lineage trusts were the main form of legally-protected pooled capital. Single lineages often had multiple trusts for different purposes; differently surnamed lineages could also invest through trusts in the same enterprise, as in the salt manufacturers of Zigong. Suggests that "self-strengtheners" like Zhang Jian may have based their efforts on lineage trusts. 
	- Joint ownership created questions of liability; liabilities were usually contractually apportioned to avoid dispute. A number of surviving Qing contracts specify which assets of the investors are and are not liable to forfeiture in case the firm defaults. A law of 1914 outlawed this practice and made it clear that all shareholders held full responsibility for a firm's debts. 778 
	- Principle-agent problem: Shanxi merchants may have been the first to create "a system of incentives that encouraged the separation of investment and management and the creation of diverse business portfolios by merchants whose base remained in their home countries." Separation between "investor shares" and "labor shares" belonging to professional staff (usually non-kin). In certain conditions the total number of labor shares might exceed that of investment shares. Separately, Quanzhou and Xiamen merchants had a system of "privileged shares (*yinggu*)" to reward effective managers. 
	- The first signs of large-scale region-based long-distance trade groups comes in the Ming dynasty, with the Shan-Shaan *bang* which based their fortunes on managing the frontier trade in tea and salt with the monopoly privileges granted under the Ming *kaizhong* system. By the Qing Dynasty, "the accumulation of capital among these traders also found its way into other ventures," with the early banking pioneers in the area usually coming out of trading and manufacturing backgrounds.
		- However, worth noticing that "regional bang" were often seen as such more by outsiders than insiders, and competition between merchants of a given place could be fierce, as in the case of Guangzhou-based merchants in Fujian tea or Huizhou merchants in Jingdezhen. 781
		- Still, the benefit conveyed by the huiguan networks did make a difference to traders' success: "The most important function played by huiguan in the marketplace was informational. That some places produced large numbers of long-distance merchants followed much the same logic as chain migration. An initial opportunity translated over time into a critical mass of experience, information, connections, and capital as relatives and fellow countrymen ventured into new markets and trades, hired those whose performance could be guaranteed by acquaintances at home and cultivated the trust that allowed the formation of partnerships, often in businesses that the suppliers of capital would never see." 782
