- Modern maritime customs agencies were first founded in China in 1854; by 1910 there were 49 customs offices employing 20,000 agents. 
- Particularities of the maritime customs system: 1. It was substantially operated by foreigners, especially British citizens; 2. Unlike the previous customs offices, its revenues were managed by the Zongli Yamen, not the Hubu, and so was separated from the standard institutions of taxation and monopolies; 3. the customs office was run as an "independent kingdom," with all of its local offices answering directly to the central customs office 总税务司; 4. Because the customs office imported Western methods of management, accounting, and record-keeping, it followed an entirely distinct set of systems from the rest of the Qing bureaucracy, with readily apparent gains in efficiency. 
	- The central customs office circulated a 总税务司通令 among branches to coordinate rates and policies, while each branch sent periodic reports to the center.
- After the Taiping Rebellion, the empowerment of provincial officials to establish their own taxes and financial agencies put them in direct conflict with the customs office for revenues; the *lijin* being the most obvious point of tension (59). Dai's main theme is the complicated relationship between the central government, provincial revenue offices, and the maritime customs.
- The Tianjin Treaty of 1858 provided for a single 5% fee for all foreign merchants upon entering a treaty port, with immunity to export fees and local transit taxes. This fee was known as the 子口税. From as early as 1860 this system came under attack from local officials who resented the loss of transit taxes, such as Xue Huan 薛焕, the 江苏巡抚。In the words of one Xian Wen: "子口增则厘金绌." 60
	- However, the debate over the Zikou tax itself, which lasted until the 1880s, directed attention away from the maritime customs offices, which were not a target of much official condemnation in the early stages of their development. 62
- The 1876 Treaty of Yantai extended the privilege of Zikou customs tickets and exemption from lijin to Chinese merchants carrying foreign goods, causing anxiety about a collapse of lijin revenues. 
- The Treaty of Tianjin made separate provisions for the taxation of opium vs. other goods. Opium was assessed a fixed duty of 30 tales per 100 *jin* at the port of entry. Western merchants had to sell the opium at the treaty port; once it left their hands it was considered a domestic product and was charged normal transit taxes. 
- In 1895-7, there were discussions (initially approved by the hubu) for the British of the customs office to take over the transit tax on Chinese-produced opium, and Inspector Hart even hoped to farm the general lijin and salt tax, but these plans were put on hold due to fierce provincial opposition. Eventually Hart did get approval to farm the lijin taxes of seven stations in the vicinity of Suzhou. However, even this plan was frustrated by concerted opposition from neighboring provinces, for example by their placement of competing lijin stations just over the border, disrupting the trade routes the existing stations depended on. 
- After the Boxer Rebellion, the Maritime Customs under Hart was assigned control over normal lijin customs at all treaty ports, partially at the recommendation of a Great Power investigation into ensuring China's sufficient fiscal stability to pay its indemnities. A total of 22 "regular" stations and 112 branch customs stations thus came under Hart's control (67). 
- Foreign control of customs immediately became a pole of opposition from disgruntled officials; Zhang Zhidong was one of the fiercest critics. Historian Ma Shi said that the foreign monopoly on customs turned the institution into an instrument for foreign debt rather than something of use to the empire. Eventually a compromise was reached whereby the maritime office only took control of stations within 50 li of a treaty port. 
	- In repeated negotiations about the limits of the maritime customs bureau, the central government was forced to acknowledge the rights of Hart over its provincial officials; the Customs office contributed to a split between the central and provincial governments by seizing some of the most important sources of local funding in a manner that forced the Qing court to appear to prioritize foreign profits over provincial needs.
- Conclusions: On one hand, the modern maritime customs apparatus introduced by the Western powers increased the revenues available to the central government; for example, the lijin offices around Guangzhou went from annual revenues of 160-170,000 taels, 90+% of which was retained in the province, to revenues of around 1 million taels, largely remitted to Beijing. However, much of these revenues were bookmarked to war indemnities, and they came at a cost of increased friction between the Qing court and its local officials.

- Especially after the 1900 war, the maritime customs offices became the channels through which indemnities flowed and the major representatives of Western credit in China; servicing their revenues became a major source of income for Western banks. As customs revenues increasingly went directly to foreign debt, the relationship between the Qing court and the maritime inspectors soured (though it is not clear what the real consequences of this were, besides some inspections and attempts to develop an independent customs service).
