- The Qing "Company Law" was passed in 1904; by 1908 there were 227 joint-stock companies registered in China. The majority were small firms, especially pawn shops and retail stores, which "probably... continued to operate much as they had before their registration." 3-4
	- Of actual industrial enterprises using Western machinery, about half of the invested capital was accounted for by a few huge firms, involved in textiles, mining, and  railroad construction. 
- In 1912 there were 363 factories employing machines in China, among which the cotton mills predominated.
- "A principal assumption of this study is the belief that industrialization in the broadest sense was the normative development to be expected if China's traditional society were to adapt to the demands of the modern world. Retardation of change in this crucial area was a major factor in the inadequacy of China's total response to the impact of the West." Explains this "retardation" by analysis of the "characteristic patterns according to which the Chinese merchant and entrepreneur undertook and operated modern-type industrial and commercial enterprises," which for F can be summed up under the system of guandu shangban. 8
- Clarifies that while only a dozen or so companies were formally operated by government officials, "the essential ingredients of the *kuan-tu shang-pan* pattern were to be found in virtually every industrial or commercial undertaking... before the end of the Ch'ing Dynasty." 9-10
	- Admits that this "system" was *ad hoc* and not always recognized as such. Sees it as a descendant of the government salt monopoly, which had used "analogous" terms like 官督商销. 
	- As a result of this bureaucratic, "fiscal" precedent, “the kuan-tu shang-pan institution — was deficient in the rationalized organization, functional specialization, and impersonal discipline associated with the development of modern industry in the West." 11
- The Dasheng Cotton Mill, operated by Zhang Qian, was founded under the auspices of Zhang Zhidong following the Treaty of Shimonoseki, as Zhang felt the need for local industries to counter the feared flood of foreign goods after this treaty. Zhang Qian was unable to raise the necessary capital from merchant investors, and so took the money for his machines from the Nanjing Commercial Affairs Bureau and Sheng Xuanhai. Basic lesson is that private enterprises relied on "the sanction and assistance of powerful provincial leaders." 15-16
- One problem with the guandu shangban system was that enterprises under official oversight were always vulnerable to exactions from other parts of the bureaucracy (*baoxiao*, local tariffs, etc.). 

Chapter 2: Economic Conditions in the Late Qing
- Ambassador to London and Paris Xue Fucheng diagnosed the failure of Chinese joint-stock companies as resulting from their inability to motivate investors, due to both institutional and socio-cultural factors. The "proper spirit had not yet been initiated" 风气不开. 32
	- F agrees that the "proper spirit" was present in Japan but lacking in China; suggests that the alternative (family, political, social) motivations binding entrepreneurs like Sheng Xuanhai prevented them from fully committing to Western-style enterprise. 
- Liang Qichao and others recognized that agricultural reform was necessary to spark industrialization, but considered the problem from a mostly extensive lens, i.e. more technology and land, and failed to consider the necessary fiscal and market reforms to incentivize the capitalization of agriculture. “Chinese agriculture could serve the cause of industrialization
only if, as in Japan, the government had the effective power to
mobilize the surpluses of agriculture by means of taxation.” 35
- "One writer was so optimistic about the future of Chinese goods, which he said would be as good as but cheaper than foreign manufactures, that he looked beyond recapturing the domestic market to ask, "We can't prevent our people from using Western manufactured goods; can they prevent their people from using Chinese goods?""
	- Likely read very differently to A.F. in the 50s than today... 
- A.F.'s summary of the major missed points in late Qing reformist thought: 1. a lack of understanding of how traditional social structure and values limited capital available for investment; 2. failure to appreciate agriculture's importance to industry, especially "of the necessity for forced saving in agriculture as a basis for the initial growth of industry;" 3. optimism about intervention from a Qing state which was in reality utterly incapable of executing their program. 39-40

Chapter 3: Sheng Xuanhuai
- Main sources: A biography written by his sons, with obvious oversights, and the papers of Li Hongzhang, Zhang Zhidong, and Sheng himself. 
- Sheng joined Li Hongzhang's camp as a secretary to the Huaijun during an expedition to Xian in 1870, and followed Li to Zhili later that year; there he became involved in charitable works which gained him some renown. After 1871 became Li's "deputy... for economic matters." 61
- In addition to serving as assistant manager of the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company, Sheng was instrumental in negotiating the establishment of the first telegraph lines with foreign companies. 63
	- After the Sino-French War of 1885, Sheng was made director-general of the CMSN Co. 
	- In 1893, Sheng gathered private capital to rebuild the government-sponsored Shanghai Cotton Cloth Mill into the Huasheng Cotton Mill, "the first successful modern cotton mill in China." 65
- In 1896, the Hanyang Ironworks, with attached iron and coal mines, founded by Zhang Zhidong was "turned over" to Sheng Xuanhuai and made into a *guandu shangban* enterprise; it was transitioned into a wholly commercial LLC in 1908. 67
- Sheng was the first official to memorialize on the importance of "chambers of commerce."
- When Sheng's father died in 1902, his resignation from office was unexpectedly accepted, and he was quickly stripped of control of the telegraph company as well. 

Chapter Four: China Merchants' Company
- The basic structure of the company was "remarkably stable" across shakeups in management. The head office in Shanghai, housing the clerks which corresponded with branches and conveyed orders from above, operated consistently from the 1870s on. 100-102
	- The head office had three sections: a shipping office supervising cargoes and operation; a fiscal office headed by a chief accountant; and a secretarial office that handled paperwork, translations, etc. 
	- Besides these "business managers" there were three "official managers" handling policy; sometimes these overlapped with the official managers. Although there was a board elected by shareholders, its responsibilities were poorly defined and it rarely had much day-to-day influence (except insofar as the managers were board members)
		- "The most ambiguous position was occupied by the official managers who stood between higher officialdom above and the company's shareholders and staff below," as they "participated both in the "official supervision" and the "merchant management" of the enterprise." 103
	- Of the company's 26 steamers operating in 1893, five each handled the routes Shanghai-Hankow and Shanghai-Tianjin, while four split business between Tianjin and Hong Kong/Canton; other smaller roots connected Shanghai to the treaty ports, while a single ship sailed exclusively in the south between HK, Shantou, Xiamen, and Taiwan, and a few reserve ships handled "miscellaneous service." 105
		- Despite the number of ships marked for Tianjin, by the 1890s tribute rice no longer accounted for a majority of the firm's revenues. 
	- The first manager of the CMSN Co. was Zhu Qi'ang, a merchant by trade who served as commissioner for sea transport of tribute rice.
