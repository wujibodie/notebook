Li Zhi 李贄, *Fenshu* 焚書 (Book to be Burned), originally published in 1590.

Li Zhi (1527-1602) was one of the most provocative and controversial intellectual figures of the late Ming, whose iconoclastic writings challenged orthodox Neo-Confucianism and earned him posthumous persecution as a "heretic." Born in Quanzhou, Fujian, Li brought a distinctly Minnan perspective to his philosophical reflections, informed by the region's maritime commercial culture and mercantile values. The *Fenshu*—so titled because Li anticipated it would be burned by orthodox scholars—collects his letters, essays, and philosophical meditations from the 1570s-1590s, providing an unconventional window into late Ming intellectual and social history.

The work is particularly valuable for understanding Minnan's distinctive worldview, shaped by centuries of maritime commerce and cultural exchange. In his famous letter "Reply to Magistrate Deng" (*Da Deng Mingfu*), Li Zhi discusses the death of He Xinyin (何心隱) and the political machinations of Zhang Juzheng, revealing the complex factional politics of the 1570s from the perspective of a disaffected provincial intellectual. His observations on the interconnectedness of oceans—drawing connections between Fujian's eastern coast and the western ocean beyond Yunnan—reflect the cosmopolitan maritime consciousness of Minnan's commercial elite. Li's discussion of reading and scholarship (*Dushu Le*) challenges the rote examination culture while celebrating authentic intellectual engagement. For historians of Minnan's fiscal and social transformation, Li's writings illuminate how the region's commercial prosperity generated distinctive cultural values that challenged agrarian orthodoxies, even as the region remained dependent on maritime trade to meet its fiscal obligations—a tension that shaped Li's own career trajectory from official service to radical philosophical dissent.
