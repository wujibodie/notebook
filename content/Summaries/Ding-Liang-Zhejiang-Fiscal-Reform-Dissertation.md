Ding Liang 丁亮. "Mingdai Zhejiang Caizheng Zhidu Biange Yanjiu—Yi Lijia, Junyao, Yichuan Weizhongxin" [Research on Fiscal Institution Reform in Ming Dynasty Zhejiang—Centered on the Lijia, Junyao, and Postal Service]. PhD diss., Tsinghua University, 2014.

This dissertation examines the transformation of Zhejiang's fiscal system from the Hongwu foundations through the Single Whip reforms, focusing on three categories of obligation: lijia, junyao, and postal service. Ding's central argument challenges conventional interpretations that view the gradual shift toward hiring substitutes as constituting "silverization" of these levies. Instead, he contends that true silverization only occurred with Pang Shangpeng's reforms, which removed institutional barriers preventing the monetization of local spending and tax remittance. The dissertation emphasizes that local experiments in reform remained constrained by central government demands on localities, explaining why surveillance officials with direct court access played crucial roles in confirming deep structural changes. Ding's methodology combines quantitative analysis of fiscal documents with institutional history, drawing extensively on local gazetteers and central memorials to reconstruct the regional specifics of reform implementation.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal reform, Ding's work provides essential comparative context for understanding Pang Shangpeng's subsequent tenure in Fujian. His analysis of how Pang's reforms established "marketized fiscal structures" and public finance management systems illuminates the institutional template that encountered greater resistance in Fujian's more commercially complex environment. Ding's insight that Pang in Zhejiang was confirming existing local tendencies rather than imposing top-down reform helps explain why Pang's Fujian reforms proved more contentious—the immediate fiscal crisis of the 1560s had already passed, and local commercial interests had developed their own arrangements. The dissertation's three-way periodization of Ming fiscal historiography—"Ming-Qing exceptionalism," "Mid-Ming return," and "Mid-Ming reform"—provides a useful framework for situating Fujian's experience within broader empire-wide trends. Ding's engagement with Japanese scholarship, particularly Iwai Shigeki and Yamane Yukio, ensures the work connects to international historiographical debates about the nature of the Ming fiscal state.
