Qi Jiguang 戚繼光, *Zhizhi Tang Ji* 止止堂集 (Collected Works from the Hall of Stopping), originally compiled during the Ming dynasty.

Qi Jiguang (1528-1588) ranks among the most celebrated military commanders in Chinese history, renowned for developing innovative tactics and training methods that successfully suppressed *wokou* piracy along the southeastern coast. His collected works, centered on the *Minhai Jishi* (閩海紀事) section, provide detailed autobiographical accounts of his campaigns in Fujian between 1562 and 1567, including the crucial battles at Hengyu, Niutian, and Lin Dun that broke pirate power in the region. The collection moves beyond mere military narrative to offer profound insight into the challenges of raising, training, and sustaining effective forces within Ming institutional constraints.

The work documents Qi's pivotal relationship with Wang Daokun (汪道昆), the Fujian surveillance commissioner who championed Qi's strategy of recruiting and training local "Yiwu troops" (烏傷兵) rather than relying on ineffective hereditary garrisons or expensive mercenary forces. Qi's funerary inscriptions for subordinates like Chen Shoubei (陳守備) and Wang Canjiang (王參將) provide rare glimpses into the social composition of Ming military forces—these were often drawn from mining communities and rural areas, motivated by sign-up bonuses and monthly wages rather than hereditary obligation. The collection also memorializes Tan Lun (譚綸), documenting their collaborative relationship in designing coastal defense systems that would later be transferred to the northern frontier. For historians of Minnan's military transformation, Qi's writings reveal how successful campaigns depended on personal relationships between commanders and local officials, the professionalization of military labor through silver wages, and the gradual eclipse of the *weisuo* system by recruited battalions—a process that accelerated precisely in the Minnan region during the Jiajing-Wanli transition.
