《海澄縣志》(Haicheng County Gazetteer)

The Haicheng County Gazetteer documents the establishment and development of Haicheng County (海澄縣), created in Longqing 1 (1567) from coastal territories formerly part of Longxi County, representing the Ming state's administrative response to maritime crisis and commercial opportunity on the Zhangzhou coast. The compilation was requested by "Nanhai Liang Gong" (南海梁公) while serving in Fujian, and edited by Wang (王兆陽) and others including Zhang Xie (張燮), a noted local scholar. The prefaces articulate a vision of administrative creation as transformation: Haicheng was originally where "mountains met the sea," but through "vigorous diking it was made to submit as soil" (處者阻師戰波臣而土之). The editors defended the need for a new gazetteer despite the county's recent establishment, comparing Haicheng to Shunde—another recently-established county with a good gazetteer—and noting that the pirate/bandit (寇) problem had improved precisely because Haicheng became a county, as its people's maritime livelihood required official protection.

The gazetteer's institutional history traces the evolution of coastal defense infrastructure from the Jiajing crisis through the Tianqi period. The city wall was originally constructed in Jiajing 36 (1557) by Vice Censor-in-Chief Ruan E (中丞阮鶚) to protect the 8th and 9th wards, predating Haicheng's establishment as a county seat. When Haicheng became a county in Longqing 1, these wards were reorganized into two forts (*bao* 堡) responsible for wall expansion. In Longqing 4, Prefect Luo Qingxiao (羅青霄) secured silver funds to hire artisans for stone reinforcement of earth walls. The gazetteer documents two key moments of maritime threat response: the Tianqi *Renxu* (1622) arrival of "Red Barbarians" (紅夷, the Dutch) at Haicheng prompted authorities to repair and expand sea-facing fortifications, and the subsequent *Dingmao* year (1627) invasion by "Zheng bandits/pirates" (鄭賊) was repelled through coordinated defense between the two forts. These passages illustrate how coastal county gazetteers served as records of ongoing negotiation between maritime commercial populations, military threats, and state administrative adaptation during the late Ming.
