Matsumoto Yoshimi 松本善海. *Chūgoku Chihō Jichi Hatten Shi* [History of the Development of Chinese Local Autonomy]. Tokyo: Kyūko Shoin, 1977.

Matsumoto's foundational study positions the Yuan dynasty as a crucial turning point in Chinese institutional history, arguing that many of Zhu Yuanzhang's centralizing reforms drew inspiration from Mongol administrative practices, particularly the long-term division of local authority designed for minority rule over a Han majority. The work traces the evolution of Ming fiscal institutions from the Hongwu land surveys and household registration systems through the mid-Ming reforms, emphasizing how the preponderance of independent owner-farmers—a condition intensified by Yuan-Ming transition land divisions—conditioned the lijia system's eventual breakdown as land reconcentrated in the hands of non-taxpayers. Matsumoto's analysis of the "equalized corvée" (*junyaofa*) system established in 1436 and its empire-wide extension by the Tianshun and Hongzhi reigns highlights how reform attempts to regularize miscellaneous corvée (*zayi*) actually created new intermediate categories rather than eliminating arbitrary impositions. The study documents the progressive disconnect between the *huangce* household registers and land ownership realities, attributing this largely to lack of genuine surveying interest rather than technical incapacity.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal reform, Matsumoto's work provides essential institutional background for understanding the pressures that drove Pang Shangpeng's and Liu Yaohui's reforms. His analysis of how the Zhengtong 1 (1436) silver commutation of southern tax grain established the principle that "the poor contribute labor, the rich contribute money" illuminates the ideological foundations for the later Single Whip reforms Fujian officials implemented. Matsumoto's observation that the Jiajing fiscal debates focused primarily on simplification of an overly complex system helps contextualize why Fujian reformers emphasized transparency and streamlining over revenue maximization. The work's discussion of how baojia systems were first implemented in Fujian during the Zhengtong years due to mining banditry, predating Wang Yangming's more famous Jiangxi implementation, places Fujian at the center of Ming local security institution innovation. Matsumoto's conclusion that top-down village structures may have been less important than "natural" gatherings of 100-300 households offers a useful corrective to state-centric narratives of Fujian's maritime commercialization, suggesting that local commercial networks may have operated through indigenous associational forms rather than imposed administrative frameworks.
