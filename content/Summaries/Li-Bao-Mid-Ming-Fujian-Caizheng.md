Li Bao 李寶. "Ming Zhongye Fujian Caizheng Shouru Tizhi Yanbian Yanjiu" 明中期福建財政收入體制演變研究 [Research on the Evolution of Mid-Ming Fujian's Fiscal Revenue System]. Master's thesis, Unspecified institution, n.d.

Li Bao's master's thesis challenges prevailing scholarly narratives about Ming fiscal decline by demonstrating that while local administrative functions contracted during the mid-Ming breakdown of the *lijia* system, government capacities actually expanded through *yifa* (役法, service levy) reforms. Fujian emerges as a pioneering region in these reforms, implementing unique innovations like the *gangyin fa* (綱銀法, framework silver method). Li argues that these reforms established a fiscal system centered on government offices, creating what he terms "local fiscal expansion"—an increase in official oversight capacity despite overall administrative contraction. The study challenges Zheng Zhenman's interpretation of provincial withdrawal from public works, noting that such works were simultaneously assigned to more specialized offices under central supervision.

The thesis traces several key reform stages: the *bafen fa* (八分法, eight-tenths method) implemented in 1520 by Fujian Surveillance Commissioner Shen Zhuo 沈灼, which converted grain and labor levies at a rate of 8 *fen* of silver per *liang*/*ding*; the *shiduan fa* (十段法, ten-section method) implemented around 1537 by Censor Li Yuanyang 李元陽, which calculated county-wide tax burdens and divided them into ten equal portions; and the ultimate *yitiaobian fa* (一條鞭法, Single Whip method), which Li argues was implemented "smoothly" in Fujian because earlier reforms had already prepared the ground. Particularly valuable is Li's documentation of how the tribute system (*shanggong wuliao* 上供物料) evolved from in-kind to monetary payments, with Fujian's levies expanding from 4 items under Hongwu to 23 in the Hongzhi era. While acknowledging that these reforms unintentionally "stimulated" commercial economy by forcing government offices to participate in markets, Li emphasizes that this was an unintended consequence of reforms designed to maintain state extraction capacity rather than a deliberate strategy of economic transformation.
