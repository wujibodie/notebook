Grove, Linda, and Christian Daniels, eds. *State and Society in China: Japanese Perspectives on Ming-Qing Social and Economic History*. Tokyo: University of Tokyo Press, 1984.

This edited volume translates seminal Japanese scholarship on Ming-Qing social and economic history into English, providing crucial theoretical frameworks for understanding fiscal reform, rural control, and social conflict in late imperial China. The collection includes several essays directly relevant to Minnan's sixteenth-century transformations. Oyama Masaaki's essay on large landownership in the Jiangnan delta examines the development of hereditary bondservant classes, citing Tanaka Tadao's research on Longxi, Fujian, where "the ancestors of these serfs sold themselves during the Ming dynasty." This material connects to broader patterns of fictive kinship and lineage registration described by Szonyi and Zheng Zhenman, illuminating how labor relations and social control operated in Minnan's commercialized agrarian economy.

Tanaka Masatoshi's contribution on popular uprisings, rent resistance, and bondservant rebellions identifies the 1448-49 Deng Maoqi rebellion as "the earliest clear-cut example of tenants rising to oppose the payment of rent," representing "the first great rebellion in Chinese history genuinely led by the tenants themselves." Tanaka traces the rebellion's origins to silver mining operations opened to meet new levies of jinhuayin, with dismissed mine workers joining vagrant farmers impoverished by labor-service extortions. The essay documents how by 1449, a majority of land in Jianyang county was reportedly owned by absentee landlords. A 1612 Quanzhou gazetteer passage cited by Tanaka reveals how cash crop cultivation encouraged rent resistance: "The tenants in the morning go out to the fields to bring in the harvest, and in the evening they go off to the town to sell their crops to the merchants...tenants made pacts among themselves beforehand not to pay rent to their landlords."

Tsurumi Naohiro's essay on rural control in the Ming dynasty examines the lijia system's evolution and breakdown, arguing that the system's collapse stemmed from uniform tax collection exacerbating actually existing inequalities among households. Yamane Yukio's contribution traces reforms in the service levy system from the fifteenth to sixteenth centuries, documenting how the equalized labor service (均徭法/junyao fa) was introduced to Fujian by Administration Commissioner Zhu Ying. Yamane notes that while land-tax commutation to silver (金花银/jinhuayin) began in 1436, labor service commutation developed later, originating in wealthy households paying others to serve their labor levies—a practice that evolved into systematic silver commutation by the early sixteenth century. The essay cites Sheng Yong's implementation in Fujian of a system similar to the shiduan fa (十段法), establishing ten divisions based on acreage and adult male figures to allocate service levies more equitably.
