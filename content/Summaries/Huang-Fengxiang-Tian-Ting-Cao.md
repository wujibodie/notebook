Huang Fengxiang 黃鳳翔. *Tian Ting Cao Ershi Juan* [The Twenty-Chapter Tian Pavilion Collection]. Wanli 40 (1612) edition. Reprinted in *Xuxiu Siku Quanshu* [Continued Complete Library of the Four Treasuries], vol. 1356, Ji bu, Biejilei, 1-406. Shanghai: Shanghai Guji Chubanshe, 2002.

This collected work by the late Ming Quanzhou literatus Huang Fengxiang contains 98 prefaces (*xu*) and numerous temple inscriptions (*ji*), many focused on the Quanzhou region including Tong'an and Anping. The collection includes a significant number of biographies (*zhuan*) and elegies, with notable attention to women's lives. The most historically significant section for fiscal and maritime history is the "Anping San Liezhuan" (Three Biographies of Anping Martyrs), written in response to the 1603 Manila massacre that devastated the overseas Chinese merchant community. Huang documents three cases of female suicide following the deaths of husbands and fathers in Luzon, using these tragic narratives to reflect on the moral tensions between commercial ambition (*tanfu xuncai*) and heroic reputation (*lieshi xunming*). The biographies provide rare contemporary documentation of Fujian's maritime trading community, including the practice of annual trading voyages to Luzon and the catastrophic human costs when Sino-Spanish relations collapsed.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal and military institutions, Huang's collection offers invaluable grassroots perspective on the social consequences of maritime commercialization. The "Anping San Liezhuan" directly connects to fiscal history through its documentation of the Manila massacre's impact on merchant families, demonstrating the risks that overseas traders faced despite—or because of—their integration into global silver flows. The collection's emphasis on women as moral protagonists in commercial society reflects broader tensions in late Ming Fujian between Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy and the practical necessities of maritime livelihoods. Huang's local focus and his connections to prominent merchant families (the biographies suggest personal relationships with the deceased) provide evidence for how commercial wealth translated into gentry status through cultural production. The collection's absence of poetry and concentration on prose genres associated with social commemoration (temple inscriptions, biographies, prefaces) itself indicates the priorities of a literatus deeply embedded in local commercial networks.
