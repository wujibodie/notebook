Wu Xiu'an 吳修安. "Fujian's Early Development" [福建早期發展]. Unpublished research or monograph on medieval Fujian economic history.

This comprehensive study traces Fujian's economic development from the Six Dynasties period through the Song dynasty, establishing the deep historical roots of coastal-interior divergence that characterized the province's later development. Wu adopts Fernand Braudel's geographic-economic approach to examine how coastal and interior regions followed distinct trajectories of economic development from the medieval period onward. The research challenges previous scholarship that treated Fujian as an integral whole, demonstrating instead that the province comprised two distinct economic zones: the coastal region centered on the Min River, Jin River, and Zhang River deltas, and the mountainous interior connected to Jiangxi through river valleys. This divergence originated in administrative decisions made during the Six Dynasties period, when the Jin dynasty placed the entire coast under Jin'an Commandery (晋安郡), separating it from inland Fujian beyond the Minzhong mountains.

Wu's analysis of settlement patterns reveals that early immigrants to coastal Fujian during the Han and Six Dynasties periods were primarily poor refugees, convicts, and garrisoned soldiers rather than literate shi families. Archaeological evidence shows that Han-style tombs in coastal Fujian resembled those in the Yangtze delta during the Jin period before developing distinct local styles by the Chen dynasty, yet these tombs were small and lacked epitaphs, suggesting immigrants lacked connections to literate elites. The study documents how population growth from fewer than 100,000 registered individuals in the Sui to over 2 million by the Song resulted primarily from immigration, particularly the Wang Chao and Wang Shenzhi-led movement of 20,30,000 people at the end of the Tang. By the late Tang, Quanzhou already had double-cropping rice, and by the Five Dynasties period, Zhangzhou and Quanzhou had developed gold- and silverworking industries while importing precious metals from overseas. Wu concludes that by the Song unification, "coastal economic development had gradually been drawn into the currents of commodity economy, while the interior's rural villages still lived self-sufficient lives, not yet drawn into commodity economy currents," establishing the foundation for Minnan's later commercial prosperity and agricultural deficit.
