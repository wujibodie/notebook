Chen Boyi 陳博翼. *Haifang zhi Dao: Mingdai Nanhai diqu de Haiyu, Junzheng, Shehui yu Zongjiao* [The Way of Coastal Defense: Maritime Domains, Military-Political Affairs, Society, and Religion in the South Sea Region During the Ming Dynasty]. Beijing: Zhonghua Shuju, 2023.

Chen Boyi's comprehensive study examines the evolution of Ming coastal defense institutions across administrative boundaries, focusing on how surveillance officials including governors-general (*zongdu*), grand coordinators (*xunfu*), and surveillance vice commissioners (*ancha fushi*, including coastal defense and maritime circuit deputies) acquired increasingly specialized authority. The book documents how these officials both enforced coordination among the scattered jurisdictions of county-level civilian administration and guard-weisuo military units, while overcoming the limitations of regional military commanders (*duzhihui shi*) who lacked capacity to coordinate larger geographic scopes and multiple organizational hierarchies. Chen critically evaluates the "commerce thesis" of piracy—summarized in the maxim "when markets are open, pirates convert to merchants; when markets are prohibited, merchants convert to pirates"—arguing that this formulation remains imprecise because "merchants" represent only one identity marker among broader populations of ordinary people driven to desperation by hunger and poverty. The study advocates for strengthened research on the daily activities of coastal populations to understand maritime violence beyond commercial determinism.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal and military institutions, Chen's work provides essential methodological and empirical foundations. His critique of over-reliance on military treatises like *Chouhai Tubian*, *Haifang Zuanyao*, and *Wubei Zhi*—which he argues creates illusions of well-organized defensive hierarchies—directly supports the dissertation's use of local gazetteers to document the "embarrassed" reality of coastal defense. Chen's emphasis on the value of local gazetteers for recording specific invasion locations and routes, and their documentation of military defense and bandit suppression in concrete geographic terms rather than abstract policy language, validates the source base for the Minnan case study. The book's attention to the "nesting" (*chao*) phenomenon—locations that were "a bit far from shore but not too far" serving as trading venues, inland raiding bases, water and food storage points, and hideouts for dependents—illuminates the spatial challenges facing Liu Yaohui's attempts to establish maritime customs and coastal security. Chen's discussion of research by Huang Zhongqing on Zhejiang-Fujian maritime guard-weisuo, water forts, and islands, alongside He Mengxing's work on the Wuyu Water Fort as a model for near-shore defense studies, provides crucial comparative context for understanding the Fujian military-fiscal apparatus. The study's call for expanded use of historical, philosophical, and collected works (*shi*, *zi*, and *ji* categories) beyond military treatises aligns with the dissertation's methodology of using collected writings by officials like Yu Dayou, Lin Xiyuan, and Pang Shangpeng to reconstruct the practical challenges of fiscal-military reform.
