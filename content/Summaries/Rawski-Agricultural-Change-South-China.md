Rawski, Evelyn Sakakida. *Agricultural Change and the Peasant Economy of South China*. Harvard East Asian Series 66. Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1972.

This foundational study examines agricultural development and peasant economy in Fujian and Guangdong provinces during the Ming and Qing periods, providing essential context for understanding how commercialization transformed southern China's rural society. Rawski approaches Chinese commerce through the lens of agricultural history, arguing that the influence of long-distance trade can be measured through the proliferation of rural periodic markets—using rising market numbers as an index of commercial influence penetrating the countryside. Her research demonstrates that Zhangzhou prefecture experienced explosive market growth, expanding from 11 periodic markets in 1491 to 65 by 1628, reflecting the region's deepening integration into maritime trade networks. The study reveals that by the 1570s, Zhangzhou land prices had inflated to 2-3 times those elsewhere in Fujian (8 taels per mou compared to 2-4 taels in Jianning), directly attributable to silver inflows from expanded trade with Manila and other overseas markets.

Rawski's analysis of tenancy systems is particularly valuable for understanding Minnan's social structure. She documents the "one field, two lords" (一田两主/yitian liangzhu) system, wherein tenants could obtain heritable cultivation rights through payment of "manured field silver" (糞土银/fentu yin), creating a topsoil right that could be sublet or sold independently of the landlord's subsoil ownership. This system, widespread by the sixteenth century, enabled tenant farmers to absorb productivity gains from improved seed strains, tillage methods, and fertilizers while landlords received fixed rents. Rawski notes that Fujian landlords, unlike their north Chinese counterparts, generally provided neither tools nor seed to tenants and maintained only tenuous links to their land through rent collection. The study also documents absentee landlordism, with Ming contracts typically stipulating that tenants deliver rent rice to landlords' warehouses, often located in walled cities. Rawski's examination of the relationship between commercialization, land tenure, and social mobility—evidenced by dramatic increases in Jinshi degrees awarded to coastal counties after 1520—provides crucial context for understanding how maritime trade reshaped Minnan's elite formation.
