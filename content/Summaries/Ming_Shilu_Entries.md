# Ming Shilu 明實錄 (Veritable Records of the Ming)

**Citation:** *Ming Shilu 明實錄* [Veritable Records of the Ming]. Compiled by the Ming History Bureau. Modern editions include Academia Sinica searchable database and Zhongyang yanjiuyuan lishi yuyan yanjiusuo editions (1961-1966). Entries referenced: Shizong (Jiajing) reign, volumes 465, 470, 564; Longqing reign, volumes 51, 63; Wanli reign, volumes 1-4 (various dates 1573-1576).

**Summary:**

The Ming Veritable Records constitute the official court chronicle compiled from daily records of imperial audiences, memorials, and edicts, providing authoritative documentation of central government perspectives on provincial affairs. The entries concerning Liu Yaohui's career illuminate his rise from a junior censor who impeached Hu Zongxian during the Jiajing wokou campaigns (1559) through various metropolitan posts to his 1573 appointment as Fujian Provincial Governor. These records document the court's evolving assessment of coastal defense policy, from the Jiajing emperor's defense of Hu Zongxian against Liu's charges to the Wanli court's approval of Liu's fiscal and military initiatives in Fujian. The Shilu entries capture the formal administrative logic of the Ming state: the precise language of imperial appointments, the bureaucratic processing of provincial requests, and the standardized vocabulary of rewards, promotions, and disciplinary actions.

For the Lin Feng pirate campaigns of 1574-1576, the Shilu provides the official chronology of events and the court's understanding (or misunderstanding) of developments. Entries trace Lin Feng's movements from his initial feigned surrender, his flight to Taiwan (Wanggang), his defeat by indigenous forces there, his subsequent appearance in Guangdong waters, and his final expulsion to Luzon. The records document the court's interventions in provincial disputes: its initial mandate for joint Fujian-Guangdong operations, its arbitration between competing claims of military success, and its ultimate validation of Liu Yaohui's strategy over Guangdong officials' preference for pacification. The Shilu's laconic accounts of "rewards and punishments" (*shangfa*) and its record of Liu's 1576 promotion to Right Censor-in-Chief following Lin Feng's defeat reveal how the central government conceptualized and incentivized provincial military success. When read alongside private correspondence and provincial memorials, the Shilu illuminates the gaps between court knowledge and local realities, as well as the formal protocols that structured late Ming center-province relations.
