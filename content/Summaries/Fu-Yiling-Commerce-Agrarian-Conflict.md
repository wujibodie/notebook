# Fu Yiling on Ming-Qing Commerce and Agrarian Conflict

## Fu Yiling, *Ming-Qing Shidai Shangren ji Shangye Ziben* (明清时代商人及商业资本)

Fu Yiling, *Ming-Qing Shidai Shangren ji Shangye Ziben* [Merchants and Commercial Capital in the Ming-Qing Period] (Beijing: Renmin Chubanshe, 1956).

Fu Yiling's foundational study examines the development of Chinese commercial capitalism within "feudal" constraints, arguing that the Ming-Qing period represented a transition phase characterized by quantitative accumulation of commercial capital without qualitative transformation toward industrial capitalism. Drawing on extensive gazetteer and literary sources, Fu documents the Song-Yuan origins of advanced commercial techniques and infrastructure, noting that Chinese trade institutions were "more advanced than in Europe" at that time. His analysis attributes Ming commercial expansion to "the great increase in production unleashed by rural class struggle"—specifically Zhu Yuanzhang's pro-farmer policies—while emphasizing how commercialized agriculture "stimulated the appetites" of landlords and led to renewed land concentration.

For Minnan specifically, Fu provides crucial documentation of Fujian merchants' transition from domestic to overseas trade preceding European stimulus during the Chenghua-Hongzhi periods (1465–1505). Citing Jiajing 3 (1524) reports of Fujian vessels intercepting tribute trade ships and mid-Jiajing Portuguese presence at Yuegang, Haicheng, and Wuyu, Fu establishes indigenous foundations for maritime commercialization. His citation of Xiaotiankun's claim that most Chinese ships at Malacca originated from Yuegang, and reproduction of Xu Fuyuan's Wanli 21 (1593) census documenting 44 ships to Eastern and Western Oceans (16 to Luzon, 4 each to Banten, Siam, Old Port, and Jiaozhi), quantifies the trade expansion that Liu Yaohui's 1572 customs establishment sought to regulate. Fu's analysis of the Haicheng *pushang* (guild merchants) responsible for tax collection illustrates how commercial self-regulation emerged alongside official maritime taxation.

Fu's conceptual framework—emphasizing how merchant organizations remained "feudal" due to lack of clear permanent structures beyond "rich investors and poor agents" and "extra-economic exploitation" of apprentices—has been criticized by subsequent scholars (particularly Wood) for teleological assumptions about capitalism. However, his empirical documentation of Quanzhou Xu clan genealogy—tracing their development from silk traders to spinning workshop owners who learned techniques from Jianning family members and established iron and paper factories in Shaanxi—provides concrete evidence of Minnan merchant capital's geographic and sectoral diversification. His citation of Wu Meicun's poem documenting Fujian merchants purchasing cotton in Taicang during the Longqing-Wanli periods, and subsequent Jiangnan village suffering when these supply chains were disrupted, illuminates regional economic integration that Minnan's maritime commerce made possible.

The work's conclusions regarding Fujian merchants' ultimate limitation—that they carried "rural landlord logic of earning without work to the simple circulation of commodities" without involving themselves in production—reflects Fu's Marxist theoretical commitments. His observation that European colonial victories in Southeast Asia reduced Chinese capitalists to "mere intermediaries" provides geopolitical context for understanding why Minnan's commercial expansion did not produce autonomous capitalist development. For the dissertation's focus on fiscal-military transformation, Fu's emphasis on the "excessively close relationship between merchants and aristocracy" as inhibiting capitalist transition illuminates why commercial prosperity failed to translate into political leverage for institutional reform.

## Fu Yiling, "Ming-Qing Shidai Fujian Diannong Fengchao Kaozheng" (明清时代福建佃农风潮考证)

Fu Yiling, "Ming-Qing Shidai Fujian Diannong Fengchao Kaozheng" [Investigation of Tenant Uprisings in Ming-Qing Fujian], unpublished research paper.

Fu Yiling's study of agrarian conflict in Fujian provides essential social context for understanding the pressures driving fiscal reform and the limits of its implementation. Opening with Cai Qing's observation that "of all the monk's fields in the world, Fujian has the most; of Fujian, Quanzhou has the most," Fu documents extreme land concentration that intensified tenant exploitation. His reproduction of Xie Zhaozhe's *Wu Zazu* observation that wealthy clans amassed land through force—particularly uncultivated and temple land—such that "the rich daily become richer while the poor daily become poorer," captures the socioeconomic polarization underlying Minnan's fiscal exceptionalism. Notably, Fu compresses Xie's original text to omit the crucial observation that low land taxes combined with high yields made Fujian land especially attractive to investors—a compression revealing Fu's theoretical prioritization of class struggle over institutional analysis.

The work categorizes three types of tenant disturbances: those inspired by feudal exactions, those stemming from measurement conflicts, and those involving direct resistance to rent payments. Fu's analysis of Deng Maoqi's 1440s rebellion—Fujian's first major agrarian uprising—emphasizes discontent with fixed grain-delivery quotas and "winter gift" (*dongsheng*) fees, arguing this represented "the first clear expression of the people's disgust with feudal tributes and labor service." His documentation of measurement disputes reveals the practical mechanics of exploitation: because various regions used different *dou* measures without standardization, landlords manipulated conversions to increase takings. The work preserves crucial records of Chongzhen-era protests in Quanzhou and Nan'an (1631–1642) over standardized measures, escalating to armed rebellion in Nan'an in 1642 that required gentry militia suppression.

Fu's analysis of tenancy "contracts" challenges their apparent contractual nature, arguing they remained "status-based" (*shenfen de*) due to lifetime terms and tenants' abject dependency on landlords. His documentation of tenants transferred with land—including an episode from the Quanzhou *Fuzhi* where an official transferred Kaiyuan Temple lands with attached tenants to the Zhu Wengong Temple—illustrates the commodification of human dependency within Fujian's "feudal" structure. The work's attention to regional variation in rental arrangements—sharecroppers taking half produce in Haicheng but as little as two or three tenths in Yong'an—provides empirical grounding for understanding how land tenure flexibility both enabled commercial adaptation and perpetuated exploitation.

The study's conclusion emphasizes that tenant-landlord inequality, combined with rural isolation and "backwardness," provided background for disturbances that threatened the social order Single Whip reform sought to stabilize. For Minnan fiscal history specifically, Fu's documentation of how military draft obligations could be imposed on tenants during the wokou crises—citing Deng Maoqi's grievance recorded in the Yanping *Fuzhi*—illustrates the intersection of fiscal militarization and agrarian exploitation that shaped popular responses to state expansion. His observation that "fees called *shizu* and *xiangzu*" (payments for transporting rent grain to urban granaries) imposed additional burdens on cultivators reveals the multi-layered extraction that silverization simplified but did not eliminate.
