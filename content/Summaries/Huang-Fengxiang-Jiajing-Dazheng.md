# Huang Fengxiang's Jiajing Administration Records

## Huang Fengxiang, *Jiajing Dazheng Leibian* (嘉靖大政类编)

Huang Fengxiang (jinshi 1568), *Jiajing Dazheng Leibian* [Classified Compilation of Major Jiajing Reign Policies], juan on "Southern Wo" (*Nan Wo*), pp. 471–485, *Siku Quanshu Cunmu Congshu* *Shibu* vol. 55 (Jinan: Qi Lu Shushe, 1996).

Huang Fengxiang's classified compilation provides crucial narrative coverage of the wokou crisis that fundamentally reshaped Minnan's fiscal-military institutions. The work's "Southern Wo" section, spanning pages 471–485, opens with the Jiajing 2 (1523) Ningbo Incident—conflict between Japanese monks Seng Zongshe and Seng Ruizuo that triggered the first major coastal crisis—and traces the subsequent fifty-year evolution of piracy, suppression campaigns, and administrative reform. Huang's preface notes that the Jiajing reign was "one of the longest in imperial history," framing his compilation as preserving historical facts (*gushi*) despite his declining eyesight preventing final revision.

The compilation documents the wokou's southward movement into Fujian from winter of Jiajing 36 (1557), when acting Xunfu Ruan E bribed pirate splinter groups attacking Fuzhou with silver—earning censure but protection through Yan Song's patronage. Huang provides harrowing accounts of Jiajing 37–38 (1558–1559), when pirates attacked Xinghua and Quanzhou, looting towns and killing officials before wintering at Wuyu and Nan'aodao: "The three prefectures of Xing, Quan, and Zhang beyond the city walls all became pirate nests, granaries were emptied, fields became wasteland." His documentation of the Jiajing 41 (1562) fall of Xinghua city to "new pirates arriving in great numbers" (*xin wo da zhi*) establishes the crisis magnitude that prompted Tan Lun's appointment as Fujian Xunfu and the dispatch of Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou's armies.

Huang's narrative culminates in the Jiajing 42–43 (1563–1564) campaigns that "exterminated the pirates," including the night raid acclaimed as "Fujian's greatest military achievement" (*min zhong zhan gong di yi*). The work preserves contemporary assessments of official conduct, noting that Zhu Wan's aggressive anti-piracy enforcement—burning ships and prosecuting gentry-smugglers—created "powerful enemies among coastal elites" whose accusations drove him to suicide. Huang concludes with Liu Ji's Jiajing 44 (1565) memorial rejecting proposals to open Ningbo trade on the Macao model, arguing that "Zhejiang's coastal ports are many but military ships are few, making it most difficult to guard against profiteering commoners provoking incidents." This documentation is essential for understanding how military crisis created both the fiscal pressures and administrative opportunities that shaped Minnan's Single Whip reforms.
