# Min Shu (Book of Fujian)

## He Qiaoyuan, *Min Shu* (闽书)

He Qiaoyuan (1558–1632), *Min Shu* [Book of Fujian], 154 juan (Fujian: Ming Wanli period manuscript; modern editions available).

He Qiaoyuan's monumental *Min Shu* stands as the most comprehensive provincial history of Fujian compiled during the Ming dynasty, providing indispensable documentation of Minnan's fiscal, military, and commercial transformation during the Jiajing-Wanli periods. As a Quanzhou literatus who served as Minister of Rites (*Libu You Shilang*), He drew upon his extensive personal networks and official experience to compile detailed accounts of maritime trade, the wokou crisis, and relations with overseas Chinese communities. The work contains crucial memorials and correspondence regarding coastal defense reorganization, including Xu Zhong's assessment that "today's battlefields are not exclusively in the Northwest but also in the Southeast"—capturing the strategic reorientation that drove Fujian's fiscal militarization.

The *Min Shu* provides systematic documentation of institutional reforms under successive Fujian governors. Its "Customs" (*Fengsu*) and "Military Defense" (*Hanyu Zhi*) sections trace the evolution of coastal defense from the Jiajing 42 (1563) establishment of the Fujian zongbing position—dividing the province into Funing, Xinghua, and Zhangzhou circuits—through Liu Yaohui's Wanli 4 (1576) reorganization placing Nan'ao under joint Fujian-Guangdong jurisdiction with a deputy commander. He's biographical entries for military officials from Zhu Wan through Pang Shangpeng preserve assessments of their tenures, including the observation that Liu Yaohui's victories against Lin Feng created surpluses where "the army had surplus cash and the granaries surplus grain" (*yu shi jun you yuqian, cang you yudai*). The work's accounts of customs revenue establishment under Liu Yaohui, including the three-tier tax system (water tax, land tax, and Luzon-specific surcharges), provide quantitative benchmarks for maritime commercial taxation.

For understanding Minnan's social history, He Qiaoyuan's documentation of the wokou crisis's impact on lineages—citing Cai Xiang's Song-period observation that powerful lineages dominated Fujian's socioeconomic structure through absorbing peasant land—establishes long-term patterns of coastal stratification. His correspondence with Zheng Zhilong and advocacy for maritime trade opening make the *Min Shu* essential for understanding how late Ming Fujian's commercial-official networks evolved from the fiscal-military institutions analyzed in earlier periods. The work preserves unique details about the Manila massacre's impact on Quanzhou merchants and the development of Penghu garrisoning, bridging the dissertation's focus period with the Ming-Qing transition.
