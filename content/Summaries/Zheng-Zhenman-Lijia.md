# Zheng Zhenman on Lineage and Lijia

## Zheng Zhenman, *Ming-Qing Fujian Lijia* (明清福建里甲)

Zheng Zhenman, *Ming-Qing Fujian Lijia* [Lijia Registration in Ming-Qing Fujian], in *Ming-Qing Fujian Jiazu Zuzhi yu Shehui Bianqian* [Family Lineage Organization and Social Change in Ming-Qing Fujian] (Changsha: Hunan Jiaoyu Chubanshe, 1992).

Zheng Zhenman's pioneering study examines the intersection of Ming fiscal institutions and lineage formation in Fujian, arguing that lijia household registration became hereditary from the mid-Ming period and served as a marker of lineage status. Drawing on extensive local gazetteer research, Zheng documents how the lijia system's collapse into "one surname per jia" (*mei jia shuyu yi xing*) reflected the consolidation of entire lineages under single household registrations, particularly after population flight from taxation reduced Xianyou County from 64 registered li to just 12 during the Zhengtong reign. His analysis of Chenghua-era lease contracts reveals how immigrant families negotiated corvée obligations through legally enforceable agreements with li and jia heads, demonstrating the routinization of tax apportionment authority at the local level.

Zheng's work is methodologically significant for connecting institutional history with social history, showing how the lijia system's hereditary transformation both strengthened family organizations and caused their "deformation" (*bianxing*). He traces the widespread adoption of heir-son practices in Ming Fujian to tax-avoidance strategies, while documenting how lineages increasingly assumed tax collection functions by the late Qing—culminating in official advocacy of "returning tax households to lineages" (*lianghu guizong*). For Minnan fiscal history specifically, Zheng's analysis of the Yongle-era register deterioration and the role of lijia heads in apportioning silverized levies provides essential context for understanding how local power structures mediated the implementation of Single Whip reforms. His documentation of lineage militia organization during the wokou crisis, citing Gu Yanwu's observation that wall-building began in Zhangzhou after 1561, illuminates the military-fiscal nexus that drove regional institutional change.
