# Gu Yanwu's Analysis of Fujian Fiscal and Military Systems

## Gu Yanwu, *Tianxia Junguo Libing Shu*: Quanzhou and Zhangzhou Sections (天下郡国利病书)

Gu Yanwu (1613–1682), *Tianxia Junguo Libing Shu* [The Strategic Advantages and Disadvantages of the Prefectures of the Empire]: "Quanzhou Wei Tuntian" [Quanzhou Guard Military Colonies] and "Zhangzhou Fuzhi" [Zhangzhou Prefecture Gazetteer] sections, in *Gu Tinglin Shiwen Ji* [Collected Works of Gu Yanwu] (Beijing: Zhonghua Shuju, 1959).

Gu Yanwu's magisterial survey of Ming provincial administration provides the most comprehensive analysis of Fujian's fiscal and military institutions during their late Ming transformation. Compiled during his extensive travels and based on local gazetteer research, the work's Quanzhou section opens with a devastating critique of weisuo military colony collapse: despite theoretical ratios of four soldiers on duty to six cultivating, mountainous terrain and non-native soldiers' unfamiliarity with conditions resulted in abandoned land and empty granaries by the Zhengtong reign (1436–1449). Gu documents how soldiers pawned land to local elites, becoming rent-collectors vulnerable to famine—creating a system "virtually useless for actual defense" that collapsed completely by the Zhengde period (1506–1521).

The work's analysis of Quanzhou land taxation reveals the complexity that frustrated reform efforts. Gu reproduces extensive documentation from Wanli-era gazetteers on land categories (guantian, mintian, temple land, confiscated land) and the "floating grain" (*fuliang*) problem—productive land removed from tax registers through false claims of flood damage. His detailed account of Peng Guoguang's Jinjiang County land survey—the only official to conduct proper *fangtian* (measured assessment)—shows how comprehensive resurvey increased registered land by 1,480 *qing* while creating political backlash. Gu's documentation of temple land appropriation under military pressure traces the progression from Jiajing-period confiscations to Wanli-era exactions, culminating in Jin Xuezeng's Wanli 25 (1597) seizure of six-tenths of monastic lands—a fiscal strategy originating in Tan Lun's Jiajing 43 (1564) "Four-Six Method" (*siliu fen fa*).

For understanding the Single Whip's impact, Gu's Zhangzhou section provides crucial testimony. He attributes the reform's invention to Wang Zongmu's lost treatise *Jun Shu* (Book of Equalization), documenting Pang Shangpeng's implementation through direct quotation: "The Single Whip method combines throughout prefectures, subprefectures, and counties the ten-year quotas of summer tax, autumn grain, retained and forwarded deliveries, and all miscellaneous corvée and surcharges into a single item, collected generally and disbursed equally." Gu's effusive praise emphasizes liberation from physical tax grain transport and intermediary exploitation: "The people need only submit their cash by the deadline and sleep soundly in their beds, free from the disturbance of summons and pursuit."

The work's analysis of the "One Field, Three Lords" (*yitian san zhu*) system illuminates land tenure complexity that persisted despite fiscal reform. Gu documents the split between "great rent lords" (*dazu zhu*) collecting rents without tax obligations, "property lords" (*ye zhu*) holding nominal ownership, and tenant cultivators (*dian hu*) bearing actual tax burdens—a system producing endless litigation and "washing property" (*xi ye*) disputes as rising prices prompted former sellers to demand compensation. His reproduction of Luo Qingxiao's memorial proposing land taxation based on market value rather than grain yields demonstrates contemporary recognition that Minnan's commercialized economy required fundamental institutional innovation beyond Single Whip's administrative simplification.

Gu's coverage of maritime taxation provides essential documentation of Liu Yaohui's 1572 customs establishment and its subsequent evolution. He distinguishes between *shibo* (tribute trade) and *shangbo* (merchant shipping), reproducing Liu's memorial requesting maritime taxes to fund military expenses at 6,000 taels annually with seventeen regulatory articles. Gu documents the three-tier tax structure (water tax by ship size, land tax by cargo value, and Luzon-specific surcharges of 150 taels per ship), noting Wanli 4 (1576) revenues of 10,000 taels rising to over 20,000 by Wanli 11 (1583). His account of the 1603 Manila massacre—where Spanish forces killed 20,000 Fujianese following Zhang Ni's fraudulent memorial about gold deposits—illustrates the catastrophic potential of unrestricted maritime expansion, while his warning about "Red Hair" (*hong mao*) Dutch traders reflects continued anxiety about coastal security.

The work's salt tax analysis reveals fiscal hybridity in practice, documenting the transition from per-capita *yanliang* assessments converted through paper money to silver, merged into Single Whip as *dingliao yin*, alongside licensed merchant monopolies (*yanke*) splitting profits with the state. Gu highlights Zhangzhou's exceptional status: lacking official salt fields, the prefecture relied on small-scale tidal pans taxed by area rather than output, with free trade (*congbian maoyi*) and minimal local levies—a contrast to state-controlled northern Fujian systems prone to merchant collusion and corruption. His reproduction of Fang Huan's protest against Wanli 4 monopoly reorganization captures local resistance to central fiscal extraction, arguing that heavy regulation deprived mountain communities of essential salt while impoverishing coastal producers.

For military history, Gu's documentation of coastal fortification construction from the Jiajing *xinwei* year (1561) onward—citing his own observation that "wall-building began in Zhangzhou after 1561"—establishes the timeline for privatized defense that Zheng Zhenman later analyzed. His accounts of lineage militia (*tuanlian*) replacing official garrisons, and the subsequent intensification of inter-village feuding, illuminate the social transformation accompanying fiscal militarization. The work's preservation of Hu Shining's Jiajing-era warning that reclaiming usurped military colony lands would "alienate the people while falling short of providing for the army" captures the fundamental dilemma facing Minnan administrators: the region's agricultural deficiency made traditional military-agricultural systems impossible, yet commercial alternatives required institutional innovation that threatened established interests.
