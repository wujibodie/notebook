《乾隆福清縣志》(Qianlong Fuqing County Gazetteer)

This eighteenth-century county gazetteer, compiled by the Fuqing County Editorial Committee in 1987, preserves biographical records of Ming-era county magistrates that illuminate fiscal-military reform implementation and local governance challenges in coastal Fujian during the Jiajing-Wanli transition. The biographies document several important patterns: first, the connection between population registration reform and anti-Buddhist campaigns, as seen in Chen Hou's (陳逅) early Jiajing tenure when he "punished deception and increased household registration while destroying licentious temples to purify people's hearts" (罪欺陰而增戶口，毀淫祠而淑入心), demonstrating how the Jiajing "cult temple destruction movement" linked demographic registration to moral reform. Second, the biographies illustrate post-wokou reconstruction efforts, as when Ye Mengxiong (葉夢熊), appointed after the county's devastation by pirates, vigorously revitalized administration, "clarified maritime occupations, verified household registers, and made powerful clans submit" (清海業，核戶版，豪宗斂手) while reconstructing the county seat using public funds without burdening the people.

The magistrate biographies provide crucial evidence for fiscal policy continuity and change across the late Ming. Luo Wancheng (羅萬程), serving in the early Wanli period, was tasked with "equalizing corvée" (均徭役)—a notable assignment given that the Single Whip reforms were supposedly already implemented, suggesting ongoing adjustment of labor service obligations. Wang Silun (汪泗倫), appointed in 1610, authored a lengthy proposal on commuting military granaries to silver (倉糧折色), indicating that grain payments to local garrisons remained partially in-kind even decades after Governor Liu Yaohui's 1570s reforms. Wang Zhengxin (王政新), serving in the late Wanli/Tianqi period, was praised for waiving substantial "fire consumption" fees (火耗數千金), revealing the persistence of informal surcharges despite official reform efforts. These biographies collectively demonstrate how county-level governance in coastal Fujian navigated between central reform mandates and local implementation challenges, with magistrates serving as critical intermediaries in adapting imperial fiscal systems to regional commercial and agricultural realities.
