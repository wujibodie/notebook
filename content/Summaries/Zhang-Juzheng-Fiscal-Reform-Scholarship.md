# Scholarship on Zhang Juzheng and Ming Fiscal Reform

## Wei Qingyuan, *Zhang Juzheng yu Wanli* (张居正与万历)

Wei Qingyuan, *Zhang Juzheng yu Wanli* [Zhang Juzheng and the Wanli Era] (Taipei: Yunlong Chubanshe, 2005).

Wei Qingyuan's comprehensive political biography of Zhang Juzheng provides crucial insights into the Grand Secretary's economic thought and reform implementation. Wei documents Zhang's early exposure to practical governance through his Hanlin-period meetings with salt, customs, and military officials, which shaped his understanding of "population, strategic passes, terrain, and the strength of the people" (*gongfu, hukou, esai, shanchuan xingshi, renmin qiangruo*). A pivotal contribution is Wei's analysis of Zhang's essay "Preface Presented to Zhou Hanpu of the Water Ministry Upon His Return to Court from Tax Collection Duty," written during his Jingzhou retreat, which articulated a proto-mercantilist philosophy that farmers and merchants "mutually support one another" (*xianghu weichi*). Wei argues this essay formed the foundation of Zhang's later reform efforts, demonstrating significantly more developed commercial thinking than contemporaries.

The work meticulously traces the bureaucratic politics of Single Whip implementation, noting that Zhang deliberately withheld his "Six Matters Memorial" (*Chen Liushi Shu*) until after Xu Jie's resignation, suggesting calculated political timing. Wei identifies Gao Gong's 1569 memorial on outdated price valuations for court requisitions as marking the beginning of practical fiscal reform, while emphasizing Zhang's unique role as "most resolute and effective promoter and implementer" (*zui jianjue he youxiao de tuidongzhe he zhixingzhe*) of the Single Whip, even if not its originator. Wei's analysis of the factional struggle between Xu Jie/Li Chunfang's "lenient government" (*kuanzheng*) and Gao Gong/Zhang Juzheng's "governing through names and laws" (*gengcao mingfa*) provides essential political context for understanding why fiscal reform required Zhang's accumulation of "near absolute power" (*jinyu juedui de quanli*) to overcome entrenched opposition from both Xu Jie and Gao Gong.

## Wan Ming, *Mingdai Fuyi* (明代赋役)

Wan Ming, "Mingdai Fuyi" [Ming Dynasty Tax and Corvée], unpublished research on Single Whip chronology and implementation.

Wan Ming's research provides the most detailed chronology of Single Whip reforms, documenting twenty-six county-level officials who participated in implementing the reform between 1534 and the 1560s. Her analysis distinguishes between "county-level dimension" (*xianji weidu*) reforms that created integrated local fiscal systems through silverization, and "provincial-level dimension" (*shengji weidu*) reforms that built broader administrative frameworks on county-level experience. Wan traces the terminology's evolution from Gan Cheng's 1534 "one whip" (*yitiao*) description of simplified lijia levies through various regional experiments—including Gu Tingdui's 1559 Pinghu County reforms, Xie Shiyan's 1562-65 grain levy combination in Wujin, and Zhan Yangbi's 1565 Nanhai County innovation.

Crucially, Wan demonstrates that Pang Shangpeng's Zhejiang reforms emerged from this cumulative county-level experimentation rather than originating the approach, with her signature formulation that "Ming tax-corvée fiscal reform adopted a bottom-up local exploration path" (*caiqu de shi zixia'ershang de difang tansuo*). Her identification of 1562-1565 as the critical period when reforms appeared simultaneously across Zhejiang, Nan Zhili, Guangdong, Jiangxi, and Shandong provides temporal precision for understanding the Single Whip's diffusion. Wan's analytic framework—distinguishing horizontal county-level integration from vertical provincial-level implementation—offers a structural model for comparing Minnan's experience with other regions and illuminates how Zhang Juzheng's "breakthrough central fiscal system reform" (*tujinshi gaige*) built upon accumulated local expertise.

## Deng Zhihua, *Pang Shangpeng yu Shehui Biange* (庞尚鹏与社会变革)

Deng Zhihua, *Pang Shangpeng yu Shehui Biange* [Pang Shangpeng and Social Change], unpublished manuscript on Pang Shangpeng's career and reforms.

Deng Zhihua's research addresses a crucial puzzle in Ming fiscal history: why Zhang Juzheng initially ignored Pang Shangpeng before granting him the dual role of Fujian xunfu and jijunwu in 1576. The work opens with Gu Yanwu's enigmatic observation that "since the Single Whip method was implemented, people first knew the joy of living" (*zi tiaobian zhi fa xing er min shi zhi you sheng zhi le*), framing the investigation around understanding this transformation's human impact. Deng's analysis centers on Pang's 1566 memorial "Reducing Expenses and Establishing Regulations to Reform Lijia" (*Jie rongfei ding fashou yi geng lijia shu*), which sought imperial endorsement to prevent successor officials from overturning his Zhejiang reforms.

The research documents Pang's personal networks during his six-year forced retirement (1570–1576), including his relationship with Li Youzi—a "close friend" (*miyou*) of Zhang Juzheng—which likely facilitated his recall despite earlier conflicts with Xu Jie's faction. Deng traces how Pang's Zhejiang experience informed his Fujian mandate, where he "immediately upon arrival resolutely submitted a memorial to implement [Single Whip]" (*fu xiache, ji yiran shangshu xing zhi*), remitted over 500,000 taels of accumulated military debt, reduced commercial taxes, and cut temple land assessments. The work's examination of Pang's ultimate dismissal for supporting duoqing controversy critics reveals the political fragility of reform implementation, even under Zhang Juzheng's consolidated power. Deng's research is essential for understanding how provincial-level reform operated within court factional politics and why Pang's implementation of Single Whip in Fujian proved more durable than his Zhejiang innovations.

## Liu Guanglin, "Wokou War and the Single Whip Law"

Liu Guanglin, "Wokou War and the Single Whip Law," unpublished paper arguing for an "exogenous" theory of Ming fiscal transformation.

Liu Guanglin's provocative study challenges endogenous economic explanations of Ming fiscal reform, arguing that "state demand is the foundation of institutional innovation" (*guojia xuqiu shi zhidu chuangxin de jichu*), with fiscal needs serving and constrained by military mobilization methods. Liu demonstrates that while Ming officials recognized Hongwu system deficiencies early, they could not abandon them until the Jiajing court reorganized armaments for border crisis response, creating marketized military mobilization and comprehensive fiscal crisis as "direct causes of subsequent in-depth reforms." His quantitative analysis shows Fuzhou's silver tax assessments rose from 2% in the Hongzhi reign to 92% by 1596, arguing this transformation resulted entirely from military professionalization needs rather than commercial evolution.

The work provides detailed documentation of how anti-wokou campaigns drove fiscal innovation: Wang Shou's 1553 proposal to regulate and tax illegal salt trade between Yuegang and Quanzhou to fund mercenary recruitment; Xu Shen's 1554 memorial calling for combined junyao silver payments delivered directly to military camps; and the Songjiang practice of financing fortifications half through labor requisitions and half through silver expenditure. Liu's charts of military expenditures from 1554–1566 Veritable Records demonstrate the fiscal pressure's magnitude. His identification of Pang Shangpeng and Zhou Rudou as naval defense officials who translated military funding frustrations into tax reform advocacy illuminates the personnel networks connecting coastal defense and fiscal innovation. Liu's argument that "without the Jiajing anti-wokou military mobilization, there would not have been widespread Single Whip implementation" (*mei you Jiajing kangwo junshi dongyuan, jiu bu hui you yitiao bianfa de guangfan tuixing*) reframes Minnan's fiscal history as fundamentally shaped by military crisis rather than commercial development.

## Lin Renchuan, "Ming Dynasty Zhangzhou and the Struggle against Tax Inspector Gao Cai"

Lin Renchuan, "Ming Dynasty Zhangzhou and the Struggle against Tax Inspector Gao Cai," unpublished research on Yuegang maritime trade.

Lin Renchuan's work provides essential context for understanding the maritime customs system established under Liu Yaohui and its later perversion under eunuch tax inspectors. Lin documents how Yuegang's complicated topography of islets and inlets made government control difficult, enabling the port's emergence as a major smuggling center from the Xuande reign onward. Citing the Haicheng Xianzhi, he notes that "after Zhengde, Yuegang's powerful families mostly built large ships for foreign ocean trade, and over time enticed pirates to inner rivers, which the law could not stop." His identification of Wanli 21 (1593) as a peak year for Yuegang-Luzon trade—with 24 ships returning from the Philippines in a single voyage—quantifies the maritime commerce's scale that Liu Yaohui's 1572 customs establishment sought to regulate and tax.

Lin's analysis of the long history of struggle against haijin (sea ban) enforcement and imperial taxation provides crucial background for understanding local resistance patterns that shaped Minnan's fiscal-military interface. His documentation of how "the people of Haicheng/Zhangzhou had prepared the ground" for later struggles against Gao Cai through earlier resistance experience illuminates the continuity between Liu Yaohui's measured accommodation and later conflicts. The work's attention to trade volume fluctuations and their connection to official policy—including Xu Fuyuan's Wanli 21 amnesty that brought 24 self-reporting ships—demonstrates how maritime taxation operated within cyclical patterns of prohibition and tolerance that characterized Ming coastal management.

## Zhang Ruiwei, "Enriching the State and Enriching the People"

Zhang Ruiwei, "Enriching the State and Enriching the People" [Zuguo yu Fumin], unpublished paper on Zhang Juzheng's monetary policy.

Zhang Ruiwei's study examines an under-discussed element of Zhang Juzheng's economic policy: the experiment with provincial copper coin smelting intended to provide common currency while reserving silver for government use. Building on von Glahn's analysis, Zhang argues that this policy demonstrates late Ming officials developed fiscal thinking beyond the "conservatism" Huang Renyu attributed to Zhang. The work analyzes Tan Lun's "Treatise on Managing Wealth" (*Licai Shu*) as a key influence, showing Tan's focus on encouraging widespread copper coin use among common people to reduce overall silver dependency—a perspective Tan saw as profitable for state revenue.

Zhang's examination of correspondence between Zhang Juzheng and Pang Shangpeng reveals both thinkers' primary concern with "enriching the state" (*zuguo*), with "enriching the people" (*fumin*) serving as subsidiary means rather than ultimate ends. Neither analyzed silver price instability in depth, instead focusing on covering government expenses through profitable state coinage. The work documents Zhang's departure from Beijing-centralized minting toward Gu Tingwu's proposal for Yunnan provincial mints near copper mines, demonstrating pragmatic adaptation to regional resource distribution. Zhang Ruiwei's analysis challenges interpretations of Ming fiscal reform as either purely conservative or proto-modern, instead revealing sophisticated statecraft aimed at balancing monetary stability with revenue extraction—a tension central to Pang Shangpeng's abortive Fujian coinage experiment.

## Zhu Dongrun, Zhang Juzheng Biographical Research

Zhu Dongrun, research notes on Zhang Juzheng's correspondence and political philosophy.

Zhu Dongrun's research preserves crucial primary source documentation, including Zhang Juzheng's letter to Fujian Governor Geng Chudong that contains a dramatic assessment of mid-Jiajing conditions: "When the Shizong emperor was in mid-reign, merchants held office and wealth flowed upward; the common people cried out in misery with no certainty of their lives—was the scene at that time different from the late ages of Han and Tang?" This quotation captures the perceived crisis of governance that reformers sought to address, framing Zhang Juzheng's later policies as responses to systemic collapse rather than mere fiscal optimization. Zhu's documentation of Zhang's early education in practical administration, through meetings with salt, customs, and military officials during his Hanlin period, provides biographical context for understanding how Zhang developed his distinctive combination of classical learning and administrative expertise.
