Zhang Juzheng 張居正. "Correspondence with Pang Shangpeng" [与应天庞巡抚, 答閩撫龐惺庵, etc.]. In *Zhang Juzheng Ji* 張居正集 or collected writings. Late 16th century. Translated and annotated in research notes.

This collection comprises several letters exchanged between Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng and Pang Shangpeng during the latter's tenure as Fujian Provincial Governor (Xunfu) from 1573-1574. The correspondence reveals the central government's perspective on fiscal reform implementation in Fujian, particularly regarding the Single Whip (一条鞭) method of taxation. Zhang expresses strong support for Pang's memorials on fiscal reform, particularly appreciating how the new system would enable better central account-keeping of silver revenues. The letters disclose Zhang's discovery of insufficient silver to cover border expenses and his inability to extract more from the populace, providing crucial context for understanding why fiscal reform was deemed necessary. Zhang positions Pang's appointment as an opportunity for rehabilitation after years of "misfortune," warning that Fujian is a "perilous position" but offering a chance to clear his name through successful reform implementation. The correspondence also addresses monetary policy, with Zhang supporting Pang's proposals to make copper more expensive relative to silver, thereby increasing the value of currency used in ordinary transactions and lessening the burden of silver taxes. These letters are essential primary sources for understanding how the Ming central government viewed Fujian's fiscal situation and the political dynamics between Beijing and provincial officials during the critical early Wanli period.

The letters demonstrate Zhang Juzheng's reform network in action, showing how he positioned trusted allies in key provincial posts to implement his fiscal agenda. Zhang's comparison of himself to Zichan (the famous Spring and Autumn period reformer) reveals his self-consciousness about the harshness of initial reform measures while maintaining that the people's welfare remained the ultimate standard. The correspondence is particularly valuable for understanding the gap between central fiscal needs and provincial capabilities, as Zhang acknowledges both the need for silver and the impossibility of further extraction from an already burdened populace.
