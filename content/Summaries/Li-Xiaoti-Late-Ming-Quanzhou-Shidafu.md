Li Xiaoti 李孝悌. "Wan Ming Quanzhou Shidafu de Shenghuo yu Qingqu" [The Life and Sentiments of Late Ming Quanzhou Literati]. In *Zhongguo de Chengshi Shenghuo* [Urban Life in China], edited by Li Xiaoti, 1-47. Beijing: Xinxing Chubanshe, 2006.

This chapter examines the cultural and social transformations among Quanzhou's literati class during the late Ming period, focusing on four key figures: Li Zhi, Lin Xiyuan, He Qiaoyuan, and Li Guangjin. Li Xiaoti documents how the rise of domestic and maritime trade generated new forms of urban ostentation, with coastal markets like Shihu and Anping developing commercial cultures that contrasted sharply with the moral purity attributed to mountain villages. The study pays particular attention to how the wokou crisis shaped Quanzhou's conservative Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy, which differed markedly from the politically engaged Confucianism of Jiangnan literati. Through careful reading of local gazetteers, collected works, and temple records, Li reconstructs the economic realities of gentry life, including land transfers, temple confiscations, and the ambiguous relationship between literati culture and maritime commerce.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal and military institutions, this work provides essential social context for understanding the constraints facing reformers. Li's analysis of Lin Xiyuan's advocacy for mobilizing "salt and fishing households" against the wokou, and his proposals to repurpose merchant vessels as warships with silver-funded retrofits, illuminates the practical fiscal-military innovations necessitated by coastal crisis. The documentation of He Qiaoyuan's correspondence with Zheng Zhilong and his arguments for opening maritime trade reveals how local elites navigated between commercial interests and state security concerns. Particularly valuable is the examination of how the 1603 Manila massacre affected Anping merchant families, demonstrating the human costs of Fujian's integration into global silver flows and the limits of state protection for overseas traders. Li's attention to the tension between Confucian moral discourse and commercial reality helps explain why fiscal reforms in Minnan required careful legitimation through appeals to both state interest and local welfare.
