# Pang Shangpeng 龐尚鵬 (1524-1580)

**Citation:** Pang Shangpeng 龐尚鵬. *Junzheng Shiyi 軍政事宜* [Matters Concerning Military Administration]. Written during Pang's tenure as Fujian Provincial Governor. Modern editions and excerpts available in Ming-Qing military treatise collections and digital archives.

**Summary:**

Pang Shangpeng's *Junzheng Shiyi* represents a systematic effort to reform Fujian's military administration during the mid-Jiajing through Longqing periods, articulating principles of "regulation and economization" (*jiezhi*) that would influence subsequent reformers including Liu Yaohui. The treatise addresses the practical challenges of maintaining effective coastal defense forces in a fiscally constrained environment, emphasizing clear hierarchy, mutual responsibility, and rigorous training as antidotes to the indiscipline and inefficiency that plagued Ming military establishments. Pang's core principle—that military forces must be organized so that "controlling many is like controlling few" (*zhi zhong ru zhi gua*) and that troops must maintain order even in the chaos of battle—reflects his synthesis of classical military thought (particularly Guan Zhong's "simple formations") with practical experience against wokou raiders. The work provides detailed regulations for verifying garrison rolls (*shishi*), selecting and training replacement soldiers, and establishing clear chains of command with harsh penalties for officers whose units flee from combat.

The *Junzheng Shiyi* is particularly valuable for understanding the institutional foundations of the fiscal-military reforms implemented during Liu Yaohui's tenure. Pang's emphasis on recruiting reliable men under age fifty, his preference for Guan Zhong's simple formations over Zhuge Liang's complex deployments, and his advocacy for training with firearms (*huoqi*) including the newly introduced Portuguese-style (*Folangji*) artillery all anticipate measures Liu would later implement. Pang's discussion of the four elements of military training—disciplining the mind (*lian xinzhi*), mastering skills (*lian jiyi*), strengthening the body (*lian jingu*), and practicing in difficult terrain (*lian xianyi*)—provides insight into how Ming reformers conceptualized military effectiveness. His observations on wokou tactics, including their use of Chinese collaborators (*hanren*) for intelligence and their ability to blend into local populations by wearing Chinese clothing, reveal the challenges of coastal defense in a region where pirates and merchants were often indistinguishable. As a precursor to Liu Yaohui's more celebrated reforms, Pang's treatise demonstrates the cumulative, iterative nature of Ming fiscal-military adaptation in response to the pressures of maritime commerce and coastal insecurity.
