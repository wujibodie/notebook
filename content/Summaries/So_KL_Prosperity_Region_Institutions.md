# So, K. L. (Su, Jilang 苏基朗)

**Citation:** So, K. L. *Prosperity, Region, and Institutions in Maritime China: The South Fukien Pattern, 946-1368*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Asia Center, 2000.

**Summary:**

K.L. So's magisterial study examines the economic transformation of southern Fujian (Minnan) from the Tang through the Southern Song dynasty, establishing the region's exceptional maritime-commercial development that fundamentally shaped its later Ming-era characteristics. The work argues that institutional factors—notably the 1087 establishment of a Maritime Trade Supervisorate (shibosi) in Quanzhou—catalyzed the region's evolution from a relatively marginal backwater to one of the Song dynasty's most dynamic commercial centers. So demonstrates that Minnan's prosperity rested on an unusual combination of intensive agricultural development (including early adoption of Champa rice double-cropping), large-scale export-oriented ceramic manufacture, and the sophisticated management of maritime commerce through networks of sojourning merchants who had developed extensive commercial relationships along the China coast prior to the institutional opening of Quanzhou itself.

The study is particularly valuable for understanding the long-term structural foundations of Minnan's 16th-century fiscal exceptionalism. So documents how Southern Song Minnan had already developed an unusual social formation characterized by the interpenetration of merchant wealth, lineage organization, and state institutions—a pattern that would persist and intensify under the Ming. His analysis of the ceramics industry (employing perhaps 65,000 people, or 6.5% of the prefectural population), the dominance of Buddhist temple landholding in the Northern Song, and the unique "twin cities" urban structure of Quanzhou-Nan'an all provide essential background for understanding why this region would later prove so resistant to the Ming fiscal system's agrarian assumptions. So's emphasis on regional variation within empire-wide institutional frameworks anticipates and supports the dissertation's argument about Minnan as a "laboratory for fiscal reform."
