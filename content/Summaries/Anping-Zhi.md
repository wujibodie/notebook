《安平志》(Anping Town Gazetteer)

The Anping Town Gazetteer documents the history of Anhai (安海), a major commercial port on the Quanzhou coast, preserving records from both Ming and Qing editions that illuminate the transformation of this maritime commercial center through the Ming-Qing transition. The Wanli era *Huji Zhi* (Household Registration section) records Anhai's administrative structure within Jinjiang County's 8th Ward, comprising 4 *tu* (圖) of 40 *jia* (甲) total, each *jia* theoretically comprising ten households but varying considerably in practice. A Kangxi 31 (1692) appended note explains that coastal evacuation during the early Qing coastal ban (遷界) threw the registers into disarray; when residents were allowed to return, the old tax levies—resumed in the 6th year after a tax reprieve—proved too burdensome for their reduced numbers and wealth. Consequently, in Kangxi 30, Quanzhou Prefect Gao and Jinjiang County Magistrate Li conducted a land resurvey (*qingzhang* 清丈) that presumably lowered tax quotas, finally allowing locals to "know the pleasures of family life, without the calamity of losing their homes." This note by Chen Mengbi (陳夢弼) documents how post-conquest fiscal reconstruction required readjusting Ming-era quotas to match reduced Qing-era population and commercial activity.

The *Gongshu Zhi* (Government Offices section) traces Anhai's institutional evolution from the Jiajing defense crisis through the Wanli commercial boom. In Jiajing's *Jiwei* year (1559?), an office for visiting officials (公館) was constructed beside the Shijing Academy (石井書院), reflecting either military needs or commercial growth. This office became a full yamen in Wanli 35 (1607), handling legal cases even from neighboring prefectures, following a Wanli 34 (1606) establishment of a *zhuzhen* (駐鎮, military/intendancy office). Two stone stelae preserved from the Ming garrison office site record the push for urban incorporation: Huang Ruliang's (黃汝良) inscription explains that an office became necessary to oversee problems arising from Anhai's growth through maritime trade after the sea ban was lifted. Local gentry and elders requested establishment of either a county (*yi* 邑) or a military town (*zhen* 鎮) at the site of the Jiajing-era fort, with County Magistrate Gu Shiqi (顧士琦) petitioning the throne to make Anhai a county following Haicheng's precedent. Huang Fengxiang's inscription celebrated Anping as a "great town" (巨鎮) comparable in culture to a prefectural city, whose "riches collected from the ocean trade are second to none."

The *Renwu Zhi* (Biographies) and later sections document Anhai's commercial diaspora and the devastating impact of the 1603 Manila massacre. The 1987 editors added merchant biographies absent from original Ming editions, including accounts of women widowed by the massacre. Chen Yingniang (陳英娘) lost her husband and brother in the 1603 killings, with a footnote quoting Li Guangjin's *Jingbi Ji* (景璧集) that "not a single person from Anping escaped" (安平無一人得脫). Another account documents the wife of Chen Xianzhang, who committed suicide after her husband, son, brother-in-law, and four servants were killed in the massacre—described as occurring in Wanli's *Guimao* year (1603) when "the beasts, treacherous and without constant nature, killed over thirty thousand Chinese in their country without cause." Juan 10 contains a genealogy-based list of overseas Anhai Chinese, identifying eighteen killed in the 1603 massacre and four in the 1639 "barbarian disturbance" (夷變), with most bearing the common surnames Huang, Chen, and Yan. These passages demonstrate how Qing-era gazetteers preserved and Qing-era editors expanded documentation of Ming maritime commercial activity and its human costs, providing crucial evidence for the social history of Minnan's maritime expansion.
