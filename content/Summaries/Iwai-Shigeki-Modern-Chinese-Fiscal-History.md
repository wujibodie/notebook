Iwai Shigeki. *Chūgoku Kindai Zaiseishi Kenkyū* [Studies in Modern Chinese Fiscal History]. Kyoto: Kyoto University Press, 2004.

Iwai Shigeki's seminal work on Ming-Qing fiscal history examines the fundamental tension between "fixed quotas" (定額) and "floating assessments" (浮估) in Chinese tax policy from the fourteenth through nineteenth centuries. The study challenges purely exploitative interpretations of state taxation by emphasizing the state's role in organizing public social services and constituting society through fiscal mechanisms. Iwai's core research question asks what mutual relationship existed between long-term fiscal structures and socio-economic activities, employing extensive archival research across Ming and Qing central and local records. The book's conceptual framework centers on how the theoretical commitment to fixed quotas in imperial fiscal ideology made reliance on flexible, floating levies practically inevitable, creating endemic contradictions within the system.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal reform, Iwai's work provides crucial theoretical grounding for understanding the structural pressures facing reformers like Pang Shangpeng and Liu Yaohui. His analysis of how the lijia system's original design—never intended to bear fiscal extraction functions—created inevitable friction when revenue collection imperatives were later imposed, illuminates the broader context of Fujian's fiscal crisis. Iwai's insight that the Ming fiscal system became "enormously convoluted and opaque" due to the internal contradictions between founding principles and practical exigencies helps explain why Fujian's exceptional commercialized economy required iterative local experimentation rather than top-down policy implementation. The book's emphasis on viewing fiscal institutions as constitutive of social relationships rather than merely extractive mechanisms offers a valuable corrective to narratives that treat Minnan's maritime commercialization solely as resistance to state predation.
