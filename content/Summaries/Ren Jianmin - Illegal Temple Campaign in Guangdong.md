**Author:** Jianmin Ren
**Title:** The Destruction of Illegal Temples and Temple Land Disposition in Guangdong in Mid-Ming China

This academic article investigates the destruction of illegal temples (淫祠) and Buddhist/Daoist temples (寺觀) in mid-Ming Guangdong, focusing on the disposition and changing nature of temple land (寺田) and its associated tax and corvee policies. Challenging existing scholarship that primarily examines these campaigns through the lens of ritual reform and the rise of clans, the author argues that the underlying dynamic was a significant conflict of interest between the local government and powerful gentry families over land resources.

The study reveals that following Wei Xiao's campaign in the early Jiajing reign, while large temples in the Pearl River Delta diminished, the released land did not simply revert to ordinary civilian land. Instead, it retained a distinct "temple land" status, subject to different and often heavier tax and corvee obligations (糧差). Crucially, the government maintained considerable control over this land, even after its sale. Buyers, despite initial purchases, could be compelled to pay additional fees through a policy of "increasing price for resale" (增價發賣) under the pretexts of funding military supplies (軍餉) or major construction projects (大工). The Jiajing period is identified as a critical juncture for this transformation, with Wei Xiao's actions serving as a starting point for subsequent disputes over ownership and the redefinition of tax policies. The article draws on local gazetteers, such as the Chenghua and Jiajing editions of the *Guangzhou Zhi*, and personal writings of key figures like Huo Tao, to illustrate how the gentry class and emerging clans not only marginalized Buddhist influence but also absorbed vast temple assets into their own property holdings, fundamentally altering the region's socioeconomic landscape.
