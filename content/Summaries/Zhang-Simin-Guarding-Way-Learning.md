Zhang Simin 張斯珉. *Weidao zhi Xue: Geng Dingxiang Sixiang Yanjiu* [The Learning of Guarding the Way: Research on Geng Dingxiang's Thought]. Beijing: Renmin Chubanshe, 2019.

Zhang Simin's intellectual biography of Geng Dingxiang (1524-1596), a prominent Ming official who served as Grand Coordinator of Fujian from 1579-1582, examines his role as both fiscal reformer and conservative Cheng-Zhu philosopher. The study draws heavily on a *Xingzhuang* (biographical account) composed by Jiao Hong, Geng's most notable pupil, to document his early career and intellectual formation. Zhang's analysis of Geng's Fujian tenure focuses on his attempt to find a middle ground between the policies of his predecessors—Liu Yaohui, who had increased mercantile levies to meet fiscal crises, and Pang Shangpeng, who subsequently cancelled some of these commercial taxes. While Zhang cannot confirm Geng's success in this balancing act, he suggests that Geng's own retrospective writings indicate satisfaction with his performance. The book documents Geng's enthusiastic response to Zhang Juzheng's 1579 order for land resurveys (*celiang tianmu*), describing how Geng overcame Fujian's challenging topography of numerous hills and fragmented farmland to produce the "Guangyutu Shi" (Trial Edition of the Broad Territorial Map), conducting surveys on a prefectural basis. Simultaneously, Geng actively promoted the Single Whip reforms (*yitiaobian fa*), consolidating multiple taxes and corvées into unified silver payments, implemented baojia security systems, and established community compacts (*xiangyue*).

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal reform, Zhang's work provides crucial insight into the post-crisis normalization of reform institutions. His documentation of Geng's famine relief efforts and fiscal planning that "allowed large numbers of Fujian's people to avoid starvation" illuminates the continuing fiscal pressures facing the province even after the immediate wokou crisis had passed. Geng's compilation of his Fujian administrative experience into the *Mushi Moyi* (Discussions on the Conclusions of Governance) establishes an important but understudied primary source for late Ming provincial administration. The study's attention to Geng's position as a Cheng-Zhu orthodox thinker—famously involved in anti-Buddhist polemics with Li Zhi—provides essential context for understanding the ideological constraints on fiscal innovation, even as Geng implemented practical reforms like silverization and land surveys. Zhang's characterization of Geng's challenge as finding reform solutions "without increasing the burden on the populace" captures the central dilemma facing Fujian officials: maintaining Hongwu-era ideals of light taxation while responding to the fiscal-military demands of a commercialized coastal province. For understanding how Zhang Juzheng's reform network operated at the provincial level, Geng's Fujian tenure represents a crucial case of central-local coordination in the implementation of empire-wide fiscal transformation.
