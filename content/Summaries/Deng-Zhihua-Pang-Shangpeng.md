# Deng Zhihua on Pang Shangpeng

## Deng Zhihua, *Fengjiang Dali yu Shehui Biange*: Pang Shangpeng Chapter (封疆大吏与社会变革)

Deng Zhihua, "Pang Shangpeng" chapter in *Fengjiang Dali yu Shehui Biange* [Frontier Governors and Social Change], unpublished manuscript.

Deng Zhihua's biographical study of Pang Shangpeng provides the most detailed reconstruction of the reformer's career trajectory and institutional innovations. The work opens with Pang's 1524 birth into a Nanhai family of "fairly educated wood merchants" (*xiangdang jiaoshu de mu shang*), tracing his education in Shunde under the Jiangxi-born teacher Chen Yong and his 1553 jinshi examination success at age 29—placing 239th in the third rank with Xu Jie and Zhang Juzheng as examiners. Deng documents the establishment of "teacher-student ties" (*shisheng zhi yi*) between Pang and the younger Zhang, connections that would prove decisive for Pang's 1576 recall after six years of forced retirement following his 1570 dismissal.

The study's core contribution is its analysis of Pang's Zhejiang reforms as cumulative syntheses of county-level experimentation rather than original innovations. Deng traces the reform genealogy from Huzhou's 1541 guantian-mintian unification through Jiaxing's 1547 elimination of tax rate differentials, identifying the crucial transition from county magistrate (*zhixian*) and prefect (*zhifu*) initiatives to broader xunan and xunfu implementation. His detailed examination of the 十段锦法 (Ten-Section Brocade Method) reveals how Pang's 1565 reform—assessing supplementary military expenses across ten jia units with provision for inter-jia compensation when one unit fell short—built upon Liang Fangzhong's observations of post-1552 Zhejiang-Fujian experiments. Deng quotes Pang's own memorial acknowledging that his system failed to prevent guiji (false registration) malpractice: "Since the wokou troubles began, because one or two salt fields reported depleted stove-households, those with land were permitted to count fifty mu as equivalent to one ding for salt tax purposes... this is why the malpractice of false registration daily deepens."

For understanding Single Whip's implementation mechanics, Deng's summary of the Kangxi-era Shaoxing gazetteer's reform description is invaluable: annual advance accounting of expected expenses, decennial resurveys adjusting ding assessments (with land conversion to ding-equivalents), and public posting of exact labor service requirements. His five-point analysis of Pang's reform benefits—preventing levy inequalities, enabling advance tax burden knowledge, limiting exemption abuses, eliminating corvée bribery opportunities, and removing travel requirements for labor service—clarifies why the system gained popular acceptance despite elite resistance. The work's examination of ideological opposition, including Ge Shouli's 戶部尚書 memorial arguing Single Whip would "suppress the root and benefit the branch" (*yi ben li mo*) by favoring merchants over farmers, illuminates the conceptual barriers reformers faced.

Deng's analysis of Pang's 1576–1578 Fujian tenure emphasizes continuity with his Zhejiang methods while noting distinctive features: remitting 500,000+ taels of accumulated military debt, reducing commercial taxes, cutting temple land assessments, and immediately implementing Single Whip "upon dismounting from his carriage" (*fu xiache*). The work's documentation of popular response—Fujian people initially "feared his reputation" (*dan qi fengcai*) but "blocked the road weeping" (*zhedao ku song*) at his departure—provides rare evidence of reform's social reception. Deng's investigation of Pang's ultimate dismissal for supporting duoqing controversy critics, despite Zhang Juzheng's earlier patronage, reveals the political fragility of even successful provincial reform within court factional dynamics.
