Kuroda Akinobu 黑田明伸. "Zhongguo Huobishi shang de Yongyin Zhuanbian: Qiepian, Chenzhong, Ruzhang de Baiyin" (中国货币史上的用银转变：切片、称重、入账的白银) [Transformation of Silver Usage in Chinese Monetary History: Silver Cut, Weighed, and Booked]. *Zhongguo Jingjishi Yanjiu* (中国经济史研究), no. 1 (2020): 17-26.

Kuroda Akinobu's article provides a synthesis of China's distinctive monetary evolution, tracing how uncoined silver by weight (銀兩) emerged from supplementary status to silk, replaced Yuan paper notes, and eventually formed a complementary dual structure with copper coins. For 16th-century Minnan fiscal history, Kuroda's framework explains why silverization proceeded so rapidly in maritime Fujian and how the region's monetary exceptionalism enabled Single Whip reforms.

## Silver as Supplement to Silk: Pre-Ming Foundations

Kuroda establishes that before becoming the dominant currency, silver served merely as a supplementary instrument to silk (絹帛) for long-distance trade settlement (p. 17). From the Han through Tang dynasties, silk functioned as the primary interregional settlement medium, particularly for horse trade with nomadic peoples. Silver only gradually assumed silk's monetary role during the Five Dynasties and Song periods.

This evolutionary trajectory is crucial for understanding Minnan's position. The Southern Song period (12th-13th centuries) saw Quanzhou emerge as a major maritime commercial center precisely when silver began supplementing silk in domestic long-distance trade (p. 18). Kuroda notes that 11th-century silver ingot production increased alongside copper coin casting, but "silver-settled transactions remained rare throughout the Song" (p. 18, citing Wang Wencheng). This suggests that Minnan's later 16th-century silver dominance represented not continuity with Song commercial practices but a fundamental transformation in monetary regime.

## Yuan Paper Notes and Silver-by-Weight Calculation

The Mongol Yuan dynasty (1271-1368) established a critical precedent for Ming fiscal practice: issuing state paper money with face values denominated in copper coin units but calculated in silver by weight (p. 18). The Yuan prohibited private silver circulation from 1276-1311, yet "government transfers and private trade moved silver ingots along Eurasian caravan routes" (p. 18). Kuroda argues that after the Yuan collapse, substantial silver remained in China proper, providing the monetary foundation for Ming silverization.

The Yuan's system of "calculating paper notes by silver weight" (按銀重計價) without actually circulating silver created the conceptual framework that Ming fiscal reformers would adopt. When Fujian officials implemented Single Whip reforms in the 1570s, they drew upon this inherited tradition of silver-denominated taxation without silver coinage—a system that minimized state minting costs while accommodating market realities (p. 18).

## Ming Silverization: From Paper Notes to Weighed Silver

Kuroda provides critical periodization for Ming monetary transformation. The Ming initially prohibited private silver use (1375), but the 1436 "Silver Rate Reform" (銀率改革) allowed tax payment in weighed silver rather than paper notes—a concession that "proved irreversible" (p. 19). By 1508, official salaries were 90% silver and only 10% copper coins, signaling the state's de facto abandonment of paper currency.

For Minnan fiscal history, the documentary evidence from land contracts is decisive. Kuroda analyzes Huizhou land deeds showing that while early Ming transactions used paper notes, from the mid-1430s all contracts specified weighed silver payment (p. 19). Critically, he cites evidence from Nanping, Fujian: three land deeds from 1401-1431 used paper notes, while five deeds from 1501-1638 specified weighed silver (p. 19, citing *Fujian Huobi Shilüe*). This 15th-century transition precisely coincides with the Chenghua-Hongzhi period (1465-1505) when Fu Yiling documented Fujian merchants expanding into overseas trade.

Kuroda's analysis thus establishes that Minnan's silver economy emerged not merely from 16th-century maritime trade influx, but from earlier Ming institutional transitions that prepared the ground for rapid silverization once Japanese and American silver became available.

## The "Silver Century" (1540-1640) and Local Adaptation

Kuroda's most important contribution for this dissertation is his analysis of how the 16th-century "silver century" transformed local exchange practices. By 1546, Japanese tribute missions recorded that goods at Dinghai were priced by silver weight—a dramatic shift from 1468 records showing copper coin pricing (p. 20, citing von Glahn). Local gazetteers from coastal Fujian and Zhejiang noted that households "previously had never seen silver, now every family owned scissors for cutting silver flakes and scales for weighing" (p. 20).

This granular transformation illuminates how Liu Yaohui's 1572 maritime customs establishment could successfully levy taxes in silver: the necessary monetary infrastructure—scales, expertise in assaying, and acceptance of silver fragments for small transactions—had already diffused through Minnan's market economy. Kuroda notes that by the early 17th century, even vegetable purchases in Huizhou were conducted with weighed silver flakes (p. 20)—suggesting that Fujian's maritime silverization represented an accelerated version of empire-wide trends.

## Complementary Structure: Copper Coins and Silver Ingots

Kuroda challenges linear narratives of monetary evolution by demonstrating that 18th-century China developed a stable "complementary structure" (相輔相成) between copper coins for local transactions and silver for interregional trade (p. 20). This dual system insulated local economies from fluctuations in distant commerce: when silver appreciated due to outflows in the 1820s-1850s, grain prices in copper coins remained stable (p. 21, Figure 1).

For understanding Ming reforms, this complementarity is crucial. The Single Whip reforms did not eliminate copper coinage from local Fujian markets; rather, they established silver as the medium for tax settlement and large transactions while copper continued circulating for daily exchange. Kuroda's framework explains why Fujian's agricultural deficit combined with commercial prosperity created ideal conditions for silverization: the region needed a lightweight, high-value medium for interregional trade in food imports, precisely what weighed silver provided.

## Imaginary Silver Units and Book Transfers

Kuroda's analysis of "imaginary silver units" (虛銀兩) reveals how Chinese merchants developed sophisticated credit mechanisms independent of state banking (p. 21-22). These "booked" silver units functioned purely as accounting denominations for offsetting transactions within merchant networks—enabling complex multi-lateral settlements without physical silver transfer.

For Minnan's maritime trade, this institutional flexibility was essential. The Quanzhou and Zhangzhou merchant networks that Liu Yaohui sought to tax operated through precisely these credit mechanisms. Kuroda's example of the Tongtaihao account books from 1821-1848 shows how merchant house silver receipts and payments were denominated in different copper-silver exchange rates even for the same ingots, reflecting the temporal dynamics of currency circuits (p. 22). This explains why maritime customs establishment required not merely physical inspection but integration into existing commercial accounting practices.

## Currency Circuits and Regional Variation

Kuroda's concept of "currency circuits" (通貨回路)—where specific commodities became linked to specific currencies— illuminates Fujian's distinctive monetary geography (p. 24). He notes that Japanese silver dollars flowed from Fujian's coast into Jiangxi and then to Shanghai, while Jingdezhen porcelain moved in reverse. This circuit-specificity meant that "even as copper depreciated against silver due to decreasing silver supply, agricultural prices in copper coin remained stable" (p. 23)—local markets were partially insulated from interregional fluctuations.

For the dissertation's argument about Minnan's fiscal exceptionalism, Kuroda's framework suggests that the region's success in implementing silver-based reforms derived not merely from silver availability, but from pre-existing mercantile institutions capable of managing currency multiplicity. When Pang Shangpeng attempted coinage reform in Fujian in the 1570s-1580s, he confronted these entrenched circuit structures; his failure confirms Kuroda's argument that "complementarity among monies" (貨幣之間的互補性) reflected deeper social organization of exchange that state policy could not easily override (p. 24).

## Conclusion: Monetary Pluralism as Historical Norm

Kuroda concludes by challenging "the myth of evolution toward perfect money" (完善貨幣的進化神話), arguing that monetary pluralism represented a rational adaptation to diverse exchange needs (p. 26). The unfixed relationship between silver and copper, and the use of imaginary silver units for bookkeeping, "brought stability to local economies against turbulent interregional trade" (p. 26).

For 16th-century Minnan, this conclusion reframes the Single Whip reforms not as proto-modern rationalization, but as sophisticated accommodation of monetary diversity. Liu Yaohui's maritime customs and Pang Shangpeng's tax reforms succeeded precisely because they operated within—rather than against—the plural monetary structures that Kuroda documents. The dissertation's characterization of Minnan as a "laboratory for fiscal reform" thus reflects not the imposition of uniform standards, but the region's exceptional capacity to harness monetary pluralism for state revenue extraction.

## Critical Assessment

Kuroda's magisterial synthesis provides essential theoretical grounding for understanding Minnan's fiscal transformation. However, his focus on long-term structural patterns necessarily underplays regional variation and political contingency. The 1574-1575 Lin Feng crisis, which catalyzed Liu Yaohui's reforms, appears in Kuroda's framework merely as one instance of "turbulent interregional trade" rather than a specific conjuncture linking military crisis to fiscal innovation. Future research might integrate Kuroda's monetary analysis with the political history of Fujian's coastal defense reforms to explain why silverization proceeded more rapidly and comprehensively in Minnan than in other commercialized regions like Jiangnan.
