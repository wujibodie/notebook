《順治潮州府志》(Shunde Chaozhou Prefectural Gazetteer)

The Shunde-era (Qing dynasty) Chaozhou Prefectural Gazetteer preserves crucial documentation of the Lin Feng (林鳳) crisis of 1574-1575, providing the most detailed provincial perspective on this multinational maritime conflict and its connection to earlier pirate networks. The gazetteer's banditry section contextualizes Lin Feng within a continuous maritime-piratical network extending from the 1550s, tracing the lineage from Xu Dong (許棟), the foundational pirate leader of the 1540s-1550s, through Wu Ping (吳平) who inherited Xu's forces and became the preeminent pirate chief of the 1560s, to Lin Guoxian (林國憲)—a Raoping bandit who "colluded with Japanese pirates" and whose "nephews/grandnephews Lin Feng and Lin Dawu were both sons-in-law of Wu Ping." This genealogical reconstruction establishes direct familial and political bonds between the 1560s pirate confederation and the 1570s Manila expedition, demonstrating that Lin Feng's 1574 attack on the Philippines represented continuity with rather than departure from earlier wokou-era networks.

The gazetteer documents the 1574 Lin Feng expedition and its aftermath with specific details about Guangdong-Fujian coordination: Lin Feng "suddenly approached the walls" of Chenghai County, where County Magistrate Zuo Chengfang (左承芳) led defense efforts, driving Lin toward Guangzhou and then to Luzon (呂宋國武瑣港). There Lin resupplied weapons and repaired warships, intending to "coerce the natives and map routes for further inland incursions." Governor Liu Yaohui (劉堯誨) arranged for weapon shipments to Luzon, where the Spanish ruler "burned the ships and the pirates suffered great defeat." When Lin Feng subsequently invaded again, Regional Commander Zhang Yuanxun (張元勳) and Surveillance Commissioner Zhao Kehuai (趙可懷) pursued him to Jieshi and then to Danshui Ocean, burning over twenty vessels. The biography of Zuo Chengfang reveals the challenges of establishing county administration in newly pacified coastal territories: as the first magistrate to actually reside in Chenghai (previous magistrates had lived in adjacent Longcheng), Zuo "brought his wife and children to the county office to demonstrate firm defense," led street construction, opened wasteland, determined *li* service assessments without private gain, and was dismissed with minimal possessions despite later impeachment. This account illuminates how Qing gazetteers preserved Ming-era documentation of the maritime crisis and the institutional responses it provoked, including the establishment of new county-level administration in coastal Guangdong.
