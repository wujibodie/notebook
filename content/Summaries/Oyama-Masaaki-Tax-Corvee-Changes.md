Oyama Masaaki 小山正明. "Fu, Yi Zhidu de Biange" [Changes in Tax and Corvée Systems]. In *Nihon Gakusha* [Japanese Scholars on Ming-Qing Fiscal and Labor Service Institutions], edited by [collective volume], 1992.

Oyama Masaaki's chapter defines the Ming-Qing fiscal system as a transitional phase between the Tang-Song two-tax system and the post-revolutionary system, with the Single Whip reforms inaugurating the fiscal structure that became the target of land reform under the People's Republic. The study begins its narrative of silverization with Zhou Chen's reforms in Jiangnan, which attempted to address the loss of tax revenues from government fields (*guantian*). Oyama's analysis of the "Ten-Section Method" (*Shiduan fa*) identifies its core innovation as levying fixed silver fees on adult males (*ding*) and land (*tian*) registered in the *huangce*, marking the first reform that abandoned the household (*hu*) as the basic unit of account—a significant departure reflecting the chaos in household registration systems. This method combined both "silver corvée" (*yin chai*) and "labor corvée" (*li chai*) into single payments, clearly prefiguring the Single Whip unification. Oyama emphasizes that reforming administrators developed the Ten-Section Method to circumvent the massive extension of tax-exempt status to degree-holders and the practice of splitting households to minimize corvée obligations.

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal reform, Oyama's work provides crucial insight into the institutional pressures that shaped Fujian's reform trajectory. His dating of the earliest Single Whip mention to a 1530 memorial by Gui E—describing debates within the Ministry of Revenue—establishes that the principles of aggregating adult males and land taxes, subtracting exemptions, and dividing to find total silver levies were under discussion decades before Pang Shangpeng's reforms. Oyama's observation that the Single Whip differed from the Ten-Section Method in collating levies at the prefectural or provincial level and collecting annually rather than decennially illuminates the scalar challenges Fujian reformers faced in implementing empire-wide principles locally. The chapter's emphasis on working around gentry tax privileges as a running theme through all fiscal reforms from the Ten-Section Method onward helps explain the specific resistance Fujian coastal elites mounted against Pang Shangpeng's equalization measures. Oyama's conclusion—that removing tax collection to the county level empowered clerks (*xuli*) closely connected to gentry networks—offers a structural explanation for why Fujian's reforms, even when nominally implemented, often failed to achieve their redistributive goals.
