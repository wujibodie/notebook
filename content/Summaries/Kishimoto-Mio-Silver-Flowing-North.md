Kishimoto Mio 岸本美緒. "Wan Ming de Baiyin Beiliu Wenti" (晚明的白银北流問題) [The Problem of Silver Flowing North in Late Ming]. *Zhongguo Jingjishi Yanjiu* (中国经济史研究), no. 1 (2020): 5-15.

Kishimoto Mio's article examines the intellectual history of debates surrounding the "flowing north" (北流) of silver to finance northern frontier defense during the late Ming period. The study illuminates how 16th-17th century officials and literati conceptualized the fiscal crisis precipitated by massive silver transfers to the nine northern military garrisons (九邊), offering crucial context for understanding how contemporaries understood Minnan's fiscal exceptionalism.

## Silver Flowing North as Political Discourse

The article documents how "silver flowing north, going without return" (白銀北流，往而不返) became a commonplace expression of fiscal anxiety during the late Ming (p. 5). Kishimoto traces this discourse to the Longqing peace agreement (隆慶和議) of 1571, when the Ming court established trade relations (款市) with the Mongol leader Altan Khan. Critics of this policy, such as Yang Jisheng (楊繼盛) in 1550, warned that "the barbarians' horses are limited, but China's wealth is finite" (胡虜之產馬有窮，中國之生財有限), predicting that within four to five years, millions of taels would be exhausted (p. 6).

The 1574-1575 Lin Feng crisis in Fujian and Guangdong occurred precisely as these debates intensified. Kishimoto notes that while Fujian Governor Liu Yaohui (劉堯誨) was establishing maritime customs at Haicheng to capture silver from overseas trade (1572), northern officials simultaneously worried about silver outflows. This temporal convergence highlights how Minnan's maritime fiscal innovations responded to empire-wide pressures—pressures that Liu Yaohui's contemporaries framed as silver hemorrhaging toward the northern frontier (p. 6-7).

## Quantifying the Northern Drain

Kishimoto presents crucial data on Beijing-sourced military allocations (京運年例銀) to frontier garrisons (p. 8, Figure 1). These transfers grew from approximately 700,000 taels annually in the early 16th century to 4 million taels by the early 17th century. For the Jiajing reign specifically, she documents the fiscal strain of anti-wokou mobilization: in 1550 alone, combined military expenditures reached 8 million taels, including 2.8 million in regular frontier allocations plus 2.45 million in "newly added military funds" (新增軍餉) (p. 8, citing *Ming Shizong Shilu* 381:6751-6752).

These figures contextualize the magnitude of fiscal pressure driving reform. The 1570s single whip reforms occurred precisely when frontier expenditures peaked—creating the fiscal-military deadlock that Liu Yaohui confronted in Fujian, where coastal defense demands competed with northern priorities for limited silver resources.

## The "Exit Hole" and "Entry Hole": He Qiaoyuan's Maritime Alternative

The article's most significant contribution for Minnan studies is its analysis of He Qiaoyuan's (何喬遠) 1630 proposal for opening maritime trade (*Kai Yang Haiyi* 開洋海議). He Qiaoyuan—author of the *Min Shu* (閩書), the most comprehensive Ming-era history of Fujian—explicitly contrasted northern frontier expenditures ("exit holes" 出孔) with maritime trade revenues ("entry holes" 入孔):

> "Today within China, there are only these financial resources—there are exit holes but no entry holes. The greatest exit hole is the northwestern frontier; the greatest entry hole is the southwestern barbarians [maritime traders]. What goes out through the exit hole is used to appease the barbarians and defend the frontier—once gone it cannot return. The entry hole is where the southwestern barbarians gather their treasures; yet everything is cut off, while [the state] merely seeks [revenue] through increased levies and heavy exactions" (p. 12).

He Qiaoyuan's framework directly linked Minnan's maritime commercialization to solving the empire's fiscal crisis. He noted that Luzon and Japan "specifically used silver to purchase Chinese products" (專用白銀購買中國產物), quantifying the opportunity cost of maritime prohibition (p. 12). For the dissertation's argument about Minnan as a "laboratory for fiscal reform," He Qiaoyuan's formulation provides contemporary theoretical justification for why maritime customs establishment represented not merely regional accommodation but a structural solution to Ming fiscal crisis.

## Conceptual Frameworks: What Constituted "Wealth"?

Kishimoto analyzes contemporary debates about the nature of money and wealth (p. 14). Xu Guangqi (徐光啟) argued in 1630 that "what ancient sage-kings called wealth was the grain that feeds people and the silk that clothes them" (食人之粟，衣人之帛), dismissing silver as merely a "token of wealth" (財之權) rather than wealth itself. By contrast, fiscal reformers like Jin Xueyan (靳學顏) and Wan Gong (萬恭) argued that increasing the money supply through coinage was essential to relieve the silver shortage.

This conceptual tension illuminates debates surrounding Fujian's Single Whip reforms. When Pang Shangpeng (龐尚鵬) and Liu Yaohui advocated silverization of taxes, they operated within an intellectual climate where silver's relationship to "real" wealth remained contested. The article notes that advocates of coinage argued copper cash "circulated inexhaustibly" (源源流通不竭) unlike silver, which "does not fall from the sky, nor gush from the earth, nor is it made by humans" (銀非雨之自天，非涌之自地，非造之自人) (p. 14, citing Hao Jing 郝敬).

## Silver Inflows and the Maritime Solution

Kishimoto documents that despite contemporary awareness of silver inflows from Japan and the Philippines, few officials explicitly connected maritime trade expansion to solving the silver flowing north problem (p. 11). Zhang Han (張瀚), in his *Songchuang Mengyu* (松窗夢語, 1593), distinguished between northern frontier trade (互市) and southeastern maritime trade (海市):

> "What the maritime trade exchanges is all private wealth of the people, without affecting state finances. The frontier trade has losses without gains; the maritime trade has benefits without harm—why do those in charge of planning not consider this?" (p. 12).

Zhang Han's formulation—that maritime trade "has benefits without harm" (有利而無害) because it mobilized "private wealth" (民間財貨) rather than state treasuries—resonates with how Liu Yaohui's Haicheng customs system operated. Rather than extracting from agricultural taxes, maritime taxation captured commercial surplus already circulating in Minnan's market economy.

## Historiographical Significance

Kishimoto concludes by situating late Ming silver debates within longer patterns of Chinese economic thought (p. 14-15). The Kangxi-era "silver famine" (康熙蕭條) of the 1660s-1680s revived similar discourses, with thinkers like Gu Yanwu and Huang Zongxi advocating copper coinage over silver. The Daoguang-era (道光) crisis of the 1830s-1840s, precipitated by opium-driven silver outflows, again invoked the "flowing out without return" (漏卮) discourse.

For Minnan fiscal history, this intellectual genealogy is crucial: the region's 16th-century silverization through Single Whip reforms and maritime customs establishment represented an early, regionally-specific solution to empire-wide monetary constraints that would recur throughout the late imperial period. The dissertation's focus on Minnan as exceptional—that is, as a region that successfully integrated maritime silver inflows into state finance—finds confirmation in Kishimoto's analysis of how rare He Qiaoyuan's "entry hole" conceptualization remained in Ming economic thought.

## Critical Assessment

Kishimoto's article provides essential intellectual context for understanding why Fujian's fiscal reforms appeared so innovative to contemporaries. However, the study focuses primarily on northern frontier expenditures rather than maritime revenue generation. For Minnan specifically, further research might explore how Fujian officials like Liu Yaohui and Pang Shangpeng understood their reforms within the "silver flowing north" discourse—whether they viewed maritime customs as compensating for northern drains, or as pursuing distinctively regional fiscal rationalization. The article's documentation of Wan Gong's 1580s *Qiangu Yi* (錢穀議), which noted that "silver is scarce so grain must be cheaply sold to transmit to officials" (銀少則穀不得不賤售而輸之官), suggests that Fujian's agricultural deficit created similar silver-grain dislocations as the northern frontier, but through different mechanisms (p. 11).
