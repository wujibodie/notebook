# Zhang Juzheng 張居正 (1525-1582)

**Citation:** Zhang Juzheng 張居正. *Zhang Juzheng Ji 張居正集* [Collected Works of Zhang Juzheng]. Edited by Zhang Shizhe 張舜徽. 4 vols. Wuhan: Hubei renmin chubanshe, 1987. Letters to Liu Yaohui primarily in Juan 5; correspondence with Geng Dingxiang in Juan 11 and 15.

**Summary:**

Zhang Juzheng's collected correspondence, particularly his letters to Liu Yaohui during the latter's tenure as Fujian Provincial Governor (1572-1576) and subsequently as Liangguang Supreme Commander (1576-1579), constitutes essential documentation for understanding the operation of the late Ming reform network at the provincial level. The letters reveal Zhang's strategic vision for coastal defense, his assessment of military personnel (famously dismissing Yu Dayou as "old and treacherous" while championing Hu Shouren as a more vigorous alternative), and his management of inter-provincial coordination during the Lin Feng campaigns. Zhang's correspondence demonstrates how he balanced support for Liu's aggressive military policies against pressure from Guangdong officials favoring pacification, ultimately validating Liu's approach while using his central position to protect Liu from bureaucratic opponents. The letters document Zhang's evolving assessment of the Lin Feng threat, from initial confidence that the pirate had been trapped ("entered the snare") to frustration with Guangdong's failures that led to Liu's transfer to clean up the resulting mess.

Beyond military matters, Zhang's letters to Liu and his successor Geng Dingxiang articulate the philosophical and practical foundations of the reform program. Zhang defends the Single Whip reforms and land surveys against charges of "harshness," arguing that stability requires sufficient revenue and military preparedness—goals that necessitated tough measures against tax evasion and official corruption. His famous dictum "if it benefits the altars of state, I would give my life for it" (*gou li sheji, sisheng yi zhi*) encapsulates the reformers' willingness to override conventional bureaucratic propriety in pursuit of effective governance. The letters to Geng Dingxiang, who succeeded Liu in Fujian, reveal Zhang's efforts to maintain policy continuity while adapting to changed circumstances, encouraging Geng to "ornament" (*xiushi*) the work of Liu and Pang Shangpeng where they succeeded and to complete what they left unfinished. Read together with Liu's own writings, Zhang's letters illuminate the patronage networks, information flows, and strategic calculations that enabled the reform collective to function across the vast distances of the Ming empire.
