Ha Yiming 哈伊鳴, "Mingdai Junshi Zhidu Yanbian" 明代軍事制度演變 (Evolution of Ming Military Institutions), Ph.D. dissertation.

This dissertation provides a comprehensive analysis of the transformation of Ming military institutions from the early dynasty through the late 16th century, with particular attention to how the *weisuo* (garrison) system gave way to professional recruited forces (*mubing*) in response to fiscal pressures and military crisis. The work challenges simplistic narratives of institutional decline by documenting how marketized recruitment and silver-based payments emerged gradually from within the existing system, accelerated by the 1550 Altan Khan raid and the ongoing *wokou* crisis along the southeastern coast. Ha demonstrates that the distinction between northern and southern military experiences was profound: while the court subsidized recruitment on the northern frontier, southern officials were left to raise funds locally through tax surcharges and service commutation, creating distinctive patterns of provincial fiscal autonomy.

The dissertation is particularly valuable for understanding Minnan's military transformation. Ha documents how Yu Dayou's troops received 1.5 *liang* of silver monthly wages plus 20 *liang* sign-up bonuses—substantially higher compensation than hereditary *weisuo* soldiers received—establishing the fiscal foundation for professional military service. The work traces how southern recruits were organized in battalions (*zong*) of 500 led by *zongba* commanders rather than *weisuo* officers, creating parallel military hierarchies that gradually supplanted the hereditary system. Ha's analysis of military expenditure—citing Ray Huang's estimates of seven million *liang* annually for defense in the 1550s, equally divided between northern and southern theaters—illuminates the fiscal pressures that drove administrative reform. The dissertation connects military professionalization directly to the Single Whip reforms, arguing that conversions to silver taxation spread in southeastern China precisely because officials needed funds for anti-*wokou* campaigns. For historians of Minnan's fiscal-military history, this work provides essential context for understanding how the region's agricultural deficit yet commercial prosperity created both the necessity and the resources for military innovation.

Ray Huang 黃仁宇, "Military Expenditures in Sixteenth Century Ming China," in *Chinese Ways in Warfare*, edited by Frank Kierman and John Fairbank (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1974), 35-68.

This foundational essay analyzes the fiscal pressures driving Ming military reform in the 16th century, focusing on how the decay of the *weisuo* system and the rising costs of modern warfare created unsustainable budgetary pressures. Huang documents the distinctive characteristics of southern military finance: funds were raised locally rather than through the Ministry of Revenue, surcharges were distributed variably across counties and prefectures (typically 40% from registered male adults and 60% from landowners), and new revenues created during the *wokou* campaigns—including taxes on previously exempt monastic properties—were never abolished afterward. The essay traces how these locally raised military funds gradually transformed into permanent surcharges on land taxes, establishing precedents for the broader fiscal reforms of the late 16th century.

Huang's analysis illuminates several crucial dimensions of Minnan's fiscal history. His documentation of the Yuegang maritime customs—where tonnage duties were designated "water force supplies" and import duties "ground force supplies"—reveals how maritime commerce was incorporated into military finance, with annual revenues of approximately 20,000 ounces of silver flowing to decentralized military administration. The essay traces rising military wages from six ounces of silver per man annually in the mid-16th century to eighteen ounces by the late century, demonstrating how silver circulation drove military professionalization. Huang's observation that southern defense installations and their finance fell largely into provincial officials' hands—and that this practice extended even to regions never affected by pirates—documents the lasting institutional legacy of the *wokou* crisis. His assessment of Zhang Juzheng's reform methods, noting that even the Grand Secretary had to urge trusted ministers to submit memorials before he could draft supportive rescripts, illuminates the negotiated nature of late Ming governance. For historians of Minnan's fiscal transformation, this essay provides essential framework for understanding how military crisis generated both the necessity and the precedents for the Single Whip reforms.
