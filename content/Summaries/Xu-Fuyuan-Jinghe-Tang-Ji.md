Xu Fuyuan 許孚遠. *Jinghe Tang Ji* [Collection of the Respectful Harmony Hall]. Preface by Ye Xianggao 葉向高 of Fuqing. Late Ming woodblock edition.

This collected work by Xu Fuyuan, a Ming official who served in Fujian, opens with a series of prefaces followed by temple inscriptions (*ji*) and a significant philosophical section titled "Shengxun Fuyan Ba Ze" (Eight Exhortations from Imperial Instructions), which emphasizes unrelenting study over natural intelligence as the path to wisdom. The collection contains a modest amount of philosophical correspondence, including a letter to Lu Yijian that cites Chen Baisha (Chen Xianzhang). Notably, the collection contains relatively little direct reference to Xu's tenure in charge of Fujian administration, distinguishing it from the more explicitly policy-focused collected works of contemporary Fujian officials like Pang Shangpeng or Liu Yaohui. The work reflects the philosophical concerns typical of late Ming literati, with emphasis on moral self-cultivation and the dangers of "falling into vulgar customs" and living as if "drunkenly dreaming through life."

For research on sixteenth-century Minnan fiscal and military institutions, Xu Fuyuan's collection provides limited direct evidence but offers valuable contextual material for understanding the intellectual milieu within which fiscal reforms were debated and implemented. The collection's philosophical emphasis, particularly the "Eight Exhortations," illuminates the Cheng-Zhu orthodox framework that shaped how officials like Xu conceptualized the relationship between statecraft and moral cultivation. The preface by Ye Xianggao—a prominent Fujian official who would later serve as Grand Secretary—suggests Xu's position within the networks of elite Fujian literati that facilitated both reform implementation and resistance. For researchers examining the tension between commercial realities and Confucian moral discourse in late Ming Fujian, this collection exemplifies how even officials with administrative responsibilities often expressed their public identities through philosophical rather than policy writings. The relative absence of practical administrative content in Xu's collected works, when compared to officials like Pang Shangpeng who documented their reforms extensively, itself indicates variation in how Fujian officials positioned themselves within the late Ming political culture.
