**Author:** Lin Feng

**Title:** A Preliminary Analysis of the Temple Land Levy in Fujian

This academic article by Lin Feng examines the *sìtián chōngxiǎng* (寺田充饷), or temple land levy, implemented in Fujian during the Jiajing reign (1522–1566) of the Ming Dynasty. The levy was introduced as a means to fund the extensive military expenditures required to combat Japanese pirates (*wokou*) along the coast. The author argues that beyond the immediate fiscal need, the levy stemmed from an overestimation of the economic power of Buddhist temples and a desire by local officials to assert greater control over Fujianese society.

The article details the historical context of temple lands in Fujian, noting their prosperity from the Five Dynasties to the Song, followed by a decline in the Ming due to chaos, annexation, and changing religious donation patterns. By the Jiajing period, temple lands constituted a mere 1.64% of Fujian's total cultivated land, indicating their limited economic significance. Despite this, various governors implemented the levy with fluctuating rates and scopes, often based on estimated rent rather than actual land ownership, extending the burden to lands with temple rent rights and even mortgaged properties. The author highlights the severe negative consequences: temples struggled to pay, leading to economic decline, abandonment, and the flight of monks. Commoners who had purchased or mortgaged temple lands faced a system of "one land, two taxes," causing immense financial strain, disputes, and land abandonment. The total revenue generated by the levy, approximately 16,560 taels annually, was negligible compared to the military expenses exceeding 300,000 taels, rendering it a "drop in the bucket." The article concludes that the *sìtián chōngxiǎng* ultimately failed to significantly bolster military funds while causing widespread hardship and social unrest, exacerbated by officials' attempts to target powerful local families under the guise of temple land taxation. Key sources include Gu Yanwu's *Tianxia Junguo Libing Shu* (天下郡国利病书), the *Veritable Records of Emperor Shizong of Ming* (明世宗实录), and various local gazetteers.
