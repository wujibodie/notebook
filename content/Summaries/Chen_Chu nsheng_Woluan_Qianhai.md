# Chen Chunsheng 陳春聲

**Citation:** Chen Chunsheng 陳春聲. *Cong Woluan dao Qianhai: Chaozhou yu Ming-Qing Haijiang Shehui de Yanbian 从倭乱到迁海：潮州与明清海疆社会的演变* [From Wokou Troubles to Coastal Clearance: Chaozhou and the Evolution of Ming-Qing Maritime Society]. Beijing: Zhongguo shehui kexue chubanshe, 2017.

**Summary:**

Chen Chunsheng's magisterial study of Chaozhou (eastern Guangdong) during the Ming-Qing transition reconceptualizes the "wokou troubles" and "coastal clearance" periods not as discrete crises but as continuous processes of socio-political transformation shaped by the complex interaction between imperial systems and local society. Drawing on extensive local gazetteers, lineage records, and official documents, Chen demonstrates that Chaozhou's difficulty of governance stemmed from its population's orientation toward commerce rather than scholarship—a characteristic that distinguished the Chao-Zhang border zone from the more examination-successful Quanzhou region despite their shared maritime commercial orientation. The work analyzes how the practice of village fortification developed during the late Ming wokou crisis fundamentally altered local power structures, creating armed communities that the Qing state would later struggle to control, culminating in the coastal evacuation policies of the Kangxi era.

For understanding 16th-century Minnan, Chen's study provides essential comparative context for the Fujian-Guangdong borderlands that Liu Yaohui governed. His analysis of the mutual recriminations between Fujian and Guangdong officials during the pirate campaigns—where Qi Jiguang blamed Zhangzhou for producing bandits while Yu Dayou faulted Chaozhou—illuminates the jurisdictional tensions that structured cross-provincial military coordination. Chen's discussion of how Chaozhou literati attributed their region's examination success to good customs while blaming piracy on neighboring Zhangzhou reveals the cultural politics of coastal identity formation. The work's emphasis on the long-term continuity of coastal social structures across the Ming-Qing transition supports the dissertation's argument about the persistence of Minnan's commercial-agricultural tensions throughout the late imperial period. Chen's documentation of how local elites used fortification, lineage organization, and strategic accommodation with both pirates and officials to maintain autonomy provides a crucial counterpoint to the more state-centered narrative of Liu Yaohui's reform efforts.
