Wan Ming 萬明. "Junpingfa Kao: Yi Qinyin Liangzhe Junpinglu Wei Zhongxin" [An Examination of the Equalization Method: Centered on the Imperially Approved Equalization Record of Zhejiang]. *Zhongguo Shehui Kexue* [Social Sciences in China], no. 2 (2015): 146-157.

Wan Ming's article provides the most comprehensive analysis of the 1566 *Qinyin Liangzhe Junpinglu* (欽依兩浙均平錄), which she characterizes as "the most complete document of a Ming Dynasty fiscal reform." The study traces the intellectual genealogy of "equalization" (均平) from classical precedents in the *Zhouli* and *Mencius* to its sixteenth-century implementation by Pang Shangpeng in Zhejiang. Wan documents how Pang, upon arriving in Zhejiang in 1565, immediately identified the lijia system as the source of accumulated fiscal abuses and secured approval from Minister of Revenue Gao Yao for comprehensive reform. The article clarifies the relationship between the Junping reforms and the Single Whip method, arguing that while both shared similar goals of simplifying levies and silverization, the Junping method focused specifically on reforming lijia regular service, whereas the Single Whip incorporated all taxes and corvées into a unified system.

For research on Minnan fiscal reform, Wan's work is crucial for understanding the institutional template that Pang Shangpeng attempted to implement in Fujian after his Zhejiang success. Her documentation of Pang's 1566 memorial advocating empire-wide implementation—"the difficulties of the lijia are surely not unique to Zhejiang"—provides direct evidence for how surveillance officials functioned as vectors for fiscal reform transmission across provinces. The article's clarification that Pang likely knew of Pan Jixun's similar reforms in Guangdong, though no comparable documentation survives, illuminates the broader network of reform experimentation in southern coastal provinces. Wan's analysis of the Junping method's specific provisions for converting corvée obligations to silver payments and establishing transparent budgeting procedures offers a benchmark for evaluating the partial and contested implementation of similar reforms in Fujian's more commercially complex environment. The article's historiographical correction—establishing that the Junyao reforms in Zhejiang began in 1450 under Zhu Ying rather than during the Tianshun reign—demonstrates the deep administrative precedents for mid-Ming fiscal innovation.
