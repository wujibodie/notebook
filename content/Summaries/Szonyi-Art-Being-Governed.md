Szonyi, Michael. *The Art of Being Governed: Everyday Politics in Late Imperial China*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2017.

Michael Szonyi's study of Ming military households (*junhu*) provides critical context for understanding the organization and dysfunction of coastal defense in 16th-century Fujian. Szonyi demonstrates that the basic role of coastal *weisuo* (衛所, military garrisons) was policing rather than confronting major military forces: "Military units stationed there did not, as they did in the north, expect to face powerful military forces keen to seize control of Ming territory. Their basic military task was to patrol the seas to limit illegal trade" (pp. 108-109). Soldiers rotated periodically into active duty at maritime forts (*shuizhai* 水寨), forming the front line of a system that was theoretically comprehensive but practically porous.

Particularly relevant for Minnan studies is Szonyi's discussion of Zhu Wan (朱紈, 1494-1550), the official appointed to address coastal unrest who "made a good start at restoring order" but antagonized powerful local interests secretly involved in maritime trade. Szonyi cites Zhu Wan's identification of Lin Xiyuan (林希元, ca. 1480-1560)—the prominent Tong'an literatus who features centrally in this dissertation—as "the worst of the Fujian literati in league with pirates and smugglers." According to Zhu Wan's memorial, Lin's family operated a substantial fleet engaged in Southeast Asian trade, avoiding maritime prohibitions by "pretending the ships were local ferryboats." This characterization of Lin Xiyuan illustrates the complex intersection of gentry status, mercantile interests, and official prohibition that defined Minnan's coastal political economy during the Jiajing reign, and the difficulty reformers faced in distinguishing between legitimate coastal trade and "piracy" when local elites actively blurred these boundaries.
