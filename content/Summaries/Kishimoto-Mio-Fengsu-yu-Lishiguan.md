Kishimoto Mio 岸本美緒. *Fengsu yu Lishiguan* 風俗與歷史觀 [Custom and Views of History]. Beijing: Shangwu Yinshuguan, 2018.

Kishimoto Mio's collection of essays presents a sophisticated methodological intervention in the study of Chinese social and economic history, particularly relevant to understanding the intellectual frameworks through which Ming officials and scholars comprehended their own historical moment. The first essay distinguishes her preferred periodization approaches from stadial visions of historical materialism, arguing that concepts like *gudai* (古代), *zhongshi* (中世), and *jindai* (近代) reflect not objective historical divisions but rather "disputed impressions of generations of historians." Drawing on Fernand Braudel, she emphasizes the importance of "structure" over "events" while removing the demand for teleological directionality in historical analysis, enabling more precise examination of relations between different timescales. The essay argues for a global "early modern" (近世) framework for the 16th-18th centuries, not because Europe and China were identical or incorporated into a single world system, but because distinct regions became increasingly interconnected with fluctuations happening in shared rhythms.

Particularly valuable for Minnan studies is Kishimoto's analysis of *fengsu* (風俗, custom/mores) as both descriptive and normative concept in Chinese political thought. Unlike "custom," "culture," or "civilization" in Western frameworks, *fengsu* possesses both "regional" and "directional" elements—local habits were evaluated and potentially altered by rulers and scholar-officials, making descriptions of custom part of political evaluation of social order. Her analysis of Gu Yanwu's historical thought demonstrates how *fengsu* served as a framework for understanding moral agency in history without necessary directionality. For 16th-century Fujian fiscal and military history, Kishimoto's framework illuminates how contemporaries understood the social transformations they witnessed—how officials like Lin Xiyuan or Pang Shangpeng conceptualized the relationship between institutional reform and social change, and how the concept of *fengsu* informed their assessments of whether Minnan's commercialization represented moral decline or improvement.
