Chen Qide 
-  Starts with Chen's narration of his prosperous early life, when a "peck" (斗) of rice cost 3-4 "cents" (分), before natural disasters in 1588-9 caused severe price increases for the first time in his memory. The next wave of famine in his home region came in the Tianqi reign. The worst famines arrived in 1641-2, when Chen wrote his essay, at which time a pig might cost 7-8 taels while a serving girl was just 1-2000 *wen* of copper.

Chapter Two: Wanli Price Regime
- The "fiscal wages" recorded in gazetteers after the junyao silver reforms tend low, with ordinary laborers being paid 4-5 taels annually; since this is below the assumed minimum for subsistence of around 12 taels/annum, these were probably part-time wages. 
- Bingbu Shangshu Liang Tingdong, in a 1630 report on illegal foreign trade, noted that Fujian sailors in the SCS could earn 20-30 taels a year, which was seen as a good wage. 60
- Wage scale for officials in the late ming ranged from 19.52 taels for 9b rank to 67 taels at 3b rank, then 88.84 for a vice minister (3a), 152 for a censor-in-chief (2a), and 265 for a grand secretary (1a).
	- Still, for Ray Huang, these salaries were "inadequate" for living expenses in the Ming, which Brook doesn't dispute, despite the fact that they are clearly above the "respectable minimum" of 25 taels or so per year. I can't accept this judgement so readily. It's worth asking what precisely these salaries were inadequate for -- maintenance of large households with many servants? Payment of scribes, legal experts, or other hangers-on? Performance of gentility? Or sumptuary separation from the base wealthy? How did this sense of inadequacy change over the 16th/17th centuries?

Chapter 3: Silver, Prices, and Maritime Trade
- "The agency assigned to manage tribute relations at the levels of both policy and practice was the Ministry of Rites. However, ­because the management of gifts and imports generated revenue, the ministry regularly found itself in competition with the Imperial House­hold, which through the early decades of the dynasty took over the administration and collection of customs duties." 78
	- The 市舶司 was staffed by eunuchs of the Imperial Household from early on, but they started with just one customs house, at Taicang; it was shut down in 1374 and replaced by Ningbo, Quanzhou, and Guangzhou. (Brook inaccurately states that the Quanzhou house was closed "during the Jiajing ban on foreign trade," rather than being replaced by Fuzhou early on.)
	- "When the tomb of eunuch Wei Juan, who headed the Canton Maritime Trade Office from 1476 to 1488, was excavated in 1964, archaeologists found three foreign silver coins, two from Bengal and one from Venice, which shows that, even in the fifteenth ­ century, foreigners ­ were bringing foreign cash to buy Chinese goods."
	- Brook's summary of foreign trade disputes in Wanli Fujian (citing Shen Defu, 萬曆野獲編): "Even so, the elite of Fujian province ­were split on the issue. Whereas the gentry of Quanzhou and Zhangzhou on the south coast, well positioned to benefit from maritime trade, agitated to keep the coast open, the gentry of Fuzhou and Xinghua prefectures further north distrusted the private wealth pouring into the southern end of the province and wanted the ban upheld. Shen supported the southern faction. Reopening trade would bring the law back into alignment with how ­ matters actually stood." 82
	- Analyzes a list of prices from a 1575 complaint by Chinese merchants about failure to receive payment for various goods at Manila. Notes that the price of pepper was "loosely comparable in Manila and Zhangzhou... because the spice had to be shipped to both ports from the Spice Islands;" in other words there was a common pepper market with common prices in the northern half of the Nanyang. Silk and ceramics were double the silver price in Manila than Zhangzhou, but most everyday goods were close in price. 
	- Brook sees little sign that the "Magellanic exchange" of silver over the Pacific drove up prices in the late Ming: "This is not to say that the arrival of silver in excess of 100,000 kilograms per year had no effect whatsoever on the Ming economy. But that effect has yet to be empirically demonstrated. The only sector in which I have been able to detect the effect of an abundance of silver driving up prices, noted ­ toward the close of the preceding chapter, is the luxury market." 104
