# Jiao Hong and the architecture of Ming biographical memory

Jiao Hong (焦竑, 1540–1620) compiled the *Guochao xianzheng lu* (國朝獻徵錄), a 120-*juan* biographical compendium of Ming dynasty figures, into what the early Qing historian Wan Sitong declared was **the only private Ming historical work comprehensive enough to serve as a basis for the official dynastic history**. More than a biographical reference, the work crystallizes a distinct late Ming historiographical moment—one in which the empire's archival monopoly collapsed, commercial printing democratized access to texts, and a generation of scholars bridging Wang Yangming philosophy and empirical research assembled unprecedented documentary compilations. Jiao Hong stood at the center of this moment: the 1589 *zhuangyuan* (top examination scorer), a Three Teachings syncretist, a bibliophile embedded in Nanjing's publishing world, a lifelong friend of the heterodox thinker Li Zhi, and an advocate for Su Shi's poetry and authentic literary expression. His compilation practices, source methodology, and intellectual networks illuminate how late Ming scholars mediated between stone inscriptions and printed books, between official orthodoxy and radical heterodoxy, and between the philosophical speculation of the Wanli era and the evidential rigor that would define the Qing.

---

## How Jiao Hong built a biographical monument from a thousand sources

The *Guochao xianzheng lu*—"Record of Evident Worthies of Our Dynasty"—originated in an abortive state project. In **1594**, Grand Secretary Chen Yubi proposed compiling an official dynastic history, and Jiao Hong was designated a principal compiler. A 1597 palace fire destroyed materials the History Bureau had gathered. Though the official project collapsed, Jiao continued collecting privately for nearly two more decades. The work's preface is dated **Wanli 44 (1616)**, when Jiao was 77 years old, just four years before his death. Only a single Ming-era printing was produced, by Xu Xiangyun's Manshan guan press.

The work covers biographies from the dynasty's founding in 1368 through approximately the end of the Jiajing reign (1566). Its organizational principle mirrors the Ming bureaucratic hierarchy with remarkable granularity: *juan* 1–4 treat the imperial house and imperial affines; *juan* 5–10 cover hereditary nobility; *juan* 11–53 proceed through the central government from the Grand Secretariat and Hanlin Academy through the Six Ministries (with separate sections for the parallel Beijing and Nanjing establishments); *juan* 54–103 cover the Censorate, provincial administration organized by province, and other offices; *juan* 104–111 address the military; and *juan* 112–119 encompass non-official categories—filial sons, righteous persons, Confucian scholars (*rulin*), literati (*yiyuan*), recluses, eunuchs, Buddhist monks, Daoist priests, and "heroes who died for the dynasty." This architecture reproduces the full complexity of the **dual-capital Ming bureaucratic system** more comprehensively than any comparable private compilation.

Jiao Hong drew on what Chinese scholars describe as **nearly a thousand different documentary sources**: imperial edicts, local gazetteers, tomb inscriptions (*muzhiming* 墓志銘), stele inscriptions (*beiming* 碑銘), unofficial histories (*yeshi*), official conduct descriptions (*xingzhuang* 行狀), and private biographical writings. The single most important methodological feature is that **for most biographies, he indicated his primary source by name**. The Chinese historian Xiang Yannan calls the work "the crown among Ming-dynasty biographical compilations" (*煌煌巨编足当明代传记之冠*). Its source-citation practice makes it invaluable not only for biographical content but for reconstructing the now-lost textual landscape of Ming historiography.

On the specific question of stone rubbings versus text transcriptions, the evidence indicates that Jiao Hong worked overwhelmingly from **textual sources rather than physical rubbings**. By the late sixteenth century, inscription texts circulated primarily through printed collected works (*wenji*), manuscript copies, local gazetteers, and earlier biographical compilations. Jiao Hong's practice of citing named sources—specific authors' collections, specific gazetteers—confirms he was working from identifiable textual witnesses. As a Nanjing-based bibliophile with access to major private libraries and the Hanlin Academy's archives, he occupied a central node in late Ming book-lending networks. The bibliophile Qi Chenghan's diary records borrowing "more than ten works" from Jiao Hong, documenting the kind of personal scholarly exchange that fueled the compilation. Jiao also employed a **cross-reference method** (*bianjian fa* 別見法) to avoid duplication across the work's 120 *juan*—evidence of careful editorial management of an enormous textual apparatus.

## Five approaches to Ming biography, from the archival to the iconoclastic

The *Guochao xianzheng lu* emerges most clearly when set against the contrasting biographical and historical projects of Jiao Hong's contemporaries. These five scholars represent distinct strategies for converting human lives into historical knowledge.

**Wang Shizhen** (王世貞, 1526–1590), leader of the Latter Seven Masters literary movement, declared that "all between heaven and earth is nothing but history" (*無非史而已*). His *Yanshantang bieji* (弇山堂別集, 100 *juan*, completed 1590) constituted an unofficial set of veritable records for the mid-Ming, combining institutional history, biographical sketches of Grand Secretaries, and critical annotations on source reliability. Where Jiao Hong preserved others' texts with attribution, Wang wrote **original critical biographies** embedded in political analysis—often driven by personal agendas, including the desire to vindicate his father, who had been executed under Yan Song's faction. Peter Bol's study of Hu Yinglin (1551–1602) situates Wang Shizhen as representing a late Ming alternative to Neo-Confucian learning, one grounded in literary aesthetics and historical skepticism. Both Wang's and Jiao's compilations fed directly into the later *Mingshi*.

**Wang Daokun** (汪道昆, 1525–1593), a Huizhou scholar-official, produced a different kind of biographical writing: locally rooted, **commissioned by families and lineages**, and notable for its innovative merchant biographies. His collected works, the *Taihan ji* (太函集), contain extensive tomb inscriptions and commemorative prose written for Huizhou mercantile families. His muzhiming and stele biographies represent precisely the kind of **raw source material** that Jiao Hong gathered into the *Guochao xianzheng lu*. Where Jiao's project was national in scope and compilatory in method, Wang Daokun's was regional, patronage-driven, and literary—"perfecting Huizhou mercantile lineage culture" by presenting merchants as committed to Confucian values, as Qitao Guo's *Huizhou* (2022) argues.

**Li Zhi** (李贄, 1527–1602) took a radically different approach. His *Cangshu* (藏書, "A Book to Hide Away," 68 *juan*) and *Xu Cangshu* (續藏書, 27 *juan*) were works of **iconoclastic historical reassessment**, covering figures from the Warring States through the Yuan (and, in the continuation, the Ming). Li explicitly rejected Confucius as the standard of moral judgment and reversed traditional verdicts on historical heroes and villains. Wang Shizhen wrote a preface to the *Cangshu* noting Li's insistence on the relativity of right and wrong. The contrast with Jiao Hong is structurally revealing: **Jiao's project was empirical and archival** (preserving source texts with attribution), while **Li's was polemical and philosophical** (rewriting received moral judgments). Yet the two men were lifelong friends, and the *Xu Cangshu* drew on the *Guochao xianzheng lu* as a source for Ming-period biographies—a direct documentary link between the compiler and the critic.

**Geng Dingxiang** (耿定向, 1524–1596), Vice Censor-in-Chief and Jiao Hong's teacher, represented the **orthodox moral center** of this network. His collected writings (*Geng Tiantai xiansheng wenji*) include philosophical essays and some biographies, but his primary role was as a gatekeeper of Confucian ethical norms. His famous public epistolary quarrel with Li Zhi—analyzed by Timothy Brook as an instance of the late Ming "public sphere of letters"—defined the poles between which Jiao Hong had to navigate. Where Geng emphasized *mingjiao* (teachings of social order), Li championed intuitive truth-seeking. Jiao, as student of one and friend of the other, embodied the tension.

The *Guochao xianzheng lu* also belongs to a longer tradition of **stele-biography collections** (*beizhuan ji*). The Southern Song scholar Du Dagui compiled the *Mingchen beizhuan wanyan ji* (名臣碑傳琬琰集, 107 *juan*, 1194), collecting spirit-road inscriptions, tomb inscriptions, and conduct descriptions for 221 eminent Song officials. Xu Hong (fl. 1500) produced a Ming successor, the *Ming mingchen wanyan lu* (24+22 *juan*), covering 202 Ming figures. Jiao Hong's work explicitly surpassed both in scale and detail. The tradition continued into the Qing: Qian Yiji compiled a 160-*juan* *Beizhuan ji* covering over 2,000 Qing figures, followed by supplements by Miao Quansun and Min Erchang. Jiao Hong thus stands at the tradition's late Ming apex.

## The scholar who bridged orthodoxy and heterodoxy

Jiao Hong's significance extends well beyond the *Guochao xianzheng lu*. He functioned as a **mediating figure** across several of the most consequential divides in late Ming intellectual life.

His philosophical position was a "radical culmination of Ming Neo-Confucianism," as Edward Ch'ien's foundational 1986 monograph argues. A follower of the Taizhou school—Huang Zongxi placed him in the "Taizhou School Case" of the *Mingru xue'an*—Jiao argued that human nature was initially "neither good nor not good" (*無善無不善*) and advocated boldly for **Three Teachings syncretism**, going so far as to claim that Buddhist texts were sometimes better suited than Confucian ones for explicating the Way. He compiled commentaries on the *Laozi* and *Zhuangzi* and defended "heterodoxy" (*yiduan*) as a legitimate source of insight. Yet he also held the empire's most prestigious examination credential and served as a Hanlin Academy compiler who taught the Crown Prince. This combination of radical philosophical commitments and impeccable institutional standing made him uniquely positioned to connect disparate intellectual worlds.

The connection to literary reform ran deep. Jiao Hong wrote a **preface to the Yuan drama *Xixiang ji*** (Romance of the Western Chamber) in 1582 under the pseudonym "Longdong shannong" (Mountain Farmer of the Dragon Cave). This preface became the direct launching point for Li Zhi's landmark essay **"On the Childlike Mind" (*Tongxin shuo*)**, one of the late Ming's most influential literary-philosophical texts, which opens with an apologetic reference to Jiao's preface. Li Zhi's *tongxin* doctrine—that the uncorrupted "childlike mind" produces authentic literature, including vernacular fiction like *Shuihu zhuan* and drama like *Xixiang ji*—fed directly into the Gongan school's theory of **xingling** (性靈, "native sensibility"), championed by the Yuan brothers (Yuan Zongdao, Yuan Hongdao, Yuan Zhongdao). The Gongan school's advocacy of Song dynasty poets, particularly **Su Shi**, challenged the archaist movement's insistence on Tang models. Jiao Hong himself wrote a **preface to a printed edition of Su Shi's collected works** (*Ke Su Changgong ji xu*), praising Su's literature as drawing "from both a study of classic texts as well as an active heart-mind that roams the vast firmament." This positions Jiao as a direct participant in—not merely adjacent to—the late Ming revaluation of literary authenticity.

Jiao Hong was also connected to the early encounter between Chinese and Western learning. He met **Matteo Ricci** in Nanjing, likely in late 1599, at a gathering that also included Li Zhi and the Buddhist monk Xuelang Dashi. Jiao quoted Ricci's opening maxim from the *Jiaoyou lun* (Treatise on Friendship): "Friends are my second self." More consequentially, Jiao served as examiner for the 1597 provincial examination at which **Xu Guangqi** passed—the man who would become Ricci's closest Chinese collaborator and one of the "Three Pillars of Chinese Catholicism." The convergence of Jiao Hong, Li Zhi, Ricci, and Xu Guangqi in late Wanli Nanjing illustrates the remarkable intellectual porosity of the era.

## From Jiao Hong to Gu Yanwu: continuity, rupture, or both?

The relationship between the late Ming historians and the more famous early Qing evidential scholars remains one of the most contested questions in Chinese intellectual history, and Jiao Hong sits at its center.

The dominant narrative, established by **Liang Qichao** in the 1920s, posited a sharp rupture: early Qing scholars (Gu Yanwu, Huang Zongxi, Wang Fuzhi) reacted against Wang Yangming-school subjectivism, blamed it for the fall of the Ming, and turned to "practical learning" (*jingshi*) and evidential research (*kaozheng*). **Benjamin Elman's** *From Philosophy to Philology* (1984) largely accepted this periodization while framing it as "an empirical revolution" that broke with Neo-Confucian teleology. Elman followed his Qing subjects in dating the emergence of exact philological understanding to the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, but parsed the shift as a historical transition from "philosophy" (Ming) "to philology" (Qing).

**Yu Ying-shih** challenged this framework, arguing for substantial continuity between Neo-Confucianism and *kaozheng*. In his 1988 review of Ch'ien's monograph, Yu argued that Jiao Hong's mind teaching (*xinxue*) and textual criticism were **independent within his philosophical system** and that no causal relationship connected them—against Ch'ien's thesis that *xinxue* gave birth to *kaozheng*. More recently, **Dai Yumin's** *On Jiao Hong and Ming-Qing Confucianism* (2023) proposes a **"dual-track transformation" model**: the Ming-to-Qing shift involved two concurrent developments—the decline of mind teaching and rise of *qi* philosophy, and the development of textual criticism—with Jiao Hong standing at the intersection of both tracks.

The late Ming historians are connected to their early Qing successors through both documentary and intellectual genealogies. **Huang Zunsu** (黃尊素), the father of Huang Zongxi, reportedly told his son on his deathbed: "For scholars, the most important thing is to know history; read the *Xianzheng lu*" (*学者最要紧是通知史事，可读《献征录》*). Huang Zongxi's *Mingru xue'an* (1676) systematized the intellectual history of the very Taizhou school figures—including Jiao Hong—who had constituted the previous generation's philosophical world. **Wan Sitong** (1638–1702), Huang Zongxi's disciple and the unofficial but indispensable compiler of the *Mingshi* draft, drew on the *Guochao xianzheng lu* as a key source. Zhang Dai's *Shikui shu* (石匱書), a comprehensive Ming history compiled over decades, built on the same tradition of private historiography that Jiao Hong had exemplified.

Institutional conditions also link the two generations. Peter Ditmanson's research on "broadening historical consciousness in late Ming China" (*Ming Studies*, 2015) and Devin Fitzgerald's Harvard dissertation *The Ming Open Archive* (2020) document how court records that were once tightly controlled became freely available by the sixteenth century. The Veritable Records entered private libraries; scholars like Jiao Hong and Wang Shizhen could produce independent compilations without state sanction. This **"open archive" phenomenon** created the documentary infrastructure that both late Ming compilers and early Qing historians relied upon. A recent study by Leigh Jenco (LSE) argues that scholars like Jiao Hong and Li Zhi "defy historiographical narratives that divide the moral philosophy of the Ming dynasty from the empiricist philology of the Qing dynasty," since both combined Yangming learning with empirical investigation.

The Qing reception was ultimately ambivalent. The *Siku quanshu* editors praised the *Guochao xianzheng lu* as "extremely broad in collection" (*搜采极博*) but criticized it as "rather diffuse, not entirely reliable" (*文颇泛滥，不皆可据*). During the Qianlong literary inquisition, the work was **banned outright**, and only a handful of copies survived—it was not reprinted until 1965 in Taiwan. This suppression paradoxically confirmed the work's significance: it contained materials the Qing court found dangerous, and its documentary breadth remained unmatched.

## The scholarly landscape in English and Chinese

The English-language foundation is **Edward T. Ch'ien's** *Chiao Hung and the Restructuring of Neo-Confucianism in the Late Ming* (Columbia UP, 1986), which places Jiao at the center of the transition from Ming philosophy to Qing philology. **Yu Ying-shih's** 1988 review essay (reprinted in *Chinese History and Culture*, Columbia UP, 2016) offers the most important critical response. On late Ming book culture, **Kai-wing Chow's** *Publishing, Culture, and Power in Early Modern China* (Stanford, 2004) and the landmark volume he co-edited with **Cynthia Brokaw**, *Printing and Book Culture in Late Imperial China* (UC Press, 2005), provide essential context for understanding how compilations like the *Guochao xianzheng lu* were produced and circulated. **Lucille Chia's** chapter "Of Three Mountains Street" in that same volume specifically reconstructs the Nanjing commercial publishing world in which Jiao Hong was embedded. On Wang Shizhen, **Peter Bol's** "Looking to Wang Shizhen" (*Ming Studies*, 2006) and a chapter in *Representing Lives in China* (Cornell) analyze his biographical writing. On the Li Zhi–Geng Dingxiang relationship, **Timothy Brook's** "A Public of Letters" and the edited volume *The Objectionable Li Zhi* (UW Press) are essential. For Li Zhi's works themselves, **Rivi Handler-Spitz, Pauline Lee, and Haun Saussy's** *A Book to Burn and a Book to Keep (Hidden)* (Columbia UP, 2016) provides the first English translation.

In Chinese, **Li Jianxiong's** (李剑雄) *Jiao Hong pingzhuan* (焦竑评传, Nanjing University Press, 1998) is the foundational critical biography, including the first systematic examination of Jiao Hong's complete oeuvre and a chronological biography (*nianpu*). **Xiang Yannan** (向燕南) published the most detailed analytical article on the *Guochao xianzheng lu*'s compilation principles and source methodology in *Wenxian jikan* (1999). **Zhan Long** (展龙) assessed the work's historiographical source value. **Huang Zhenping** (黄振萍, Tsinghua University) edited the *Jiao Hong wenxian jikan* (焦竑文献辑刊), a major documentary collection reprinting 17 Ming-era original editions from overseas libraries. **Dai Yumin's** (代玉民) recent monograph advances the "dual-track" theory of Ming-Qing Confucian transformation. **Lin Qingzhang's** (林庆彰) *Mingdai kaozhengxue yanjiu* provides a comprehensive examination of Ming evidential scholarship, with significant attention to Jiao Hong. A comprehensive review article, "Jiao Hong yanjiu zongshu" (焦竑研究综述), catalogs over 50 scholarly works across all dimensions of Jiao Hong studies and is the best single entry point into the Chinese-language literature.

## Conclusion

Jiao Hong's *Guochao xianzheng lu* was not merely a biographical reference work but a **theory of historical knowledge materialized as compilation practice**. By citing sources for most entries, Jiao made visible the evidentiary chain linking stone inscriptions, manuscript biographies, gazetteers, and printed collections to historical claims—anticipating the source-critical methods that Qing evidential scholars would systematize. His work drew its nearly thousand sources through the personal networks, library exchanges, and commercial publishing infrastructure of late Ming Nanjing, transforming scattered documentary traces into a navigable architecture of dynastic memory.

The late Ming generation—Jiao Hong, Wang Shizhen, Li Zhi, Wang Daokun, Geng Dingxiang—does constitute a coherent historiographical moment, though scholarship has been slow to theorize it as such. What united them was not method but **conditions**: the opening of the Ming archive, the publishing revolution centered in Nanjing and the Jiangnan region, the philosophical expansiveness of late Yangming thought, and the shared conviction that biographical knowledge mattered for governance, moral philosophy, and literary culture alike. Their projects diverged radically in purpose—Jiao compiled, Wang Shizhen criticized, Li Zhi provoked, Wang Daokun memorialized, Geng Dingxiang policed—but each depended on the same expanding textual ecosystem. The early Qing scholars inherited both their documents and their debates. Whether we see the transition as rupture (Elman), continuity (Yu Ying-shih), or dual-track transformation (Dai Yumin), Jiao Hong's compilation stands as the clearest surviving artifact of the documentary foundations on which all subsequent Ming historiography was built.
