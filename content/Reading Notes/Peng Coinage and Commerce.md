
Chapter 5

- According to quotes from the Guoyu on 218, supporting commerce was understand to involve lowering tariffs/taxes and improving infrastructure.
- Scholars disagree over whether the Eastern Zhou spate of city-building was motivated by military or economic concerns; but in any cases walled cities, once built, would be home to production and commerce. 
- The Warring States saw the widespread use of iron tools by peasants; since most peasants could not cast their own iron, they had to be involved in trade to purchase these tools. 223-225
- Numerous inscriptions have been found referring to government-run markets or tax posts, especially on pottery that may have come from state workshops. 
- 258: Bronze boards which served as tax exemption certificates from Chu dating to 323 BC. These exemptions specify ordinary routes and taxable products.  
- Survey of the Warring States road system on 231-2. Later in the Warring States canals were dug connecting the Yangtze, Huai, and Huanghe and numerous tributaries. 
	- "It can be seen that northern land routes and southern water routes joined together mainly at two points: Nanyang and Kaifeng." 234. 
- Evidence for a large grain surplus and frequent long-distance trade of grain in the Spring and Autumn. Interstate treaties included clauses on free trade of grain and solidarity in times of bad harvest; Qin famously invaded Jin after its grain charity was not reciprocated. 247
	- 249: "To sum up, a strong social demand and broad application of new techniques provided a big surplus in grain production in the Eastern Zhou period, especially in the Warring States period. As a result, grain became the most important commodity in trade. The volatility of grain prices in markets, therefore, attracted broad attention as a new social problem."
- Salt was another major article of trade, and was largely sold by Qi; Jin also possessed a salt lake called Gu, which was noted to produce great fortunes. 249-50
- The Haiwang chapter of the Guanzi argues for taxing iron production rather than putting it under direct state monopoly. However, archaeological evidence for central state iron foundries has been found in the capitals of Yan and Qi; Qin was also recorded as having central iron production. 
- Discussion of livestock trade with the steppe on 256-8; a general shortage of meat and the military need for horses made the trade in livestock very lucrative. Steppe burials show that the elite imported silk, bronze mirrors, and jewelry in exchange for animals and jade. 
- Various discussions of land-labor ratios in ancient China support the idea that the Jin region was overpopulated, while Qin and Chu had relatively sparse populations and thus encouraged immigration and settlement of wastelands. As a result the Guanzhong/Henan/Shandong regions tended to specialize in crafts for export and formed the "center" of a "commodity economy." 267-8.
	- The largest-scale bronze workshops of the Eastern Zhou have been found at Houma, the Jin capital. 268
- Draws a comparison between the Macedonian and Qin unifications as "victory of agriculture and a failure of the commodity economy," in vindication of the Shang Yang/Xunzi/Han Fei condemnation of the *mo* vs. the *ben* 275.
