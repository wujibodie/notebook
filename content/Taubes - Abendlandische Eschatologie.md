Intro 
- Taubes: Born in Vienna in 1923, moved to Zurich and ordained there as rabbi at 19, finished his habilitation at 23, thereafter published only books of essays. (See Vom Kult zur Kultur and Der Preis des Messianismus). Worked in New York, Jerusalem, and Berlin.
	- "Arch-enemy" and foil to his political theology was Schmitt; corresponded with Scholem but had an "irreparable break" in 1952-3. Cited very early by Karl Lowith (whose *Meaning of History* sketches a similar genealogy from Daniel to Marx). 
	- "Schmitt, an "apocalyptician of counterrevolution," shares an eschatological view of history, but he advocates *translatio imperii*... along with the retarding force of the katechon, described in *The Nomos of the Earth* as "the restrainer who holds back the end of the word." Schmitts view of history can thus be said to be *katechontic,* seeking divine legitimation of power, while Taubes's is emphatically *apocalyptic*, seeking a theological *delegitimation* of political power as a whole." By contrast, Lowith deplored the authoritarian appropriation of chiliastic language (e.g. Mussolini's use of *Duce* derived from St. Francis and Joachim); "what Lowith seems to confuse, however, is precisely the spiritual, apocalyptic tradition with the imperial, "katechontic" one."
- Theme of apocalypticism as revolutionary force "springing from situations of exile" (or alienation), driven by the drive for a "turn" in history or the individual soul (*Wende, Umschlag, Umkehr; shuwu, metanoia, conversio*)

Elements
- Opening question: "How is history possible in the first place? What is the sufficient condition on which history as possibility rests?"
	- Short answer: "It is in the Eschaton that history surpasses its limitations and is seen for what it is."
- General picture: time is unidirectional and therefore exists with an awareness of the end (Taubes couches this in ontological terms where I prefer subjective ones). "Apocalypse" means revelation and the passage from time to eternity, or, rather, the final knowledge of Time's End. History is the "tragedy of freedom" before knowledge of the End of Time; this freedom unfolds as a "tragedy" because it can only be opposed to God (in that it departs from the necessity of nature and the law). "A *non posse peccare* is no different from a compulsion to do good... Therefore, the freedom of negation is the foundation of history." 5
- "The world as history has its roots [Schwerpunkt] not in the eternal present of nature but rather is nurtured by the original creation and directed toward the final redemption. The individual event serves the passage from creation to redemption. It does not have any significance in itself, but reveals a glimpse of the order of creation, pointing forward to the order of redemption."
- Quotes a Leisegang on the notion that apocalypticism and Gnosis "cannot simply be described as dualistic or monistic, since the structure of htese thought patterns is incompatible with modern philosophical terminology. One would have to say that the structure is both dualistic and monistic. The starting point, and at the same time final goal, of Gnostic thinking is the union of opposites, the *coincidentia oppositorum*..." But disagrees that this is imcompatible with modern philosophy: it is a forerunner to Hegel and Marx. "The *dialectic* is both dualistic and monistic. The particular way of thinking and the unique concept of development of apocalypticism and Gnosis is in fact the dialectical method which reconciles the opposition of thesis and antithesis into synthesis within the course of history."But this logic "is not circular but spiral. The 'bending backward' characteristic of the dialectic does not progress back to the thesis in a circular manner but broadens out into a spiral toward the synthesis." 34-5
	- Emphasizes the theological formation of Hegel, who apparently patterned his early writings on the New Testament to escape Aristotelian logic 
- "An important Gnostic source renders the dramatic, eschatological pattern of apocalypticism in the from of a programmatic questioning that holds for all of Gnosis: salvation will come on the recognition of 'who we were, what we became; where we were, where we were thrown into; where we are hastening to, from what we are redeemed; what birth is, what rebirth is.'" Quote is from the *Excerpta ex Theodoto.* 37

From Daniel to John
- Opening lines: "Apocalypticism and classical antiquity are base on mutually contradictory axioms. What is considered to be reality in one domain is taken to be vanity and semblance in the other." The first model of Apocalypticism is the Book of Daniel, written precisely to protest the Hellenization of Judea under the Seleucid king Antiochus Epiphanes. 43
	- Daniel is notable because even in this earliest incarnation "transcendent eschatology and national messianism are already inextricably intertwined." It will never be possible to separate political messianism from the "transcendent apocalypse." 45
- John and Jesus: There survive fragments of anti-Christian tracts by followers of John, which see Jesus as misleading the flock by dissolving Moses' laws. Jesus never explains the "Kingdom of God" because he added nothing new to the content of expectations regarding it; he was novel in promising that it was to come imminently. But Jesus does not "bring" the Kingdom; it is a motive force which has already overcome Satan in Heaven, such that he now "rages" with his remaining powers on Earth. The idea of possession and exorcism is potentized in the Gospels; "the era of Jesus is itself possessed, as in *besessen*, with the power of Satan. To be sure, the possessed have existed everywhere and at all times, but not all ages have been demonized ages."
- Sees a long-term move in Antiquity away from "nature" (and collective culture, divine law, etc) towards the Self, which brings with it a desire to transcend the self. As a result Christianity takes root by offering a "superego," which "is one and the same in each member of the community, so that the community represents a collective of the spirit (*das pneumatische Wir*)". "It outshines and devalues the Caesarian superego." In fact this Caesarian superego is not wholly unlike the Messianic one; "what Nero has in common with Christianity is his loathing of the world and its people... basically, they are just individual features of the contempt and hatred which the era feels for itself." But only the Christian path offers salves this hatred by offering an escape from the world. "For nobody wants to exchange forever the substance of their being for a corporate identity (*Sammel-Ichs*), equally devoid of substance, whose feasting and circuses are no less degrading for the individual than are its brutalities." 65  

Part III
- Muntzer on tedium: "The pathway leads from wonder through faith into tedium, the torment of spiritual emptiness (*innere Oede*) of the soul. This journey through the torment of hell is like encroaching waters, which whirl up from the depths to engulf the ego. 'When man, tossed and moving in the wild sea, becomes aware of his origin, he must act like a fish which has followed the dirty water downward. It then turns round, swims, climbs back up through the water so that it can reach the place from which it originated. That is what I call tedium (*Langweil*)'. This stage of tedium or emptiness is what both the individual and the community have to go through in order to be renewed. 'It is also necessary for people to be severely punished for slovenly wantonness, for passing their time voluptuously, so that they lack the courage to engage in serious contemplations about their faith. Therefore, very few of them have any experience of the first stirrings of the spirit. Because of this they mock the experience of tedium (*Langweil*), which however is the only way we find the work of God.'" 115  
- "The Lutheran Reformation places all emphasis on individual eschatology because it abandons society to the world. There is sharp condemnation of the Anabaptist tendencies of chiliasm in the Augsburg declaration. It is expressly dismissed as a Jewish doctrine, as was chiliasm in the early Church at the Council of Ephesus; the Kingdom of Christ is taught to be a purely spiritual matter." 120
