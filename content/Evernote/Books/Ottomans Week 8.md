Krstic - Illuminated by the Light of Islam

* Opening question: "What accounts for the appearance of Ottoman self-narratives of conversion starting in the second half of the sixteenth century? Several Ottomanists have already pointed to a marked surge in autobiographical narratives in Ottoman Turkish beginning in the second half of the sixteenth century and especially in the seventeenth century... But which developments in particular influenced the emergence of the Ottoman self-narratives of conversion? Is it a coincidence that they appear at the same time when Catholics and Protestants, in the aftermath of the Council of Trent (1545–1563), resorted to polemical self-narratives by converts as principal tools of proselytization and theological dispute?" 36
	* Cites: Kafadar, "Self and Others"
* Krstic's answer: the rise of conversion narratives was "a manifestation of the Ottoman participation in the age of "confessionalization." 37
	* Specifically, these narratives emerged out of "trans-regional confessional polarization enhanced by competing imperialisms in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, between Ottomans and Habsburgs on one hand and between Ottomans and Safavids on the other." In particular, in the second half of the sixteenth century, "when it became clear that neither side could decisively defeat the other or their respective internal "others," the Hapsburg, Ottoman, and Safavid territories underwent the "mature articulation" of parallel "imperial identities and religious orthodoxies," as part of an "intense traffic in people and ideas" in the early modern Mediterranean.
	* Messianism as an early phase _before_ a final victory of one party proved impossible?
* "while devsirme recruits and voluntary converts of noble descent were central to the development of the Ottoman state in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, they represented only a small number of overall converts to Islam during the Ottoman period." 42
* "From Stefan Gerlach’s diary that spans the years 1573–1578, we know that Murad was assigned to be an interpreter to the Habsburg embassy in Istanbul, where he had a chance to discuss Christian theology with Lutherans, such as Gerlach, and even anti-Trinitarians, like the infamous Adam Neuser, who fled to the Ottoman Empire, converted to Islam, and became the inspirational leader of a sizeable circle of German converts to Islam in the Ottoman capital." 48
* "In addition to humanist and Reformed spiritual ideals, Murad’s account echoes other developments of the era, most notably the intense inter-imperial competition. Murad refers to the Western prophecies about the demise of the Ottoman dynasty, which was supposedly going to transpire in the reign of Su ̈leyman as the twelfth ruler of the Ottoman line.56 In response to Christian prophecies, Murad upholds the claim of the Ottoman sultan to be the messianic world conqueror (sa ̄hib-kıra ̄n).  " 50
* "Although the Ottomans did not institute an Inquisition like the Habsburgs, Su ̈leyman dedicated special attention to the transgressions traditionally associ- ated with Safavid Shi‘a supporters known as the kızılbas ̧ (the red heads) and radical messianism. While prior to Su ̈leyman’s reign the Ottoman Empire was quite ecumenical when it came to the variety of Muslim beliefs, it now wit- nessed a boom in heresy trials and concerted efforts to define the boundaries of belief." 51

Terzioglu ー Sunnitization

* Cites Fleischer, "Lawgiver" as the "seminal article" on the maturation of Ottoman Sunni identity after the abandonment of universalist projects of world domination.
* Cites Schelling, Reinhard, and Krstic on confessionalization.
* Characterizes Krstic as "operating essentially within Schilling's model," and as seeing the sunnitization of the 16th century as a top-down state project, but that of the 17th of a bottom-up "confessionalization-from-below." 305
* The similarities between Sufism and Shiism "were accentuated, when a broad cross-section of Sufi groups, some of which were evolving into _silsila-tarikats_, partook in the surge of Alid loyalty as well as of messianic expectations during the 14th and 15th centuries." Citing Mazzaoui and Bashir. 307
* Argument: "the emerging "Sunni orthodoxy" of the early 16th century was not merely an "invention" of statesmen to aggrandize Ottoman state power and to bolster its legitimacy in the face of the Safavid ideological challenge. It was also, at least in part, the result of a more gradual process of ulema empowerment and the spread of what one might term "Islamic literacy" in the Ottoman lands." 309
* Süleyman I ordered the construction of hundreds of Friday mosques, and attendance was mandatory for all Muslims. Simultaneously, imams and muezzins were empowered as "semi-public officials who represented the local communities before the state officials and vice-versa" in a "development that closely paralleled the rise of parish-based social disciplining efforts in early modern Europe." 315
* In general Ottoman Sunnism of the late 16th and 17th centuries showed "a greater expectation of confessional exactitude in matters of doctrine and ritual as well as a heightened concern with social discipline in the broadest sense." 318
* A new phase of sunnitization after the 1630s, with calming of conflict with Safavids and end of persecution of Kizilbash.

**Karakaya-Stump - The Kizilbash/Alevis in Ottoman Anatolia - Chapter 6: From Persecution to Confessionalisation**

* Summarizing goal of previous chapters: "recovering the Sufi genealogies of the Kizilbash," which "overturns the conventional narratives of "otherness" and "parochialism" heaped upon \[them\]... and allows us to re-envision the Kizilbash milieu as transregional netwrok of convents, dervish groups and _sayyid_ families, all with their own constituencies... who rallied around Safavid leadership on the basis of a set of Sufi ideas and institutions." 256
* This chapter's goal: "to examine the transformation of Kizilbashism from a proselytising, revolutionary movement into a quietist religious order of closed communities with a distinct confessional identity." = Kizilbash confessionalisation
* "The concept of confessionalisation, in the sense of elite-driven pro- cesses of confessional boundary-making and identity enforcement, was originally coined and employed by historians of Reformation Germany. More recently, a group of Ottomanist historians have used it to study in a more integrated fashion the widely recognised but imprecisely defined nexus of changing Ottoman imperial discourse and Sunnitisation policies during the sixteenth century and beyond." Parallels between early modern Ottoman context, Shi'itisation in Iran, and "analogous developments within Europe." 256
	* Footnotes cite: Zeeden, Oestreich, Schilling + Reinhard, and Ronnie Hsia for initial confessionalisation paradigm
	* Kristic and Terzioglu on Ottoman confessionalisation
	* "It is also true that the original confessionalisation theory as proposed by Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard links it to processes of state-building in the early modern period; they argue that the official promotion and enforcement within a given ter- ritory of a particular denomination helped produce overlapping religious and political domains, and served as an instrument of social discipline, religious homogenisation and political centralisation.5 However, Schilling and Reinhard’s original confessionalisation model has been challenged and reshaped on various fronts. Among other things, critics have faulted the model for its state-centric, top–down focus and lack of attention to non-state actors in promoting confessional self-discipline." 258
	* K's model of confessionalisation is "looser" and refers to "the development of internally coherent and externally exclusive confessional communities through social disciplining," with less emphasis on texts and state building. 258
* Chapter arguments:
	* 1\. " The Ottoman state’s persecutory impulse against the Kizilbash, persisting as it did even when the latter’s early political radicalism had largely ebbed, cannot be reduced to a direct and inevitable outcome of the Ottomans’ rivalry with the nascent Shiʿi Safavid state. Instead, it has to be understood in the broader context of imperial confessional politics, which involved Sufism as a key site of conflict and negotiation."
	* 2\. "The Kizilbash communities were not simply victims of the confessional age, but were – in their own way – also engaged in confessionalisation. Within this context, the historical experience of persecution, or the threat thereof, served as a unifying force binding closer together the diverse groups who made up the Kizilbash/Alevi milieu, thus strengthening group cohesiveness and internal solidarity as well as intensifying their differentiation from the rest of the Islamic polity."
	* The novel development of the early modern period for the Middle East was not the split between Sunni and Shia as such, but the emergence of a powerful Shia state "which, in turn, was both a result and a driving force of the escalating polarisation between the two confessions." 258
		* This followed a "period of relative confessional rap- prochement within the framework of Sufism, as indicated by the growing preponderance of ʿAlidism as a suprasectarian orientation in post-Mongol Islamdom."
* Before the reign of Selim, Anatolian sympathizers with the Safavids were termed "Sufis;" the term Kizilbash was introduced as part of a "confessional turn" as the Ottoman polity transformed into one that "was more homogenous in its religious outlook and ideologically more closely integrated" with a Sunni social base. 262
* The Shakulu Uprising of 1511 was not directly organized by the Safavids, and Shakulu was even hailed as a messiah in his own right by his followers.
	* Many of the initial followers of Shakulu were lower-ranking timariots whose fiefs were being taken away by government policy favoring devsirme recruits; many of these abandoned the uprising once their timars were restored.
* The earliest Ottoman measures targeting the East Anatolian Kizilbash were aimed at preventing movement into Iran, and predated the Shakulu Uprising by 10 years. However, Bayezid II was broadly conciliatory towards Shiites in his territories, and made overtures to bring them into the fold of sympathetic Sufi orders. By contrast, Prince Selim was openly hostile to the Safavids and Kizilbash from the beginning.
	* "As prince-governor of Trabzon, he gave shelter at his court to members of the Akkoyunlu elite, who were escaping Safavid conquests and the attending persecution of Sunni Muslims. These Sunni émigrés from Iran would later emerge as key agents in the process by which a vicious anti-Kizilbasah progranda was engineered and promoted in the empire." 267
	* The only Kizilbash uprising which was directly connected to Shah Ismail was that of Nur Ali Halife in 1512. K sees this as a case of Ismail supporting Price Ahmed against Selim in the Ottoman civil war, rather than part of a plot to conquer Anatolia
* Sees Selim's hostility to the Safavids and Kizilbash as part of a strategy to present himself as a gazi and an ambitious conquerer worthy of the throne, and in direct competition for the loyalties of rootless Anatolian soldiers who might otherwise go over to the Safavid cause; interprets Selim's raids into Georgia as a way to strengthen this reputation in competition both for the fighting men of east Anatolia and for the Porte.
	* Once Selim became sultan, his initial anti-Kizilbash/Safavid stance combined with his ambitions to become a global conquerer and heir to the Akkoyunlu heritage. "Such grandiose attributions to Selim were also fully in tune with the chiliastic fever of the early sixteenth century that manifested itself in the political arena as competing claims of universal rulership. The widespread sense of the immanency of the apocalypse, which influenced the political vision and rhetoric of virtually all great empire-builders in Eurasia at the time, must have not only facilitated the authentication and internalisation of the religious mission attributed to Selim by his support- ers, but also created a persisting need to project and promote an imperial Sunni cult.  " 272-73
* The persecution of the Kizilbash was at first part of the universalist imperial ideology developed to legitimate Selim's rule, and then part of the state's commitment to sharia as "the linchpin of moral and political unity in the empire."
* As part of preparations for war, the state circulated fatwas against the Kizilbash, calling for execution even for those who repented and ordering total social mobilization against the heretics. "This call on all self-professed Muslims for active cooperation in the fight against the Kizilbash amounted to a vision of total mobilisation of the populace and was symptomatic of the powerful disciplinary forces this persecutory process unleashed on the entire society." 278
* Says the process of Ottoman confessionalization really took off following the Amasya Treaty of 1555 - no citation but this is a claim Krstic makes as well.
