Coercion and Capital

Intro

* Basic program: Coercion of populace and preparation for war were central to development of state structures in Europe; and “relations among states, especially through war and preparation for war, strongly affected the entire process of state formation.” Thus the important variables for histories of state formation are 1. Concentration of capital 2. Concentration of coercion 3. Preparation for war 4. Position within international system.
	* Rulers’ attempts to coerce resources and manpower from their populations depended for success on the preexisting social organization of their states; thus “the relative success of difference extractive strategies, and the strategies rulers actually applied, therefore varied significantly from coercion-intensive to capital-intensive regions.” 15
	* Important point: “No one designed the principal components of national states — treasuries, courts, central administrations and so on. They usually formed as more or less inadvertent by-products of efforts to carry out more immediate tasks, especially the creation and support of armed force.” Example of Richelieu sending out personal agents to bypass tax collectors in order to raise revenues for 30 Yrs War. Immediate needs are more explanatory than political philosophy of rulers, although the latter may suggest alternative solutions to challenging problems when “tradition” fails. “Only in faulty retrospect do we imagine the intendants as deliberately designed instruments of Absolutism.” 26
	* Both interactions with other states (especially warfare + peace treaties) and “struggle and bargaining with different classes in the subject population” significantly affected the path of change for each European state. Impossible to think of governments acting outside these constraints

4. States and their Citizens
	* Major differentiating factor between state formations is which powerful classes were able to bargain out major concessions and even inclusion in state structure: “On the whole, officials of states that grew up near the network of trading cities stretching from northern Italy to Flanders and the Baltic found themselves near the first extreme, bargaining with municipal oligarchies that had their way, survived, and became major components of the state... Agents of states-in-the-making that formed outside the city-state band more often found themselves bargaining with great landlords and their clientele and creating new representative institutions in the process. In those larger states, nobles often gained confirmations of their privileges... in return for their collaboration with royal attempts to build national armies. **But all along the continuum bargaining over the state’s extractive claims produced rights, privileges, and protective institutions that had not previously existed.**” Church officials had a different pattern of inclusion or defeat: “England’s Henry VII stripped his country’s church of its lands and its ties to Rome, but thereby took on the obligation to provide stipends forever to all priests who accepted his version of Reform.” 103
	* In case of French Revolution, counterrevolution did not necessarily become strongest in places most opposed to Paris, but in regions with powerful opposed blocs of supporters and opponents, especially the major cities of the South and West. This process began especially when the Revolutionaries attempted to directly eliminate the authority of nobles and priests, who then gathered their forces to resist.
	* “As national legislatures extended their own ranges well beyond the approval of taxation, they became the targets of claims from all well-organized groups whose interests the state could or did affect.Direct rule and mass national politics grew up together, and reinforced each other mightily.

5 - Lineages of the National State

* Cartesian schema of Low/High Integration with Hinterland and Low/High Position in Market Hierarchy determined the sort of power which predominated in Europe‘s cities. In regions with dense cities, states usually had to bargain with the capitalist classes of each individual city, leading to a more “fragmented sovereignty” than in regions of less market integration, where the dominant classes bargaining with the state were landholders.
	* “The broad urban column that reached roughly northwest from the Italian peninsula to southern England dominated the map of fragmented sovereignty, the zone of capitalist strength in state formation; as commerce on the Atlantic coast, the North Sea, and the Baltic gained importance, the column acted as a kind of percolator, pumping merchandise, capital, and urban population up from the Mediterranean and the various Easts to which it connected. Big, powerful national states formed chiefly at the edges of the urban column, where cities and their capital were accessible but not overwhelming. Father out, states occupied even larger territories, but until fairly late exercised only episodic control over the people and activities within them.” 133
