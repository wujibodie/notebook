Introduction 
Philosophy, as opposed to “world religions,” attempted at first wto create an ontology grounded on reason’s experience of itself. However, the idea of a worldview grounded purely on reflection has become untenable in light of modern science; thus philosophy converges towards a _theory of rationality_, i.e. a framework for the formal requirements of rational action. 

* “All attempts at discovering ultimate foundations, in which the intentions of First Philosophy live on, have broken down.” 2

”The situation is somewhat different in _political economy_ \[as opposed to political science\]. In the eighteenth century it entered into competition with rational natural law and brought out the independence of an action system held together through functions and not primarily through norms. As political economy, economics still held fast at the start to the relation to society as a whole that is characteristic of crisis theories... Economics as a specialized science has broken off that relation. Now it too concerns itself with the economy as a subsystem of society and absolves itself from questions of legitimacy. From this perspective it can tailor problems of rationality to considerations of economic equilibrium and questions of rational choice.” 3-4. 
By contrast, “sociology became the science of crisis _par excellence;_ it concerned itself above all with the anomic aspects of the dissolution of traditional social systems and the development of modern ones.” 4 

* Sociology’s focus on rationality (particularly the connections between rational _action_, rational _conduct of life,_ and rationalized _worldviews_) grows out of its origins in the attempt to explain the changes undergone in European communities as a result of capitalist modernization. Habermas defends sociology’s (especially Weber’s) choice to focus on rationality as more than just a contingent historical choice — there are methodological, metatheoretical, and empirical grounds for why sociology cannot escape the question of rationality. 

**Chapter 1 - “Rationality” - A Preliminary Specification**
Begins with a brief series of examples demonstrating that A, who makes a claim about the world in order to communicate an belief, and B, who takes an action expecting a certain result, are both “rational” insofar as their claims and expectations can be defended as valid.. Further, if A’s claim is about the results of the action which B undertakes, then they are operating on the same knowledge.

* Two possible starting points: rationality as “cognitive-instrumental rationality,” or as “communicative employment of propositional knowledge in assertions,” (communicative rationality, logos). 
* “Along both these lines the analysis of rationality can begin with the concepts of prepositional knowledge and the objective world; but the case differ in the way in which the knowledge is used. From one perspective the telos inherent in rationality appears to be _instrumental mastery_, from the other _communicative understanding._ Depending on which aspect is the focus of attention, our analysis can lead in different directions. The two positions may be briefly elucidated as follows. The first, which for the sake of simplicity I shall call “realistic,” starts from the ontological preposition of the world as the sum total of what is the case and clarifies the conditions of rational behavior on this basis. The other, which we can call the “phenomenological,” gives a transcendental twist to the question and reflects on the fact that those who behave rationally must themselves presuppose an objective world.” 11

Sums up the _social_ requirements of rationality/reasonableness as: “actions regulated by norms, expressive self-presentations, and also evaluative expressions, supplement constative speech acts in constituting a communicative practice which, against the background of a life world, is oriented to achieving, sustaining, and renewing consensus — and indeed a consensus that rests on the inter subjective recognition of criticizable validity claims. The rationality inherent in this practice is seen in the fact that a communicatively achieved agreement must be based in the end on reasons.” 17 Within a given community, the judgement of a person as “rational” rests on his/her speech and actions conforming to the consensus of valid claims and their interconnections (which claims can be grounded on which others).

* Rationality is judged in part by the actual success of the subject in making claims recognized as valid, as judged through _argumentation_, but also by that subject’s ability to learn from mistakes (“the refutation of hypotheses and the failure of interventions” 18).
* _Theoretical discourse_ is “the medium in which... negative experiences are assimilated,” as “the form of argumentation in which controversial truth claims are thematized.” 19
* ”Any explicit examination of controversial validity claims requires an exacting form of communication satisfying the conditions of argumentation. Argumentation makes possible behavior qualifying as rational in a specific sense, namely learning from explicit mistakes.” 22 

Excursus on argumentation. Focuses on two problems with most existing theories of argument: 1. They focus on the period of argument itself, but ignore the development of community norms which ground the judgements of validity made within and around a given dispute; 2. A failure to combine the spheres of argument (e.g. rhetoric (process), dialectic (procedure), and logic (products), following Aristotle) into a universal emic identity:  what disputants think they are doing when they argue, and how the apparent internal connections between statements in an argument map on to an external nomological structure. 

* Toulmin “general conceptual framework” of the logic of argumentation, as divided into “functionally specified contexts of action,” i.e. institutional instantiations of the general framework. However, the “universal” framework is not accessible to us; thus “the concept of rationality is said to be accessible only to an historically oriented empirical analysis of _change_ \[emphasis mine\] in rational enterprises.” 33-34 H takes Toulmin to task for not realizing that the same statements can have many different functions depending on context, and specifically for not separating “conventional claims, which are context-dependent,” from “universal validity claims.” According to H, the latter can only be used in situations of true “discourse,” which he limits to situations when participants “suppose that a rationally motivated agreement could in principle be achieved,” given enough time and open debate. 42
* Klein: “What people, dumb as they are, are doing when they argue.” Argues that the goal of argumentation is to use statements to “incentivize” agreement in particular listeners within a particular (relative) background of consensus validity. Reduces “logic of argumentation” to fixed regularities across instances of argumentation. Habermas’ critique: “As a consequence of this _restriction to the level of abstraction of rhetoric,_ the internal perspective of reconstructing validity connections is neglected. There is lacking a concept of rationality that would make it possible to establish internal relations between their standards and ours, between what is valid “for them” and what is valid “for us.”” Overly relativistic view of argument misses that arguers _themselves_ are attempting to make universally valid statements, and that any research must ultimately be concerned with how “validity” connects to “truth;” Habermas doesn’t want to fully throw away the idea of particular “validities” being analogous to truth. 

2. Mythical and Modern Ways of Understanding the World

    ”Rationality” as a concept is too “individualistic and unhistorical” to be useful to sociology; but even when used to judge an individual, it makes reference to _systematic_ tendencies across domains and across time; that is, “we also speak of the rationality of a _conduct of life_.” “And in the sociocultural conditions of such a conduct of life there is reflected perhaps the rationality of a life world shared not only be individuals but by collectives as well.” 43 Thus begins the study of communicative rationality, and the (collective, social) structures which make such a rationality possible. 
    Jumping-off point is a study of the “mythical understanding of the world,” which H insists does not require that adults are incapable of “logical operations” and “higher-level competences” equivalent to those seen in the modern West. “The rationality of worldviews is not measured in terms of logical and semantic properties but in terms of the formal-pragmatic basic concepts they place at the disposal of individuals for interpreting their world” (45). 

* The mythic worldview, per Levi-Strauss, works by uniting the perceptual surface of the world via analogies and contrasts. “Analogical thought weaves all appearances into a single network of correspondence, but its interpretations do not penetrate the surface of what can be grasped perceptually.” The connecting media between seemingly disparate appearances are “mythical powers,” and the existence of such powers renders the human use of magic plausible and possible (just requires us to supplicate agentic invisible powers or to find hidden correspondences which are already “there”). 

    From the modern perspective, “mythical” worldviews confuse nature and culture, objects and agents, teleological and communicative action. Thus the “demythologization of worldviews means the desocialization of nature and the denaturalization of society.” The “categorical distinction between \[these\] object domains” further depends on “a process of differentiation that can better be analyzed in terms of _basic attitudes about worlds._ “We make the correct conceptual separations between causal connections of nature and normative orders of society to the extent that we become conscious of the changes in perspective and attitude that we effect when we pass form observing or manipulating \[“the objective world of what is the case”\] to following or violating legitimate expectations” (49). Further, in the mythical “confusion” it is difficult to draw a line between the sign of speech and the referent about which we believe we are communicating, and similarly there is little distinction between semantic and causal connections; “there is not yet a precise concept for the non empirical validity we ascribe to symbolic expressions.” This further means that the linguistically constitu7ted worldview can not be “detached,” analyzed, and changed, since that linguistic order is reified along with relations that actually exist in the world. Rational communication requires “decoupling” of formal world-concepts and validity claims, so that each can be tested by the other. This decoupling also makes possible the separation between an external world and interiority, between objective and subjective. 

*    The presuppositions about the modern understanding of rationality revealed by a contrast with ‘the savage mind’ is not necessarily universal; it may still be “a reflection of the particular features of a culture stamped by science.” 53

    The question of the relativity of rationality became pressing in the late 19th century debates around the foundations of the _Geisteswissenschaften._ Failure of historicism’s inquiry into uniqueness or commensurability of civilizations/worldviews, partially because there was no radical confrontation with the question of whether the investigator’s own (rationalistic) assumptions were valid across domains. Claims anthropology picked this problem up again, and summarizes the debate in 6 parts.

* 1\. Can an investigator ask whether a research subject is acting irrationality, or is it plausible that they are acting under different standards of rationality? Consensus: “Truth,” “sincerity,” “authenticity” in general are not context-dependent, but only the connection between statements, actions, and beliefs. Thus the investigator must employ “hermeneutic severity” to his own presuppositions, and investigate the beliefs which underlie a subject’s words and actions; only then can the researcher be permitted to say that the subject acts irrationally _by their own standard._ 55
* 2\. Evans-Pritchard: Anthropologist must learn and recognize the logical consistencies of the culture he studies, but he can distinguish between statements which are logical and scientific and those which are logical and unscientific (i.e. based on truth claims which are not empirically tested and validated, but rather conventional or idiosyncratic). Winch: The notion of “science” itself rests on a particular worldview, and using it to interpret the beliefs of people holding a different worldview obstructs the understanding of that worldview. Concepts must be examined inside the linguistic system in which they appear.
* 3\. H objects: While it is true that worldviews are like portraits in that there can be multiple different but authentic or accurate representations of the same true “object,” they are unlike portraits in that worldviews themselves are what _make possible utterances that admit of truth_; they necessarily have an indirect relationship to truth, and so ask to be judged by their “cognitive adequacy,” measured “as a function of the true statements that are possible in this language system” (59). Anticipates counter-criticism that “worldviews are comparable only in respect to their potential for conferring meaning,” and since we can not fully inhabit two worldviews to the point of making this comparison, they therefore “structure forms of life that are _incommensurables in their value,”_ and cannot be reduced to this “cognitive adequacy.”

    Question of how rational worldview develop through learning processes; how do cultures come to choose rationalization? Starting from Piaget’s structural development in order to conceptualize the “devaluative shifts” which make certain kinds of reasons less acceptable as cultures change stages. 

* For Piaget, cognitive development occurs alongside/via the “construction of external and internal universes,” namely the delineation of objective/physical, moral/social, and internal worlds. Thus the formal reference systems of these three worlds become progressively differentiated. 
* In particular, an interaction between individual subjects creates a “totality in itself which creates new properties while transforming the individual in his mental structure;” the two subjects mirror to one another their subjective conceptions of all three worlds. “The function of the world-concepts, however, is to prevent the stock of what is common from dissolving in the steam of objectives repeatedly reflect in one another.” Help reach a common perspective. Subjects acting communicatively thus “always come to an understanding in the horizon of a lifeworld” and thus come to agree on their mutual situation.
* This lifeworld is developed over generations and serves as a store of collective experience over that time. “It is the conservative counterweight to the risk of disagreement that arises with every actual process of reaching understanding; for communicative actors can achieve an understanding only by way of taking yes/no positions on criticizable validity claims.” Critically, the more decentered this (conventional) worldview becomes, the more interpretative work needs to be done by interlocutors themselves, and thus “the more frequently we can expect rational action orientations.” 70 
	* “The more cultural traditions precede which validity claims… must be accepted, the less the participants themselves have the possibility of making explicit and examining the potential grounds on which their yes/no positions are based.”
* Based on the above, H gives a list of formal properties a cultural tradition must possess if “rational action orientations” are to be possible, and if they are able to “consolidate into a rational conduct of life”:
	* It must make available formal concepts for the objective, social, and subjective worlds, and correspondingly allow differentiated validity claims and attitudes (propositional - objectivating ; normative rightness - norm-conformative ; subjective truthfulness - expressive). 
	* It must permit a reflective relation to itself; the system itself must allow its individual pieces to be taken out and examined critically. “Cognitive activities of the second order emerge: learning processes guided by hypotheses and filtered through arguments in the domain of objectivating thought, moral-practical insight, and aesthetic perception.” 
	* It must permit feedback connections to domains of argumentative evaluation such that the learning processes can be socially institutionalized. Thus cultural subsystems arise (science, law, music, art, literature) in which tradition take shape in an environment of “permanent criticism” — “but at the same time professionally secured.” 
	* Finally, It must interpret the lifeworld such that “action oriented to success” can be decoupled from sociocultural preservation, communication, and understanding.
* The list above does not describe the “ideal” society, but merely provides one pole of a spectrum of “the success or failure of the endeavor to realize a form of life in which unconstrained individual identities, together with unconstrained reciprocity among individuals, become palpable reality” (Wellmer). A _critique_ of the problems Habermas thinks are caused by maximizing this tendency can only proceed after showing that “the decentration of world understanding and the rationalization of the life world are necessary conditions for an emancipated society.” Before then, we can not be sure whether the problems we see in European modernity are contingent historical expressions of European contradictions, or are necessary outcomes of hyperrationalization. 74

3. Relations to the World and Aspects of Rationality in Four Concepts of Action

“The concept of reaching an understanding suggests a rationally motivated agreement among participants that is measured against criticizable validity claims... These expressions can be more closely analyzed in two ways — with respect to how they can be defended and with respect to how actors relate through them to something in a world.” Proposes to analyze action concepts by way of “the relations between actor and world presupposed by each.”

*     Popper’s Three Worlds: 1. Physical objects and events; 2. Mental states and episodes; 3. Semantic contents of symbolic systems (scientific theories, propositions, works of art). 
	* Distinguishes between embodied and implicit semantic contents (i.e. meanings which have been “discovered” and recorded vs. those which merely could be discovered). “Unembodied” symbolic formations are both products of the human mind, and problems which it must confront through labor. In this sense the third world is _autonomous:_ it grows through human labor, but it cannot be mastered by any one individual or even all individuals, and all labor presents new problems, some of which are unsolvable. Overall the third world “encompasses the scientifically processed, cognitive components of the cultural tradition.” This focus on _science_ and _cognitive processing_ becomes a limitation for H. 
	* Jarvie argues that social actors produce theories through their everyday interactions with the world and others, and these theories in turn become objects which others encounter, test, and respond to in their own theorizing. H hates this. 3 objections: 1. Ignores the role of tradition in identifying situations + coordinating action — tradition is not problematized, merely applied; 2. Limits meaning-objects to cognitive interpretations; but H thinks “cultural values” are at least as important as “theories” for action. But if we assimilate norms and values to Popper’s 3rd world, it gets harder to see how this is the realm of “systems of knowledge” and “validity claims analogous to truth.” 3. Doesn’t separate culture values and institutional embodiment, i.e. doesn’t recognize the resistance and _coercion_ inherent in objective norms and institutions.
	* H suggests modifications to 3rd World theory: 1. Replace ontological “world” with phenomenological “world/lifeworld” pair. “Sociated subjects, when participating in cooperative processes of interpretation, themselves employ the concept of the world in an implicit way.” At times, culture serves as the shared lifeworld that forms the backdrop for communicative action (“unthematically given horizon”). At other times it can be made “thematic”/“problematic” and reflected upon critically. 2. Replace “objective mind” with “cultural knowledge” differentiated by validity claims. 3rd world thus includes several “spheres of validity,” some of which have to do with truth claims abt 1st world, others of which are nonrepresentational problem domains which obey internal regulations of rightness, normativity, etc, inspired by particular values. 3. Rather than seeing the 3 world as indices of one true “being,” they are indeed 3 ontologically distinct realms which form a “reference system that is mutually presupposed in communication processes” 84. It is not true that speakers use the second and third worlds to act on the first; rather all 3 are equally “primordial” — the “descriptive,” “signaling,” and “self-expressive” functions of speech “lie in one and the same evolutionary plane.” 
* Four basic action concepts. Corresponding ontological presuppositions of these 4 models reveal progressively “stronger implications for rationality” (87). In all cases the teleological structure is presupposed to a certain extent, but the latter three each suggest that structural changes occur in _social_ action as a result of the specific challenge of coordinating among individuals with their specific ends.
	* 1\. Teleological action: decision among alternative courses with a view to the realization of an end, guided by maxims, and based on an interpretation of the situation. Expanded into _strategic_ model when the actor must anticipate decisions of at least one other goal-oriented actor; this is basis of decision theory and game theory. Von Neumann. 
		* Presupposes relation between actor and objective world. Agent has “cognitive-volitional complex,” ie can form beliefs via perception and develop intentions with the aim of reaching certain states of affairs. There is one world, though potentially with many subjects possessing inner worlds. 
	* 2\. Normatively regulated action: Members of a social group orient action to common values. Members are entitled to expect that others will fulfill a given norm of action in a given situation. Basis for “role theory.” Durkheim, Parsons.
		* Two-world: The “objective” and the socially normative; actors have both a “cognitive” and a “motivational” complex. “Existing norms gain action-motivating force to the degree that the values embodied in them represent the standards” which the community would apply to a given situation. Actors simultaneously see the objectivating and the norm-conformative aspects of their situation. 
	* 3\. Dramaturgical action: Participants in an interaction constitute a public for one another and seek to evoke particular impressions of themselves by “more or less purposefully disclosing \[their\] subjectivity.” Actor controls and stylizes access to information to which he has privileged access, i.e. his subjective state. Goffman.
		* Two-world: Actors see the social world and their internal world, in their attempts to negotiate the information barrier between the two. Desires and feelings are paradigmatic; they serve as the two kinds of needs driving actors, the volitional (drive towards need satisfaction) and the intuitive (perceive situations in need of potential need satisfaction). 
	* 4\. Communicative action: 2 speaking actors establish interpersonal relations. Seek to reach understanding about a situation and plans of action in order to coordinate by way of agreement. _Interpretation_ is central to negotiating definitions which admit of consensus, and thus language is crucial. Mead, Garfinkel. “For both parties the interpretative task consists in incorporating the other’s interpretation of the situation into one’s own in such a way that in the revised version “his” external world and “my external world can — against the background of “our” lifeworld — be relativized in relation to “the” world, and the divergent situation definitions can be brought to coincide sufficiently” (100). 
		* In addition to actor-world relation, introduces the additional “linguistic medium.” All functions of language are taken into effect, and indeed operate simultaneously. One statement expresses propositional content, offers an interpersonal relationship, and shows the intention of the speaker (96). Thus, the only three-world model: all actors must judge the validity of an utterance’s claims wrt the objective, social, and subjective worlds. 
		* Intriguingly separates “operations,” which are part of the “infrastructure” which translates intention into actions according to “the rules of the game” (e.g. kicking the ball in order to score, picking up your knight on the way to hop the pawns), from “rational actions,” which are _motivated_ by ends that are not simply given as rules of symbol structure, and follow the conditions of actor-world relations (98). Thus “communicative utterances” are not to be analyzed at the level of grammar, but at a pragmatic level: what these utterances do to help involved actors “reach understanding.” 

4. The Problem of Understanding Meaning in the Social Sciences

* In social science, researchers are tasked with identifying an actors “subjectively purposive-rational” behavior, and also with comparing this with “a corresponding _objectively_ purposive-rational course of action.” This requires comparing how various actions “would have gone,” given knowledge of circumstances (including intentions of all involved) that probably exceeds that of the actor. Normatively-regulated actions can be “rationally interpreted,” from the subjective standpoint of whether the agent was justified in thinking his behavior accorded with given norms, and from the objective standpoint of whether (either) the norms he believes he is following are worthy, or whether it is sensible to follow norms at all (interesting that H feels compelled to include this). Similarly, in the “dramaturgical” model the interpreter can judge whether the actor is deceiving others or himself; H thinks that although interpretation of unconscious motives is difficult, “therapeutic critique” shows that it is possible (though he admits this is debatable). In any case, communicative actions always require an interpretation that is rational in approach; “access to the object domain of social action through the understanding of meaning itself makes the rationality problematic _unavoidable”_ (106). This is so because, in communication (as opposed to other models of action), success depends on whether participants themselves can agree on an “inter-subjectively valid appraisal of their relations to the world.” There is a “rational infrastructure of action oriented to reaching understanding,” and this structure must somehow be represented in the understanding of an interpreter.  
	* “The more clearly an action corresponds to the objectively purposive-rational one, the less we need additional _psychological_ considerations to explain it” (103).
* The Dilthey-Husserl-Heidegger-Gadamer tradition on one hand, and methodological debates w/in the social sciences on the other, have come to similar conclusions about _Verstehen_ and _Verständigung._ Objects encountered by a social scientist embody the pretheoretical structures of knowledge by which acting subjects produced them. “The problem of _Verstehen_ is of methodological importance... because the scientist cannot gain access to a symbolically prestructured reality through _observation_ alone, and because _Sinnverstehen_ cannot be methodologically brought under control in the same way as can observation in the course of experimentation” (108). The scientist must describe something to which he belongs, as he cannot understand without in some sense belonging — “in order to understand them, he must in principle be able to participate in their production; and participation presupposes that one belongs.”
	* The notion of a fully “empiricist” take on Geisteswissenschaften sank after the debate on science represented by Kuhn, Popper, Lakatos, Feyerabend, summarized by Hesse: “data are not detachable from theory, and.. their expression is permeated by theoretical categories; language of theoretical science irreducibly metaphorical and unformalizable; logic of science = circular interpretation, reinterpretation, and self-correction of data in terms of theory, theory in terms of data.” Social sciences differ in requiring a double hermeneutic; both theory construction and the understanding of basic data require it, because everyday experience is symbolically structured and requires interpretation to be translated into “data.”
	* Skjervheim, Winch, Bernstein. Basic thesis: Understanding “meaning” is itself a mode of experience which requires establishing an intersubjective relation. Meaning is accessible only “from the inside;” “the lifeworld is open only to subjects who make use of their competence to speak and act;” interpretation of meaning thus necessarily means entering communicative action (111-112). But how can the objectivity of understanding be reconciled with the _performative_ attitude of a communicating ego? Further, is it possible to separate _understanding_ the meaning of an utterance, and _reacting_ to the validity claim contained therein? 
	* Because researcher is participating from a different “action structure,” that of science, he does not have ends of his own besides participation; he attempts to be a “virtual participant.” Even so, when watching debate among communicating actors, “the _description_ of reasons demands _eo ipso_ an _evaluation,”_ because a reason is such as cannot be described without reactions of affirmation, negation, or abstention; and this evaluation must be made by the observer’s own standards of judgment. Thus “there is a fundamental connection between understanding communicative actions and constructing rational interpretations” (116).
	* Criticizes the teleological, normative, and dramaturgical theories of action as ensuring “a methodologically relevant gap between the level of the action interpretation and that of the interpreted action,” since we are not ascribing to the actors the same communicative-interpretive intent and capacity as we as researchers bring to bear. But when we use the theory of “communicative action,” “our own ontological presuppositions are no longer more complex than those we ascribe to the actors themselves;” the subjects of research are now capable of mutual criticism. Thus using any of the prior models requires describing action independent of rational interpretation; they become useful as abstractions, “stressing only one aspect of the complex of interaction proceeding by way of validity claims.” 120
* Reflections of ethnomethodology and hermeneutics on question of how natural everyday hermeneutics are connected to social scientific interpretation lead to conclusion that researcher’s participation in communicative circle makes it possible to reflexively critique and transcend the self-understanding of that circle; purely because communicative action always contains this potential for critique. 
	* Schutz gives 3 methodological decisions: 1. Social reality is understood as a “construction of the everyday world issuing from the interpretive accomplishments of those directly participating.” 2. “In order to explain human actions the scientist has to ask what model of an individual mind can be constructed and what typical contents must be attributed to it in order to explain the observed facts as the result of the activity of such a mind in an understandable relation.” 3. The explanations of a given lifeworld postulated by a scientist must “link up” with the pretheoretical concepts in which members interpret their situation. Solution seems to lie with the “theoretical attitude,” which allows the researcher to suspend the value system of “everyday life” and particularize the object of study within an academic value system; H dislikes this, and suggests that the researcher instead needs to find the conditions of objectivity within the structures of communication that he enters; must judge for himself whether, given the conditions of his understanding, he can reflexively consider his own role. 
	* Ethnomethodology starts from the belief that communicative actors recreate “momentary consensus” with the appearance of normativity with each sequence of interaction, as they grope from one problematic context to another. Indexicality is used to establish connection to concrete context. The trouble is that this applies to social scientists as well, who are forced to fabricate contexts to interpret documents. “The observer necessarily imputes an underlying pattern that serves as the essential context for seeing what situation and actions are, while these same situations and actions are a necessary resource for seeing what the context is” (126). 3 solutions:
		* Radical self-application: Interpretive sciences must give up the claim to produce objective knowledge. Theory becomes a reflexive activity like art or religion, and truth is something “methodically conferred” as the “upshot of contingent courses of linguistic, conceptual, and social behavior.” 
		* Trivialization: Either the consequences of this context-dependency problem are harmless, or it is a “pragmatic research question” which can be modified as merely a “question of degree.” It may thus be possible to use this insight to produce a more objective social science. 
		* Change of paradigm: Research focuses on “common sense knowledge” and the “routine grounds of everyday activities” in order to discover “_the invariant features of the interpretive procedures used by participants in communicative action_” (127). Looking for universal features of reference systems, e.g. narrative organization of temporal sequences. However, H thinks ethnomethodologists largely fail to secure their methods for investigating this, bc they are unwilling to either claim special knowledge a la Husserl, or to transcend “everyday interactions” from within. H blames an unwillingness to consider the “structure of action oriented to validity claim” as more than “mere phenomena.” This is because Garfinkel treats “rationality” the same as all other conventions. H is irritated by the implication that ethnomethodological theory somehow stands outside the limits imposed on all other “conventions.” H again insists Garfinkel must accept the universality of “the validity basis of speech,” and thus the universality of “rationality,” or else be shackled to absolutist or relativist solipsism. Only VBS will allow the researcher, in participating as an equal, to elevate a given interaction to self-awareness. 
	* Philosophical hermeneutics, rather than focusing on “microprocesses” of normal situational interpretation, looks at interpretation as an “exceptional accomplishment” which is necessary only when the “culturally stable background” of the lifeworld breaks down, and implicit understanding becomes problematic. **Strong relevance to my thoughts on decision-making.** Example of reading a traditional text: the more one reads, the more one suspects a gap between one’s own concerns and context, and those of the author. Interpreter differentiates his own and author’s thought, then gains access to author’s lifeworld. This means we must ask “why?”: “Only to the extent that the interpreter grasp the _reasons_ that allow the author’s utterances to appear as _rational_ does he understand what the author could have _meant_. Gadamer holds that “understanding means primarily to understand the subject matter and only secondarily to isolate and understand another’s opinion as such” (134). Summary of philosophical hermeneutic position on 135. Understanding means to correctly know under which conditions a given statement would be valid, but it does not mean agreement as such. 
	* In summary, the question of understanding in sociology necessarily leads to a perspective of rational participants in communication which centers on basic validity of certain claims. This means that sociology’s theory of rationality must be sufficiently “universalistic” that theory-formation is not limited by the particular notion of rationality given to Western modernity by History. Objectivity in social sciences requires an objective notion of rationality. 
	* Three ways to proceed in finding a universal theory of rationality:
		* A formal-pragmatic reconstruction of universal rules of speech “oriented to reaching understanding.”
		* Second, empirical assessment of the usefulness of formal-pragmatic insights. Research into “pathological patterns of communication,” in order to establish “normalcy conditions;” into anthropogenesis, to find structures of rational communicative action developing in course of hominization. Third, developmental psychology could sketch out universal acquisition of above formally described structures. 
		* Third, historical-sociological research into development of theories of rationalization. 

II. Max Weber’s Theory of Rationalization

* In his history of religion, Weber employs a complex concept of rationality in showing how the internal conditions for “rationality’s” development were fulfilled in the West; but in his societal history he limits himself to Zweckrationalität, a concept shared with Marx (Adorno). Criticizes Weber, Marx, and Adorno for comparing an empirical societal rationalization and an incompatible ideal rationality. Reason is confusion between individual action-theory and systems-theory: “the rationalization of action orientations and lifeworld structures is not the same as the expansion of “rationality,” that is, the complexity of action systems” (145). 
	* Condorcet: “that nature has set no term to the perfection of human faculties; that the perfectibility of man is truly indefinite; and that the progress of this perfectibility… has no limit other than the duration of the globe upon which nature has cast us.” _Sketch for a Historical Picture of Progress of the Human Mind_, 179
