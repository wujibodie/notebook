Book One
In the clash between the Portuguese "empire," Asian "pedlars," and Protestant "companies," the eventual victory of the Companies is best understand by reference to the latters' better control of the market and better internalization of protection costs.
Major theoretical problem: how to understand the economic role of "protection?" Starting from Polanyi's argument that the "supply-demand-price" market was produced in the 18th/19th centuries, the problem becomes how the 17th C Indian Ocean operated and how the Companies intervened in this operation; suggestion that, quite besides innovations in European market organization, the Companies succeeded in introducing a unique degree of price stability which rested on their ability to protect shipping. Conflict bw two "market forms" mediated by violence

* Frederick Lane: Protection as permanent and organized violence which keeps "highwaymen" at bay. 2 concepts: tribute (income transferred exceeding production costs of protection) and protection rent (difference in profits b/w two entrepreneurs with different protection costs); the "protection producer" essentially sells the service of certain and stabletransaction costs. Can be seen as either entrepreneur (selling his protection at other than market rates) or monopolist (able to push prices to the substitution frontier). Steensgaard attempts to combine these perspectives; protection producers attempt to redistribute profits through violence, but a "dynamic element" is introduced by option of substitution (for example, merchants shifting trade routes) (18-19).

Chapter One: The Peddling Trade

* Follows Van Leur in defining the international trade of SEA as "a small-scale peddling trade," where traders shipped small but high-value cargoes with the monsoons, either independently, in small companies, or on _commenda_.
	* Takes Hovhannes as the prototypical peddler and the example for the rest. Argues that while the "technique" of Hovhannes overland travels is "primitive," the organization is sophisticated; many moving parts and well-defined partnerships are involved, and Hovhannes is able to remit, loan, and lend at different times.
	* The European overland merchants active around the same time worked with similar amounts of capital, but were less inclined to directly enter the zones of production, preferring to stick to market centers for buying as well as selling.
	* Sophisticated organizational forms (commenda, bills of exchange), but no "comprehensive coordinated organizations" like the Fuggers. The "spreading" of trade among individual entrepreneurs is the crucial difference re: the Companies.
	* In general customs duties were more expensive than transport costs -> preference for overland over maritime trade among individual merchants
	* “All this presupposes a rational attitude. But why not? In the literature on modern capitalism great emphasis has been laid on "the spirit of capitalism": the rational calculation of the possibilities of making a profit or a loss. But perhaps this emphasis upon a mental attitude is putting the cart before the horse. The pedlar might well have possessed the habit of thinking rationally, but he had no possibility of making a rational calculation of his costs in the modern sense so long as the protection costs and the risk remained unpredictable and the market non-transparent.” 59
* Chapter Two: The Redistributive Enterprises
* Persia successfully maintained the safest roads along the whole caravan route; in exchange merchants paid relatively small and steady levies at each sentry station.
* The Ottoman Empire was widely seen as being in decline by 1620; both contemporary commentators and Steensgaard date the decline to around 1584, when the currency was devalued and prices rose due to an influx of silver and insufficient state attempts to regain fiscal stability; as a result fiefs were liquidated, the army shifted towards mercenary fighters, no taxes increased. Memorial by Koja Beg.
* views the Portuguese as seeking only violent monopoly, ie redistribution of profits, rather than anything like open commerce. Warrior mentality dominating over mercantile interests. This means that the Portuguese did business on the “Asian pattern” and did not introduce anything new.
* By the late 16th Century the Portuguese had fully abandoned the idea of blocking the Red Sea trade in favor of issuing cartazes to almost all traders moving from Diu or Malabar to the Red Sea, although Acehnese ships were outside Portuguese control (meaning what?)

Chapter Three - The Companies

* Concludes that the English EIC, which always resisted royal intervention and maintained its character as an organization seeking shareholder profit, was only interested in commercial profit and not in “customs collection” or violent capture of wealth. Even the few exceptional cases of English offensives from the time, like the Red Sea blockade of 1612 and the capture of Hormuz, were meant to make up profit shortfalls and did not end with the occupation of territory or the establishment of settled customs duties.
* by contrast, while the Dutch voorkompagnien were genuinely shareholder-oriented capital-increasing enterprises, after their reorganization as the VOC in 1603 commercial interests were partially subordinated to raison d’etat - hybrid form between the Estado and the EIC. Not least the VOC was ordered to attack Iberian positions: “but there is no doubt that those circles, who, in the establishment of the United Company, had seen the creation of an instrument of power for use in the fight against Spain-Portugal immediately took the initiative.” 132
	* Thinks the political turn of the VOC was partially responsible for its success in developing a large stock of capital which permanently circulated in Asia, as it was able to issue relatively small dividends as long as shareholders knew that its funds were financing wars backed by the state.
	* “The important factor, however, is that the merging of the Republic's and the stockholders' interests that took place at the union of the Com- panies in 1602 as a consequence of the pressure of the Republic, made possible a conservative dividend policy as well as the accumulation of reserves in Asia, but it did not on the other hand alter the Company's fundamental nature of a business. The yardstick of success was not victory or conquest, but the financial results.“ 137
	* Ultimately the Dutch success is explained by the Company’s hybrid character: a business first and foremost, but one allowed to suck up extraordinary investments and hold on to a large circulating capital due to its political commitments.
* Companies and Market: The India Companies innovated with regard to stock formation and interventions in European prices. The VOC held huge warehouses of cloves and pepper, which they released slowly while they held a monopoly to raise prices, but dumped all at once when they were threatened by competitors. 142-143
* Major institutional innovations of the companies were the fact that they provided their own protection, I.e. internalized protection costs and avoided paying tribute to separate actors, and their efforts towards establishing price stability in Europe. **While they were never able to achieve complete monopoly control, they could reduce the unknowns in their calculations much more successfully than the peddling entrepreneurs.**

Chapter IV - The Fall of Hormuz

* Basic problem: the Portuguese carrack trade neither impacted overall European consumption nor killed off the overland caravan trade, but the Northwestern India Companies did both. When did this “structural crisis” come about and how deep were its effects?
* Shows that in the years after 1600 European trans-Cape imports from Asia more than tripled, and that this must be explained as a victory of the companies over the caravan trade more than by growth in overall consumption. Pepper was imported by English and Dutch ships to the Levant and Italy by 1620. Silk was the only commodity where “peddlers” successfully defended a market share of about one half.  (Role of the Persian state, and specifically Shah Abbas, in keeping the silk trade out of the hands of the Companies; commercial alliances between "carracks" and "caravans")
	* Suggests that the main groups to suffer from the loss of transit trade where those who specialized in caravan protection, eg customs officials and tribal chiefs who controlled important routes.
* Levantine trade in the  years from 1600-1625 were characterized by a slow shift in European strength from Venice to England and France, who could better protect their ships and had more access to silver coin, and the decline of Aleppo and Alexandria (old centers of spice trade) to Smyrna (focused on exports of Persian silk and imports from Europeans)
* Chief cause of Hormuz’s fall was the Captain’s increasing monopoly on trade and neglect of all other matters than their own profit; this in turn was a consequence of the Crown’s policy of triennial captaincies and sale of offices, necessary to raise funds for war against the Dutch and incentivize people to take far-off posts. “The King has known about these conditions for several decades, yet he can do nothing about it because the redistributive system, the triennial periods of office, the patronage and the venality of offices, in short: those institutions that safeguarded the practice of offices being used as sources of personal income, were in fact Estado da India. Should these institutions be abolished there would no longer be any reason for remaining faithful to the hierarchy in the distant outposts along the Asian coasts.”
* Further the early 1600s saw a vicious cycle where Portuguese reverses and a general decline in the Gulf trade after the entry of the Companies, without a corresponding decrease in extortionate customs duties, pushed merchants to seek other routes than the one passing through Hormuz. High protection costs relative to realizable profits without protection actually being provided -> peddlers take their trade overland via Kandahar.

Part Three: After the Fall

* Silk: The english attempted to gain special export privileges for Persian silk, but were ultimately unsuccessful at building up a monopoly and eventually gave up; meanwhile the Dutch purchased silk on the market and built up a substantial trade, which however never matched the Levant trade in terms of European imports. Main effect was to provide the VOC with an important source of income in years when pepper’s price was low; another sign of the companies’ flexibility and attention to market conditions.

From his interview with Blussé:
"I took the word peddlar from Van Leur, because it was there in the literature. This peddlar was obviously not a man who was walking around with a knapsack with a little bit of this and a little bit of that. But he did not keep a large stock of goods. He had to sell them within the monsoon. That was Van Leur's peddlar, and mine too. He had to sell, if he did not want to lose a year. That was another kind of market than the market of the companies. It is so obvious to me. There is however a problem with the word peddlar, because Asian historians feel insulted by it. It is not meant that way because most European trade in this period might be characterized as peddlars' trade as well. But the monsoons gave an extra emphasis on this short range of planning in the Asian trade. So if there is a better word for the same concept I would be perfectly satisfied. The term is not meant to be insulting but it points to a certain structure, an empirical market which existed in a certain place in a certain time."
