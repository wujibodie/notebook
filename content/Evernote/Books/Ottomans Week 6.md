**Faroqhi**

* \[In 1982\] "the history of Ottoman transportation as a whole still remains to be written;" previous works, e.g. by Cengiz Orhonlu, have looked only from the POV of the administration.
* Celali rebellions as time when merchant safety was compromised, lasting until the 18th century.
* “Although transportation by camel became an important feature of pre Ottoman and Ottoman Anatolia, wheeled transportation never disappeared, as it did for the most part in Syria and Egypt.” 524
* Basic research question is **the relative costs of camels vs. wheeled transport** at different times and places in Anatolia; suggestion of taking large chunks of time (100-150 years) to study discontinuities, rather than assuming that the use of these two forms of transport was uniform across the pre-rail period.
* Sources: the only sources dealing specifically with camels are government surveys of the camels available to either be levied from localities, or raised in royal stables. Camel-raising also comes up incidentally in documents dealing with land/housing sales (where camel stables might be included) as well as theft and rural banditry.
* Seasonality: nomads raised camels for their own needs, but mostly used them during the spring and early (?) summer; they tended to rent them out during the fall and winter in order to raise cash to pay taxes, and the urgency of this need sometimes led them to force their “services” on neighbors who did not really need them.
* “Tavernier remarks that caravans passing from the Ottoman Empire to Iran spent about three weeks in Erzurum, both for the purpose of customs inspection and to hire new camels.'4 But at the same time, merchants probably made use of the opportunity to sell off certain goods and buy others, so that transportation costs decreased relative to total turnover. From the camel drivers' point of view, this arrangement also possessed the advantage that they might more easily find a merchant who would require their services for the return trip.” 526
	* Major transport centers as places where camel caravans were dissolved and reformed, along with customs payments and transactions.
* When the Ottoman state needed camels for supply lines during war, they would either use camels from state stables, in which case rooms at provincial _han_ would be requisitioned for stabling, or they would hire camels, in which case the costs would be shouldered by provincial taxpayers. Camels could also be requisitioned for war at state-set prices via the _mübayaa_ system. Evidence suggests both that the prices paid by the state were below market rates, and that _mübayasir_s tended to abuse their authority in various ways.
* “even at the artificially lowered transportation prices paid by the Ottoman central administration, grain transportation of this type must have cost several times as much as the purchase price of the grain in question. Only extra-economic constraint, as exercised by the powerful Ottoman state, could make overland transportation of grain from Payas or from Manisa to Erzurum a reality.” 531
* In sixteenth century Anatolia, certain nomadic groups (some identified as “Tatars”) preferred to use (ox-drawn?) wagons as their main means of transportation; while wagons were not normally part of merchant caravans in Anatolia, they frequently appeared for this purpose in the Balkans, especially in the traffic with Poland, where Armenian merchants in Lwow organized large wagon caravans to the Ottoman Empire. 532
* In the early seventeenth century, there seem to have existed wagon trails linking Eastern Anatolia to Syria and Van, probably established for the transport of cannon to the front of the war with Persia; these were also available for non-military use, as in the case of a high functionary’s widow who was provided with horses for a wagon trip to Damascus.
* General conclusion: the Ottoman road system was probably in better shape around the mid-sixteenth century, and wagon transport was thus more common, than two hundred years later. The choice between camel or wheeled transport had to do with a number of factors related to cost, road maintenance, and ability to provision from local transport networks, that shifted over the course of Ottoman history, without a clear direction of “progress” or “decline.” 536

**Mikhail - Unleashing the Beast**

* Begins by situating himself within "the current literature on the history of human-animal interactions." Connects the question of human-animal relations to the issue of capitalist agriculture, and the Brenner and Great Divergence debates specifically.
	* "To fully under- stand the transformations of agrarian societies, we must therefore understand the historically significant causes and consequences of the shift away from a world characterized primarily by intense human-animal interactions."
	* Sees the transformations of Egypt in the decades around 1800 as a clear example of the sorts of processes discussed by Brenner and Pommeranz, as the "massive accumulation of land by a small group of rural leaders" led to "the most centralized government in centuries," followed by wars of expansion, infrastructural and institutional investment, and rapid urbanization. "Why all or any of this happened has proven to be one of the most vexing and pressing questions facing historians of Ottoman Egypt. As Brenner, Pomeranz, Richards, and many others make clear, similar processes of political and social transformation and of the commercialization of early modern agrarian economies occurred across the globe in this period." 319-320
	* **"The Egyptian example is therefore instructive in our efforts to understand these worldwide phenomena because it illuminates the specific mechanisms through which rural economies transitioned away from animal labor."**
* Three traditional explanations for Egyptian transformations, each critiqued as outmoded "great man" or "teleological" narratives:
	* The French Expedition of 1798-1801 (Marshall Hodgson 1974; Albert Hourani 1962)
	* Mehmet Ali as "the founder of modern Egypt" (Henry Dodwell 1931).
	* A newer explanation which "seeks explanations derived from longer, more structural processes," according to which Egyptian merchants, intellectuals, and "religious figures" developed the hallmarks of capitalism and Enlightenment before any external influence (Peter Gran 1979). 
* Mikhail's alternative: "There is in fact a rather different conclusion about the period from 1780 to 1810 that can be drawn from an engagement with three distinct though clearly interrelated historiographies: environmental histories of the human-animal relationship, the literature on transitions from subsistence to commercial agriculture in the early modern period, and Ottoman economic and social history." "The changing economic status of domesticated animals during the decades between 1780 and 1810" now emerges as central to the broader transformation of Egypt. 321
* Basic crux of this narrative is that, beginning in 1780, Egypt suffered massive die-off of animals, which forced the rural Egyptian elite to shift to more expansive landholding. They thereafter began to make up the deficit of laboring animals through "corvée of the Egyptian peasantry" in a fundamental transformation in the energy regime of Ottoman Egypt." 322
	* This transition from animal to human power was further crucial in "the transition from a primarily agrarian subsistence economy to a market-driven commercial economy." 324
* Animal labor as fundamental to Egypt's role as major breadbasket of the Ottoman empire; livestock as the major form of capital for people in rural Ottoman Egypt. Animals could be part-owned, with "shares" representing portions of produce (milk) or time being bought and sold. The buffalo was usually the most valuable animal in the Egyptian countryside, with the horse being second.
* Animals were the key form of wealth of rich members of society, and were vital in the running of capital-intensive agriculture: "The exceptionally rich Bedouin shaykh and amir Humam ibn Yusuf ibn Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Humam ibn Subayh ibn Sıbıh al-Hawwarı of Upper Egypt (d. 1769), for example, employed more than 12,000 head of cattle in the cultivation of sugarcane on his estate.52 In addition to this impressive number, he used cattle to thresh grain, power mills, and turn waterwheels; and his estate included many water buffalo and dairy cows as well."
* A series of plagues and droughts led to a collapse in animal populations along the Nile between 1784-1792. As most infrastructure was built for animal, not human labor, peasants found it difficult to replace their beasts of burden, while the prices for animals grew unaffordably high.
* "Egypt’s human and animal populations had of course been wiped out before by disease, environmental destruction, and war, but what made the late-eighteenth-century demographic shifts both different from previous instances of population decline and fundamentally constitutive of later periods were the several preceding decades of attempts by provincial elites in Egypt and throughout the empire to pull away from the Ottoman imperial administration." 337
* The new elite seeking to increase their autonomous hold on power bought up the land and animals of impoverished peasants, while many of those same peasants either became wage laborers on these estates or moved to swell the urban populations. These new estates began to invest in more complicated irrigation etc schemes with a view to commercial production, for which human workers were more suitable than animals.
* "Thus, Egypt’s economic transformation from subsistence to commercial agriculture was fundamentally based on the replacement of animals by land as the largest and most basic source of rural wealth." 340
* Cuno, _The Pasha's Peasants,_ as key reference on growth of rural elites in 18th C Egypt. Cuno shows that "market relations and commercial agriculture began to emerge before the middle of the nineteenth century," but without exploring the movement from animals to land.
	* To what extent was "commercial agriculture" really absent in 16th-18th century Ottoman Egypt? What would be the differences between the "breadbasket" Egypt of the earlier period and the "commercialized" Egypt of the latter one? How did the "early modern" state provision supplies from Egyptian producers -- cash payments?
* Movement from "usufruct" to "landholding" is complete when, in 1812, Mehmet Ali seized all of Egypt's _iltizam_s, "making the legal ownership of land the primary means of staking claims to land." 341
* "Indeed, between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries, as a general phenomenon, small- scale peasant labor moved from being under the local supervision and utilization of village tax farmers to being controlled by the centralized, more distant, and more abstract bureaucracy of Mehmet Ali. This shift away from the local to the centrally bureaucratic is embodied in the response of a group of peasants in May 1814—two years after the abolition of the tax farming system—to their local tax farmer’s de- mands of labor from them: “Your days are over, and we have become the pasha’s \[Mehmet Ali’s\] peasants.” 343
* Identification of the "human-animal laboring nexus" as having existed for "millennia," with corvée as fundamentally new to the 19th century. "Moreover, because corvée was so different from the human-animal laboring nexus that had existed in Egypt for millennia before the nineteenth century, and because it was used so extensively throughout Egypt, it had massive effects on the social and economic structure of rural people’s everyday lives."
* Concludes by putting the processes experienced by Egypt in comparison with other regions: "What happened in rural Ottoman Egypt as the eighteenth century gave way to the nineteenth also occurred in other early modern agrarian economies between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries. From Qing China to southern England, as land became concentrated in fewer and fewer hands, rural economies moved from subsistence to commercial agriculture." 348
	* But doesn't the comparison to other regions tend to contradict the specific cause posited by Mikhail (Egypt's plagues and famines in the 1780s-90s) as these factors were presumably missing elsewhere?

**Husain, Changes in the Euphrates**

* Basic focus: an avulsion of the Euphrates and its social consequences: "Between 1687 and 1702, these timeless, ever-recurring regional and seasonal changes were eclipsed by intense ecological disturbances that transformed the Euphrates’ hydraulic architecture. A dramatic rupture in the river’s flow occurred when a large segment of it, approximately 100 miles in length, escaped its established channel and gushed into a new one." 1
* Beginnings of the Euphrates' avulsion lie in a previously under-noticed drought in the Anatolian highlands in 1687-8, during a low point in global temperature. 
	* Chroniclers noted that the river's flow slowed down in that year, leading to sedimentation and overspilling.
* In 1688, in response to weak flow, one Sheikh Diyab broke a Euphrates levee to help fill his canals, which was burst through in 1689, leading to the Euphrates bifurcating into two even weaker branches. The state's ability to respond was weakened by a severe plague which struck Baghdad and Basra (in connection with the flight of refugees from famine upstream) in 1690-91, after which the tribal leader Sheikh Mani seized Basra from the local governor. Severe fiscal pressures also contributed to the choice to ignore the bifurcation of the Euphrates for the time being.
	* It was only after 1700 that the central state returned to the issue of redirecting the Euphrates, at which point it began requisitioning wood and other materials from upstream (Maras, Raqqa, Malatya, Hilla, and Aleppo).
* The campsite for the (mostly military) laborers on the new levee project was supplied via river with a fully stocked marketplace, with sufficient food and pleasures for all 4,000 workers. The old riverbed was successfully reclaimed, but trouble was had in damming up the new channel (even with the help of "a few thousand oxen") (17). The camp itself was overrun in a flood, and the project was abandoned on March 30, 1702.
* The area which suffered the most from the redirection of the Euphrates, around the Rumahiyya fort, was the most profitable agricultural region in the province, and specialized in stockbreeding: "The complex mosaic of weeds, stubble, artificial canals, freshwater marshes, and seasonally filled depressions created by the Euphrates provided extensive pasturage for a considerable sheep population, three times larger than Diyala’s, and offered natural wallows for water buffalo;" the result of this was the empowerment of pastoralists, who could resist the Ottoman state at times of weakness, as happened after the Ruhmahiyya fort was abandoned following the redirection of the river. 20-21
	* After 1700, the Khaza'il tribe of the Rumahiyya region declared independence south of the (new) Euphrates; the Khaza'il were primarily buffalo breeders, which distinguished them from the (mainly shepherding) tribes of the drylands nearby.

Dolbee, Empire on the Edge

* In 1890, two Ottoman administrators proposed to reform the desert peripheries of the Iraqi and Syrian provinces into a "Desert province" which formed a "natural governing point," with a goal of eventual "environmental transformation through effective administration." 130
* Participation of the Ottoman state in a broader global trend of "denigration of arid environments as wasteland and their mobile peoples as unproductive and savage." A vision of creating a settled, extensively irrigated society 132
* Strong influence of James Scott: "Nevertheless, in works like James C. Scott’s _The Art of Not Being Governed_, people flee into upland Southeast Asia from “state space” into “nonstate space,” with the environment seeming to function as a means of escape from politics." 135
	* However, for Dolbee, nomads did not "escape" into the desert so much as they "operated... on the edge," moving across borders to their own advantage.
* In the nineteenth century, the dominant tribal group in the Jazira were the Shammar, who migrated there from Najd following the rise of the Wahhabis.
* The Shammar both challenged "settled" society and were integrated into it: "But both uses could obscure the degree to which those who practiced nomadic pastoralism were never as rigidly separate from cultivation as such characterizations might imply.43 The term _aşiret_ in Ottoman or ʿ_ashīra_ in Arabic— denoting “tribe” or “confederation”—was a category of Ottoman statecraft and **thus derived meaning from governance**."
	* Connection to the literature on state constitution of named ethno-religious groups in "corporatization." See Bulow and Meier on the same tribe
* The Ottoman state reconsidered the Jazira in light of loss of other territories (Egypt, Greece): "The dilemma made the empire, in the words of historian Chris Gratien, a “_contracting_ settler state,” its expansion into the edges of the Jazira driven by losses elsewhere." 140
	* The Ottomans settled Chechen refugees from Russian wars in the northern Jazira, hoping to denomadize the region.
	* "One report suggested that an Ottoman official had visited the Chechen settlement at Ras al-Ayn and told them, “There is a great deal of sport in the desert; I don’t mean gazelles and hares but camels, horses and sheep.”80 He was referring, of course, to the livestock that constituted the lifeblood of the Shammar. According to this story, the official concluded his visit by telling the Chechens, “You can amuse yourselves as you like.” Reports emerged subsequently of a raid against the Shammar." 143
* The two brothers leading the two main branches of the Shammar tribe experienced state reforms in very different ways: one, Farhan, became an Ottoman official as the leader of a branch which agreed to settle in a new district near Baghdad, while the other, Abd al-Karim, led a rebellion against attempts to redraw boundaries of lands previously under Shammar control.
* Lasting relevance of Ottoman attempts to redraw the borders of the desert: "Ottoman provincial borders were of course different from post-Ottoman borders, but at the same time, the Shammar split between Iraq and Syria actually had roots in Ottoman efforts to divide the Shammar between Mosul and Zor several decades before. Whether the Shammar and other groups were constrained by the borders associated with Sykes-Picot (or found ways to take advantage of them), they did so as a variation on the Otto- man legacy of provincial borders." 155
