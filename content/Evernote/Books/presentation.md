1. Question of how group boundaries are modulated; what are the logics which our different authors (especially M+N) deploy in explaining shifts in Christian attitudes towards Jews and Muslims?
2. Meyerson, page 3 of his introduction: Compares _convivencia_ to the _dhimmah_ contract, but: "There was a crucial difference between the Christian and Islamic systems. Whereas the _dhimmah_ contract was sanctioned by revelation and was therefore universally applicable and essentially stable, the Christian system was based primarily on a series of surrender treaties and compacts concluded between Christian monarchs and individual minority communities, and was consequentially more subject to change." Goes on to say that "because tolerance was institutional, an artificial government creation, it by no means guaranteed a harmonious intermingling of religious groups." A latent hostility -- sense of enemy relation in war -- exists as a crucial aspect of coexistence even when there is a great amount of mutual interaction. We are set up to believe that the differences between Isabel and Fernando’s policies should both be understood in reference to this background hostility; but Fernando’s sponsorship complicates simplistic view of zcatholic monarchs seeking out religious unity as key motive in itself; so what ultimately separates the two? Shows that the potential tensions resulting from frontier raiding and warfare between Aragon and Muslim polities was largely kept from spilling into large-scale violence within Valencia b/c the majority-minority cohabitation established by the 15th C. was materially beneficial to both the King and the mudejars themselves.
	1. Relationship between ethnic violence and war: Case of 1455 riot against the moreria of Valencia after threats that the sultan of Granada will invade; tensions are more powerful among the Christian "public," especially urban poor, than with the nobility/government.
	2. Meyerson's ending asks: to what extent was popular Christian violence against Mudejars inspired by or connected to the royal rhetoric of crusade? Suggestion that both the 1455 and 1521 riots against mudejars were largely linked to socio-economic grievances, in the latter case w/ anti-noble rebels even targeting them as natural allies of the landholding elite; religious antagonism is brought "to the fore" by economic stress. And the reason for Fernando holding out on the forced conversion/expulsion of the mudejars is also seen as largely economic in nature.

Nirenberg: Begins by stressing the various uses of sexual metaphors (God as both father-begetter and husband) in establishing the boundaries of the Christian community; seemingly drawing a long-term continuity in Spain of Catholic theology and "honor" society. Three phases: 1. relatively socially permissive medieval understanding, where "policing" sexual boundaries was left as a duty for prostitutes (why?); 2. The birth of anxiety to distinguish "old" from "new" Christians and both from Jews after 1391, and emergence of concern with sex "in the very first moments of mass conversion;" eventually a move towards spatial segregation with the preaching of St. Vincent, who mobilized anxieties about sex between Christians and Jews to argue in favor of the complete separation of the two communities, as "prophylactic heightening... of physical distance"; concern is still "social proximity." 3. After the "generation of 1391," a new fear of insincere or unorthodox _conversos_ is tracked by a "reproductive" fear that Jewish blood will damage the "breeding stock;" discourse of _limpieza de sangre_ alongside the Inquisition.  Although sexual discourse tracks political developments, Nirenberg sees it as not being intended as a way to pressure more conversions -- abjures the idea that these ideas were manipulated for a specific end rather than reflecting broader concerns. Incidents of sexual scandal are also not seen as directly causing the shift in understandings outlined above; it was the anxiety about boundaries resulting from mass conversions which did (1078). Causality lies with the need to distinguish groups after boundaries become blurrier
	1. More focus on Jews than Muslims, especially in the later case: why? Ofc 1391 plays a crucial role in the narrative of change, but were there no corresponding shifts in attitudes towards Muslim sexuality, either in response to conversions or war?
	2. Frequently mentions that Jews and Muslims policed their group boundaries in similar ways as Christians; how so? If true, how does it change our understanding of the causes of this sort of behavior, since it can't just be due to Christian theology or something like the consolidation of hegemony by a dominant group; and why guard against inter-community sex in particular?

Digression: the case of the Chinese in Manila: the non-Christian Chinese are segregated almost immediately from wider life of Manila, but that segregation is frequently made porous by Spanish greed; arguments to re-segregate the Chinese into the parian, when they aren't inspired by direct threats of rebellion or invasion, cite sexuality as a key factor; "sodomy" particularly suspect, even among supposed Christians. Accusations of sodomy and fears of "corrupting the morals" of the local people are constant, but only become an impetus to action when socio-political environment is right; in 1672 Manila receives a dressing-down from Queen Mariana for extraditing two Chinese men convicted of sodomy to Taiwan instead of executing them, due to diplomatic agreement with the Zheng regime on Taiwan, whereas in the 1680s, immediately after the destruction of the Zheng in 1683, "sodomy" becomes one of the key charges mustered in the ultimately successful argument by a set of priests in favor of permanently enforcing a population limit of 6,000 Christian and pagan Chinese in Manila.
		1. Digression to digression: sumptuary regulations become crucial in distinguishing "sincere" Chinese converts from insincere ones, as people who are unwilling to cut their hair in Spanish fashion are seen as harboring intentions of returning to China; and both major "Chinese" polities attached equal or greater importance to hair and clothing in demonstrating loyalty (Ricci nearly being executed for having cut hair). Worth remembering too that both the Ming and Qing were if anything more anxious to keep foreigners separate from their own people as the Spanish were, but developed their own forms of compromise with economic gain over the years (Macao, Canton system).
