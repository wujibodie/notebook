Thick description

* “So, there are three characteristics of ethnographic description: it is in­terpretive; what it is interpretive of is the flow of social discourse; and the interpreting involved consists in trying to rescue the "said" of such discourse from its perishing occasions and fix it in perusable terms.”
* “To look at the symbolic dimensions of social action, art, religion, ideology, science, law, morality, common sense, is not to turn away from the existential dilemmas of life for some empyrean realm of de­-emotionalized forms; it is to plunge into the midst of them. The essential vocation of interpretive anthropology is not to answer our deepest ques­tions, but to make available to us answers that others, guarding other sheep in other valleys, have given, and thus to include them in the con­sultable record of what man has said.“

Religion

* Culture: “an historically transmitted pattern of meanings embodied in sym­bols, a system of inherited conceptions expressed in symbolic forms by means of which men communicate, perpetuate, and develop their knowledge about and attitudes toward life.” Meaning is the dominant concept of “our time” and semiotics the most promising theoretical trend.
* Basic theory of the “sacred symbol” is one which synthesizes a people’s “ethos” and “world view.” “In reli­ gious belief and practice a group's ethos is rendered intellectually reasonable by being shown to represent a way of life ideally adapted to the actual state of affairs the world view describes, while the world view is rendered emotionally convincing by being presented as an image of an actual state of affairs peculiarly well-arranged to accommodate such a way of life.” Religion thus makes ethical judgements and objective structure of the world mutually supporting.
	* Religion is thus “1) a system of symbols which acts to (2) establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods and motivations in men by (3) formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and (4) clothing these conceptions with such an aura offactuality that (5) the moods and motivations seem uniquely real­ istic .”
* Distinction between models _for_ and models _of;_ cultural patterns have both aspects.
	* “The perception of the structural congruence between one set of processes, activities, relations, entities, and so on, and an­ other set for which it acts as a program, so that the program can be taken as a representation, or conception-a symbol--of the pro­ grammed, is the essenceI of human thought. The intertransposability of models for and models of which symbolic formulation makes possible is the distinctive characteristic of our mentality.”
* “But perhaps the most important difference, so far as we are concerned, between moods and motivations is that motivations are "made meaningful" with reference to the ends toward which they are conceived to conduce, whereas moods are "made meaningful" with ref­ erence to the conditions from which they are conceived to spring. We interpret motives in terms of their consummations, but we interpret moods in terms of their sources.”

Talal Asad, Anthropological Conceptions of Religion

* Geertz's understanding omits the role of power in structuring religious beliefs, and comes from a standpoint characteristic of modern Western societies where religion has been "displaced" and given a role separated off from the rest of social life. The historical study of religion's relationship to power in the case of early Christianity shows that there can be no universal definition of religion.
* Geertz begins by separating religion, as a system of symbols, from active participation in life. Symbols become external impetuses which enter into peoples' consciousnesses; this leaves aside the question of where the symbols come from, or what social processes lead to these specific symbols being reproduced or granted authority.
	* mentions Vygotsky's theory that children's intellectual development depends on "internalization of social speech;" i.e. formation of a child's "symbols" is conditioned by the social relations to which it is exposed.
* Geertz understands religious symbols by reference to their efficacy in producing certain "moods and motivations" which persist into daily life; but what connects given symbols to a character of truth which gives them the power to move men? Answer: power, or rather, structures which enforce those symbols' truthfulness.
	* Gives example of Augustine's focus of _disciplina_ and _per molestias eruditio_: men have to be brought to the "choice" of faith by a process of painful "softening-up." In other words: "It was not the mind that moved spontaneously to religious truth, but power that imposed the conditions for experiencing that truth."
	* The role of "authenticating discourses" in determining which symbols and interpretations survive to seem authoritative. Example of the Catholic Church's role in accepting or rejecting potentially "pagan" or "heretical" practices and beliefs.
* The Church moved slowly towards recentering religious authority on individual "conviction" or "faith," but in doing so a need arose to define "religion" as opposed to the non-religious. Lord Herbert in the seventeenth century produced a definition of "Natural Religion" which supposedly applied to all societies, not just Christian ones. Association with global exploration: "Exploration and commerce had widened the horizon, and in many writers of the century one can see that the religions of the East, however imperfectly known, were beginning to press upon the European consciousness."
	* From this Asad takes the conclusion that religion was a concept developed to specifically define the limits of the Christian church, then generalized to understand all other cultures by way of analogy with it.
* In the end Geertz is not able to say much about religion's function except that it "affirms" some sort of answer towards the "Problem of Meaning" which makes a universal human condition appear more explicable or tolerable. But this makes the actual content of religious discourse, as well as the material/social conditions about which that discourse speaks, irrelevant; this reflects a privatized, secularized view of a religious "sphere," from a society where religion has lost the power to produce new authoritative discourses.
* In Geertz's discussion of the movement between the religious world and the world of common sense, Asad takes issue with Geertz's failure to consider that the _religious world_ might be affected by the _common sense world_ rather than only vice-versa. What about religious training, discipline, the work of developing religious bodies and minds, and of sorting out accepted from heterodox ideas?
* Instead of Geertz's separation between the religious and the everyday, Asad proposes that the starting point of anthropology of religion should be the "historically distinctive disciplines and forces" which produce the authority and content of religious behaviors, beliefs, symbols. Wants to overcome the distinction between "expressive action" and "technical action" by emphasizing the "technical actions" required to produce and reproduce specific religions.
	* "Such disciplines are preconditions for specific forms of thought and action, but they must be taught and learnt, and are therefore themselves dependent on a range of social institutions and material conditions."
* Ultimately, universal definitions of religion hinder the attempt to explain how religion is produced in specific cases because they direct attention towards essences (meanings!) instead of "concrete sets of historical relations and processes."
