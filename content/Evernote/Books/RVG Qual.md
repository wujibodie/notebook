Hartwell

* "The administrative history of China from the mid-T'ang through the early Ming is one of a progressive shift in the balance of power from the central government to large regional administrations. This was the cost of maintaining a minimal degree of efficiency in managing the daily affairs of an empire with vastly expanded areas of dense populations. The chief changes in governmental organization were: 1) an increase in the number and authority of the lowest level of administration, the district (hsien), and a decline in the importance of intermediate levels of government, the prefecture (_chou_ and _fu_), 2) the emergence of large regional administrations, the province (sheng), and 3) **a diminution in the direct influence of the central government on the routine management of the affairs of the empire**." 395
* The transformation of the political elite: Over the first 150 years of the Song, government office was dominated by two distinct groups, the "founding elite," who were descended from the allies of Song Taizu and mostly clustered in Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Hebei, especially around Luoyang and Kaifeng; and the "professional elite," a group of 10 influential clans with origins all over the empire but which resettled in Kaifeng and Luoyang, and which largely descended from elite bureaucratic families of the Tang and Five Dynasties periods. The second of these groups became more important at the expense of the first over time, until by 1086 not a single member of the "founding elite" families held high office.
	* "The combined effect of the continued dominance of the two groups of late T'ang elite families preserved the political dominance of the North for several generations after the economic and demographic transition of the South had been accomplished." It was only after the Shenzong reign (1068-85) that the South began to provide a quantity of high officials proportionate to its population. 414
	* "The social transformation of the Chinese bureaucratic elite at the end of the eleventh and the beginning of the twelfth century was far more than simply a demographic transition. It marked the disappearance of the professional elite as a cohesive status group made up of families who specialized in government service and the coming to the fore of a multitude of local gentry lineages who encouraged a division of labor among their progeny with government service as only one possible career choice." 416

Thorny Gates of Learning

* “The examination system occupied a critical nexus in Sung society. A complex institution, it served many interests and performed many functions, not merely of bureaucratic selection but also of elite advancement and representation, social and intellectual control, and imperial symbolism, to name just a few. Yet none of the studies mentioned above has focused upon **that conjunction between institution and society.** Such is the aim of this study.” 13
* Basic questions: “Did certain groups of regions benefit more than others from the examinations’ rules, and if so, why? Upon what theory or theories of education were the examinations predicated? And how did China’s elite culture adapt to the powerful organizing force of the examinations?” 13
* Chapter 2: The structure of recruitment
	* In general, through the Song degrees and kin privileges each provided roughly half of officials, with a few other paths (such as facilitated degrees for multiple-time test takers and purchasing of titles) providing a relatively small number. However, it seems that both facilitated degrees and protection provided more officials over time (Chaffee compares 1046 to 1213). 26
		* Of these, the facilitated degree holders were ordinarily of very low rank and often did not hold any office, but by the late 12th century increasing numbers made their way into high officialdom.
		* Protection privileges were normally extended liberally to the highest officials, and the factional conflict of the late Song meant that this privilege was at least briefly given to a much larger number of people than in the more settled past. “This made it virtually impossible for a family to perpetuate itself in high office over several generations… but it also meant that more families reached high office and thus gained, at least momentarily, the great protection privilege that that entailed. The net effect was therefore to increase protection even though no single family coupled have compared with some of the great Northern Sung families or lineages in their use of it.” 29
* Chapter 3: For the Utmost Governance
	* Points out that the examination system as it was established in the early Song was vulnerable to “Confucian” criticism on the basis that its impersonality risked creating a class of “alienated and selfish” men who only cared about “hollow fame.” 48
	* Song Taizu selected relatively few officials via examination; it was with Song Taizong’s exam of 977 that the latter standard of awarding 100-200 high degrees per year was instituted. 49
* Chapter 4: To Be Rooted in Schools
	* In 1043, Fan Zhongyan and Han Qi submitted a ten-point memorial for reforms to the imperial system, of which five dealt with the recruitment and advancement of officials. These proposals included the limitation on _yin_ privileges and the revision of the exams to focus on 論 and 策. Further reform proposals (to establish prefectural schools and to expand the capital University) were approved in the following year, before the entire set of reforms (known as the Qingli Reforms) was rescinded. The ideas of Fan Zhongyan and others became an inspiration to future reformers such as Wang Anshi, who also saw reform of the examination and education of officials as key to general moral reform.
	* The early reform movements attempted to deemphasize poetry in the examinations, while Wang Anshi's reforms removed the poetry component entirely. It was reinstated after the death of Shenzong, then removed again for the 30 years after 1094, before being permanently reinstalled. The main reason for poetry remaining a part of the exams despite almost all reformers arguing against it seems to have been simply that it was far more popular than specialization in classics among test-takers themselves; it was often seen as a better mark of true talent and cultivation than mere "expertise."
		* The zhuge degrees, by contrast, were permanently done away with, as part of the general preference for "generalism" in education as opposed to specialization.
	* Wang Anshi's reforms introduced the office of school preceptor, a figure who oversaw prefectural education along the lines of a centrally-mandated curriculum. 53 prefectures were assigned preceptors (out of 320), the majority of them in the north; Chaffee sees this as evidence that the South was seen as having a strong enough tradition of local education not to need assistance. 77
	* Cai Jing, Chancellor under Song Huizong, instituted a hierarchal system of "Three Hall" (three-grade) schools, reaching from primary to county, prefectural, and capital schools; the award of a _jinshi_ degree was accomplished by ascending through each grade, with the exam at the end being deemphasized. Altogether these schools educated 200,000 students at their peak, all of whom were provided room and board by the state. 78-80
		* One memorial of 1112 charged the administrators of these schools with systematically seeking profit, in particular by selling their stipend grain after "converting from real prices to market prices," as well as "borrowing from students" (to lend at interest?). 80
	* Although the Three Hall system collapsed after the loss of the North, Chaffee sees it as setting the stage for the expansion of the literati and the development of local schools, simply by providing education to a vastly greater number of people than before, however briefly. 83-84
* Chapter 6: The Geography of Success
	* "so great was the range in the examination performances of the circuits of southern China wthat we can divide them into two groups: successful and unsuccessful." Fujian, Liangzhe, Jiangnan, Zizhou, and Chengdu were successful, and together accounted for 84% of _jinshi_ in the Southern Song. This was true even though the density of schools was broadly similar across most circuits.
	* Two key questions about changes in examination success over time: first, why did the Lower Yangtze and Southeast Coast regions become so dominant despite having previously had almost no representation in the imperial bureaucracy; and second, why did the Zhe coast suddenly rise to being the most successful part of these successful regions in the Southern Song?
		* Follows Hugh Clark to suggest that Fujian developed a strong tradition of classical education under the Min kingdom; Quanzhou produced a _jinshi_ in the very first year after it joined the Song, and many of the elite bureaucratic clans of the Northern Song had origins in Fujian's wealthy maritime prefectures.
		* The Zhe coast probably followed on the flourishing maritime trade pioneered by Fujian and the Yangtze region, but also developed its own strong traditions of education, with a number of philosophical schools and academes rivaled only by Fujian.

Sukhee Lee, _Negotiated Power_

* Chapter One:  Elites, Locality, and State
	* Lee argues that Song era Mingzhou was characterized by an increasingly large “local elite” which depended for its status on connection to the state as one important pillar, though not as the sole basis for prominence. Argues against the notion that the examination system alienated elite families from the state by reducing their ability to reliably produce officials; new elite families emerged out of the examination system, while both new and old elites found other ways to strengthen their relationship with the state and each other besides office-holding.
	* “As long as we equate the state with the examination system and the offices it distributes through the system, then the divorce of the majority of the social elite from the state during this period si a logical conclusion. But is it accurate to view the premodern China state as merely an administrator of the examinations and a supplier of offices?”
		* Emphasizes importance of local administration, undertaken with participation of local prominent families
	* In the Northern Song Mingzhou produced 127 _jinshi_, ranking it 21st in the empire; in the Southern Song it produced 746 _jinshi_, behind only Fuzhou and Wenzhou, and its tax quota more than tripled in the same period, reflectingMingzhou’s post-1100 shift from “cultural periphery” to “flourishing center.” 30
	* The most prominent Mingzhou family were the Shis 史, who produced three Grand Chancellors, including 史彌遠, who served for 26 years and was known for favoring the advancement of Mingzhou men at court.
	* Both Lee and Chaffee cite this passage from Yuan Cai (a native of Quzhou, inland of Mingzhou): “士大夫之子弟苟無世祿可守·無常産可依而欲爲仰事俯育之資, 莫 如爲儒. 其才質之美能習進士業者, 上可以取科第致富貴, 次可以 開門敎授以受束脩之奉. 其不能習進士業者, 上可以事筆札代牋 簡之役, 次可以習點讀爲童蒙之師. 如不能爲儒, 則醫卜星相農圃 商賈伎術, 凡可以養生而不至於辱先者皆可爲也.”
	* Uses a large number of known marriages from Song Mingzhou to test the Hartwell-Hymes theory that the Northern Song elite followed a strategy of long-distance interprefectural marriages while the Southern Song elite preferred to strengthen local ties; Lee finds that in both the Northern and Southern Song periods Mingzhou elite families showed a preference to marry outside their own counties, but most frequently stayed within Mingzhou or the immediately neighboring prefectures; prominent migrant families had no difficulty immediately marrying into local society at any time. Meanwhile, a small number of families systematically practiced long-distance marriage in the Southern Song, including the famous Shi lineage, who preferred to marry with other elite officeholding families like the Wangs of Jinhua; in general Lee concludes that itinerant official careers substantially changed marriage patterns, with men who served even in relatively low positions outside their home prefectures often choosing to marry their daughters to acquaintances made in the bureaucracy.
	* Chapter conclusions:
		1. While the literati elite included families without officeholders, the pursuit of office (through exams or privilege) was universal and official position provided immediate practical benefits to one’s status within the locality; the goal of state service never disappeared as the chief aim of most elite families, even as more and more of them were forced to turn their attentions to other activities due to fierce competition.
		2. “Mingzhou was not a place where leading elite families found it easy to secure their position for a long time, even at the local level.” Few families remained prominent for more than a few generations, and by the early Ming an entirely different set of lineages dominated the region than during the Southern Song. 83-84
		3. There was no sea change in marriage patterns from Northern to Southern Song. Instead, Mingzhou families were always ready to pursue different strategies in their marriages, with both intracounty and long-distance marriages being arranged depending on individual circumstance. In general, officeholders were more likely to have meaningful contacts and interests outside Mingzhou, and thus tended more often to intermarry with outside families.
* Chapter Two: Local Governance
	
	* Basic argument: an interest in local government clearly grew over the course of the Southern Song, and the aim of the government shifted from the reshaping of society by state fiat to the “caretaking of local interests.” 87
	
	* It seems that military defense was not a major preoccupation of elite families in Mingzhou, with the only noted militia leading family leaving its martial past behind as it attempted to “gentrify” into the civil elite. Lee sees this as a result of efficient official administration of local defense.
		* From 1163 on, the Song naval forces based in Mingzhou (as many as 6000 men at a time) were under the direct command of the local administration.
		* The Mingzhou government also had the authority to build forts and naval bases, as well as to fund the salaries of soldiers based there, and did so in response to a broad variety of concerns, from bandit suppression to national-level coastal defense strategies.
	* Uses the example of the reconstruction of a floodgate in Fenghua to test the role of government vs. gentry in financing public works; finds that direct donations from prominent families accounted for 10% of the cost, while 40% came from direct govenrnment finance; the remaining 50% was "gotten from the people," probably meaning a levy on households nearest the floodworks.
		* "One might emphasize the financial restrictions of the local government, forced to rely on local people’s cooperation in meeting its expenses. On the other hand, one might instead stress the coercive administrative power of the county government to extract capital in excess of regular taxes. Neither interpretation, I think, is far from the truth. I would rather emphasize that this practice, based on a “beneficiary pays” framework, was enacted and carried out by the government to enhance local infrastructure." 104
	* There exists a debate in the literature on whether the Southern Song court aggressively centralized revenues at the expense of local governments and the people, or whether the central government’s “presence was virtually unfelt in local society” and checked by the locally powerful (Hymes). Either way, Lee seeks to explain how Mingzhou funded local administration, whether it was challenged by excessive centralization or a lack of state power. 126-128
		* One suggestion: the prefectural and county governments shifted responsibilities to the well-off families of the 保正 and 副正 (baojia heads and vice-heads). Zhu Xi claimed that these figures were exploited ruthlessly: “如修造廨舎, 迎送官員, 整葺祠宇, 置造軍器, 似此之類, 其名不 一. 竹木瓦磚, 油漆麻苧等物, 例以和買爲名, 不曾支給分文. 又如 役使工匠, 科差人夫, 勒出錢米, 陪備供輸, 椎剥肌髓, 至此爲甚.” But to Lee this is not a sufficient explanation.
		* Lee finds that of local revenues of around 1.2 million strings, only 74,000 strings remained for discretionary expenses after transfers to the center + fixed salaries were deducted. This was not enough for major projects, so the admin turned to the commercial economy, especially its monopoly on liquor. “The government either directly ran breweries or licensed local applicants to run some of them and collect tax. The unlicensed, private manufacturing and selling of liquor was strictly prohibited, and an exclusive selling district was set up for liquor produced from each of the officially recognized breweries. There were ten main breweries, which were also liquor tax offices, and as many as 121 licensed branch stores across Mingzhou around 1226. Under the monopoly system of the dynasty, however, the income from liquor was supposed to be under the direct control of the Ministry of Revenue and little was left for prefectural expenses.” After 1166, however, the prefecture operated two breweries under its sole control, with annual revenues of around 70,000 strings — doubling the surplus of tax revenues. 131
			* After the first prefectural breweries were established, later officials invested ever more into this source of funds, with such positive results that later administrations were even able to selectively abolish taxes.
* Chapter Three: Cooperation and Tension
	* Question of the definition of Mingzhou as an 義郡: “Hong Mai 洪邁 (1123–1202) provides a contemporary definition of yi, as in the compounds “charitable estates” (yitian 義田) and “charitable service” (yiyi), as meaning “sharing with other people” ( yu zhong gongzhi 與衆共之).5 Conrad Schiokauer and Robert Hymes argue, in their co-authored introduction to Ordering the World, that “yi came more and more in Southern Sung to be applied in contexts having to do with voluntary social action and semiformal governance at a level lying between family and state.” Question of how both families and state were involved in such “semi formal” public-minded activities. 152
		* Lee’s suggestion: “far from emerging from the retreat of the state, the local activism of the Southern Song, especially in affluent regions, was the product of converging interests of the state and local elites.” 153
	* Conclusions: while elites did show more interest in promoting "community building” ventures of direct relevance to their social status, such as schools and granaries, than in infrastructure, their role remained secondary to that of the prefectural and county administrators; thus "the rise and spread of local activism during the Southern Song period, at least in Mingzhou, was undergirded by the activism of local government." 198
* Conclusion
	* Overall, against the theory of a general "local shift" towards a landed gentry during the Southern Song, elite families in Mingzhou were highly dynamic and connected with other regions (e.g. in marriages); evidence suggests that "elites of Mingzhou adopted flexible strategies because being influential in local society may have demanded more than an uncompromising adherence to their locale, which leads to my final and perhaps most significant point about social elites during this period: the continued importance of the state in elite strategies." 266
	* Lee questions ideas that preparation for the exams became merely a "status marker" independent of expectations of actual government service; he sees the Southern Song literati as intimately tied to the state even when outside formal office.
		* Though landed wealth usually preceded examination success, the latter remained highly coveted because " one needed to translate his local wealth and power into something that was officially recognized, not simply because of one’s ideological respect for the state but also because of the extraordinarily practical benefits such recognition would bring." 267
	* "From the twelfth to the fourteenth century, Mingzhou was a place of opportunity. Opportunity generated interests; interests created tension; and tension triggered negotiation. In Mingzhou, both the state and the social elites wielded resources of power, a power that was constantly negotiated. Mingzhou’s colorful history shows that increasing awareness of the importance of this negotiation in local governance by the state and elites lay at the heart of the dynamics of the middle period of Chinese history." 273-274

Von Glahn, Song-Yuan-Ming Market Towns

* Previous scholarship has largely assumed a linear growth of market towns from the Song to the early Ming, which was arrested before resuming in the late Ming; the Yuan is often seen as a period of pause or decline in this growth. RVG argues that the early Ming instead saw a dramatic collapse of marketization in the Yangtze delta.
* Cotton was introduced to the Yangtze delta around the end of the 13th century, and quickly spread as a crop suitable for the sandy hills of the “Moors” region.
* Unlike European villages, which tended all to cluster around a church, only around 1 in 10 Jiangan villages had a substantial temple of any kind.
* Lay Buddhism started to spread, particularly following the model of Mao Ziyuan's White Lotus movement, from the 12th century. Temples and monasteries increasingly attracted enormous crowds for "Lotus assemblies" and festivals. Similar mass participation was seen in festivities at temples to chenghuang, Dongyue, and Tian Hou/Mazu.
	* "Lay Buddhist movements like the White Cloud and White Lotus were by no means an exclusively urban phenomenon, but their activities had a profound effect on the delta's market towns, many of which underwent prodigious growth because of the building projects initiated by these groups." 196

McDermott

* Seeks to understand the relationship between Huizhou merchants' commercial networks and their forms of kinship organization. "What kinship units and relationships did these merchants use for practicing their trade in the Yangzi valley? How were these units and relationships then woven together to form long-distance market networks stretching hundreds of miles among multiple centers of trade in the Yangzi Valley? And, thus more broadly, how did these kinship units -- family, branch, or lineage -- achieve such success and then over time maintain it, especially when they established settlements far apart from one another? And, finally, to what extent did kinship ties actually affect the direction and circulation of trade, as kinsmen sought to trade in places where kinsmen were already based rather than simply follow the "logic of the market" to determining \[sic\] their preferred market places?" 233-34
* Huizhou merchants were broadly classed by levels of capital, with the wealthiest possessing hundreds of thousands of "silver ounces;" these were unable to productively employ all this capital in their own ventures and thus invested in the trade of others; beginning as money lenders in their local areas these merchants (especially from Xiuning County) then set up pawnshops (典铺) throughout east China, "laying a foundation for the emergence of China's premodern banking industry."
* In general the Fangs of Yuetan followed strictly "familial" structure which deemphasized the greater lineage; of about 1000 male members who gave to the lineage hall, most gave very small sums and there was little momentum for collective organization of economic or other activity. Fang Tingke, the first really successful member of this lineage, avoided any business ties with even his own brothers, and exclusively relied on sons, nephews, and grandsons. These Fangs also avoided business ties with any other surname groups, whether or not they came from Huizhou.
* Finds that extreme importance was attached to account books, the oversight of which was the responsibility of the pater familias of a given family firm, and takes this as evidence that "the business capital of the Fangs was by and large monetarized," and that there was a clear preference to invest this money rather than hoard it. 241
* Chengs of Taitang: a trading family from Huizhou whose genealogy contains a "special chapter" providing "the single most informative, contemporary account I have read of the organization of a Ming lineage's commercial network," including history, geography, products of specilization etc.  249
* The Taitang Chengs escaped rural poverty by breaking into the Yangzhou salt trade, and become one of the most recognizable names within that trade -- but McDermott says nothing about how they initially broke into this milieu. 251
	* The Cheng salt boats also took part in long-distance trader between the Lower and Middle Yangtze, bringing textiles to Hubei in exchange for local grain, cotton, oil, and wood. They particularly engaged in "letting-out" of cotton cloth production around Wusong, supplying weavers with raw cotton cloth.
	* "Their vertical integration of this textile manufacture -- matching the raw cotton of Wusong and southern Hubei with the skilled labor force of Wusong to make products to be distributed throughout the Lower and Middle Yangzi Valley -- established their control over virtually all stages in the production and distribution of this cloth." 252
	* Profits from trade were also invested in pawnbroking, "which arguably would have tightened their grip over the production process in both the delta and Hubei," though McDermott does not elaborate on the mechanism he has in mind.
* Sees the development of the Hubei-Jiangnan trade in the 16th century as having occurred initially "to solve a problem faced by merchants flush with profits from the prosperous salt trade between Yangzhou and Wuchang," as these merchants looked to invest their capital in safe ventures along that trade route. 254
* By contrast to the Fangs, the Taitang Chengs possessed a far more self-conscious and impressive lineage structure, with the central lineage hall in Taitang (and the most important offshoot in Xingguo) helping manage relations between distant cadet branches across the Yangtze Valley.
	* By the late sixteenth century, the lineage had registered a growth from 196 members in the 16th generation to 521 in the 19th - over 150% growth in 4  generations, despite the failure of about one third of lineage men to produce any offspring. 259-260
