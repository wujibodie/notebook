The first 6 priests in the Philippines were all Augustinians: Andrés de Urdaneta and 5 companions. The Franciscans were next. Then, 10 years after Manila’s founding, the Jesuits sent 4 men, including Alonso Sanchez. These founded their base in Ermita in September of 1581 (12-14).

Chirino claims the Molucca’s lie within Spain’s half of the world per Tordesillas (15)

On March 14 1582, Sanchez travelled to Macao to inform the Portuguese there of the union between Spain and Portugal. 

* “They already knew in the Philippines about the well-built fleets of the coasts of China, and that it was not possible to go to Macau without coming upon them. For their defense and safety, then, \[the\] Father had to go armed with the most powerful weapons they used in these nations and in all the world, a safe-conduct or letter of credence, which the Filipinos call “Silat” and the Chinese and Portuguese “chapa,” in Chinese language and script was given to Fr. Alonso Sanchez. It was inscribed by a Chinese captain who was then in Manila, where since the Spanish resided there have been very many from that nation.” 35
	* elsewhere says that a “chapa” is a thin silver plate. 44
* Upon reaching the Chinese coast, Sanchez is stopped by a fleet of 1,500 ships, and granted an audience with the admiral on his flagship.
* After a long journey through China, in which he is alternately menaced and welcomed by officials, Sanchez successfully convinces the Portuguese of Macao to accept Philip II as king. 
* On his way back to Manila he crashes in Formosa and has to wait for his men to build a new ship 

Sanchez’s Second Trip to China (1583) 

* Sanchez travels to Macao to pick up the mutinous sailors of the _San Juan_, after they were captured while attempting to enter China. Meanwhile he confers with Bishop Ruggieri on the possibility of entering China, and they agree it cannot be done. On the return journey Sanchez is blown off course and comes to Manila by way of Borneo. 
* Sanchez’s conclusions on the impossibility of proselytizing in China are summarized in Chapter 14, “Chinese Isolation and Repugnance to Our Religion.” He advises a total ban on travel to China, and suggests consolidating the colonies at Macao and Manila before proceeding further.

Sanchez’s Mission to Europe (1586)

* After five years in Asia, Sanchez is sent to report on the situation of the Philippines to King Philip II. Chirino summarizes the reasons for this trip: “Father Sanchez journeyed to Spain, because the Spaniards found themselves among spirited and warlike people, as are all these Filipinos, and surrounded by others, who are not only warlike, but experiences in arms and tactics of war, as are the Japanese, Chinese, Borneans, Ternateans. On the one hand, each one and all of them are capable of the Faith, and on the other, well-provisioned for a very profitable and rich trade. For either of these reasons, and even more to live with more security, fortification, and defense, there was a great need to resolve many issues of war and peace, government and morality, penalty and reward, commerce and trade, clergy and laity.” 62 Here is the concrete “extension du l’horizonte informatique” 

In the late 1580s, the Dominicans and Augustinians set up separate ministries for Chinese Christians in Tondo. Two Dominicans, Juan de Castro and Miguel de Benavides, attempt to enter China, but are whipped and sent back to Manila. Meanwhile the Jesuit church in Ermita tends to many Japanese, including one named Gabriel, “from Mexico.” Eventually the Japanese Christians are separated and given “a special church for their race” administered by Franciscans (when exactly does this happen?). 

* Cute stories about a pagan whom God saves from a crocodile, and a sinful Christian whom God feeds to a crocodile, on 76-77
* Note: A division is made between “Chinese” and “Japanese” _chinos_ early on, but it is not clear that a divide is made between Fujianese and Cantonese until 1655 

Chirino reproduces a written report which Sanchez gave to Philip II describing the Philippines

* Chapter 1: The Grandeur, Population, and Races of the Philippines. “First, although by “Philippines” \[some\] mean Luzon, others “Spanish Islands,” taken together, it is a mountainous terrain with a group of islands dispersed and stretched from Java to Japan.” Seemingly includes Taiwan, Borneo, and maybe even the Moluccas. 
	* Further claims that the Philippines are ideal for colonization, because they are broken up into many small chiefdoms, making them easy to conquer with few men. Also, the people are quick to adopt the Spanish language and Christianity once pacified. Estimates the population of “the Philippines” as ~250,000 in the tributary regions, and another 1 million or so yet to be pacified. 
* Chapter 2: Qualities of the land. After listing the foods native to the region, Sanchez adds a list of things commonly brought from China: “wheat, flour, common biscuit, sugar, oil, ham, wine, oranges, chestnuts, walnuts, plums, raisin, dried figs of that region, preserves, grain, and many other food supplies.” Also mentions abundant textiles, gold, and wood, specifically recommending that ships can be built there very cheaply. 
* Chapter 3: Greater Benefits of the Philippines. “God located them in the center of that new world;” \[each Asian country\] “are all within sight of the Philippines, which serves as the junction for such distant things as the Eastern and Western Indies.” Further, Christianity cannot be conserved in East Asia without support from the Philippines. Manila is strategically vital to support Iberian enterprises in Japan, Macau, Malacca, the Moluccas, Siau and Mindanao, Borneo, and secondarily the rest of Asia. “His Majesty, then, should consider how God has placed that small grain to make it fructify in the middle of such a vast and deserted field... the Philippines is like a fortress and outpost which God has stationed for... all those quite remote and very extensive lands.” 
* Chirino summarizes the rest of Sanchez’s report, which apparently included advice to maintain the yearly galleon trade at Acapulco without undue strictness of inspections. 
* Sanchez further spent 5 months consulting with the ministers of Castile, mostly giving information on the state of the Philippines. 
* Sanchez wrote another report defending the Spanish right to colonization in the Philippines, which ends with a note about how the King must defend Manila to defend the Christians of the Philippines from outside attack: there is a “heavy obligation and need for His Majesty to keep that land and Christian community defended and secured, in the face of the quite powerful foreign enemies, who usually and can any day attack and ruin everything — the Japanese, who frequently come, and once surrounded our people and placed them in great danger; the Chinese, who already at one time surrounded and attacked Manila, and from those who come and go, and reside there, an uprising is feared every day; and, even more, one or several English boats...” 94. First known example of Spaniards expressing fear that Chinese will rise up, in Sanchez’s meeting with Philip II in 1586. 

Chapter 22: The Resolutions passed by King Philip II regarding Manila. 

* Exempts residents of Manila from almost all taxes, and decrees that the income of the city be provided by half of all judicial fines and half of the residency fees paid by Chinese. 
* Decrees that Chinese traders will not be allowed to sell their own goods in China, but instead will be forced to sell to Spanish wholesalers. Birth of the _pancada_ system. 
	* “Third, they ask his Majesty to forbid from remaining in the Islands the numerous Chinese, hawkers and infidels, who ordinarily add to their stock the goods which later in times of need they resell at high prices, and because of the many vices and sins they teach the Indios, particularly the unmentionable, the superstitions, and other bad things they pay the Spaniards and Indios, and the continuing suspicion they wish to take over the country. His Majesty orders carrying it out as petitioned.” 121
* In order to stop the domination of Mexican traders, decrees that only Spaniards living in Manila can sell goods on the Manila galleons. 
* Petition granted for 100 Spanish settlers to come to the Philippines to farm and teach farming to the natives; what happened to these people? 
* The King orders cows and horses to be imported from both China/Japan and Mexico, in addition to indigenous carabaos
* Also assigns a permanent garrison of not less than 400 soldiers at a time, as well as a fleet of “six or eight galleys or frigates."
* Regarding the habits of the natives, the king orders an end to (what the Spanish perceive as) slavery and to “frequent lawsuits"
* Orders an end to unauthorized missionary expeditions to China, which the petitioners (i.e. Sanchez) claim are putting the Chinese on guard 

Chapter 26, “Treatises of Father Alonso on Philippine Issues,” covers Sanchez’s reports written in Madrid and Rome on the Philippines. Probably the first major compendia of information on the Islands to make it to Europe. The first set of treatises largely discuss Spain’s right to conquer the Philippines, and were unsurprisingly well-received by the King and Popes. A few treatises discuss the best strategy for proselytization in China, and argue that it is not yet desirable to send missionaries. One treatise describes the journey from Rome to Manila, “encircling the world,” and offers the first description of the Philippines to Rome. 

Sanchez hand-picked Gómez Pérez Dasmariñas as Governor, because he fulfilled all the criteria Sanchez hoped for, “except that he has a son. But I wanted him to take the latter along there, because I believe God gave him for that reason.” Maybe your first instinct was right, Alonso... 

* Sanchez gives Dasmarinas the advice not to allow priests to travel to China, but rather to focus on converting the people of the Philippines. Dasmarinas accepts and charges the Jesuits exclusively with the conversion of the Indios, leaving the Augustinians in charge of Christian Chinese. 

When Dasmarinas travels to the Philippines he is accompanied by the first two Jesuits aside from Sanchez to reach Manila, one of whom is Chirino himself. 

Towards the end of his journeys, Sanchez gifted the church of Loretto a piece of white Chinese damask to cover the icon of the Virgin, and supposedly this damask miraculously grew after being left in the church. Silk, Ivory, Christ, the Virgin. 

Book one ends with another treatise of Sanchez’s defending the use of force in colonizing + converting the Philippines; and with a series of Papal bulls responding to Sanchez’s mission in Rome. Nothing much new found there on my skim through. 

BOOK TWO 

Claims that the name “Luzon” comes from the indigenous word for a rice husker. (249-250)

Defines the Philippine archipelago as stretching “from Mindanao to Japan;” again, clearly including Taiwan. 250

In 1589, both ships headed for Mexico were lost at sea. 250 

Mentions a volcanic eruption at Albany in 1601, which “threw up an enormous river of fire.” 253

Says that one _tae_ (tael?) is worth 10 pesos of eight. 

Brief description of Tagal-Chinese trade on 257; claims the natives robbed and enslaved the Chinese, but that after the Castilians “freed” 40 of them in 1571, “opened the port not only to large-scale Manila trade, which has been the richest and most profitable in the world, but also brought them over promptly in the thousands at a time, since the beginning of April 1572.” “And they have worked at all kinds of jobs, as silversmiths and painters, even wage earners and porters, breed much heavy livestock in these Islands, supply them with all kinds of goods and favors.” 257

Describes the defeat and forced resettlement of the “Zambales,” a head-hunting forest people in Ilocos. “Setting aside their arms, they picked up the hoe, like the Ilocanos, their neighbors, and became farmers, for their own good and the tranquility of the entire region.” 

* the Igorots are classified among the Ilocanos

Describes a 1591 smallpox epidemic, which killed many natives but no Europeans. Chirino attributes this to “the temperament produced by the good soil of Spain.” 

Describes how the natives of Taytay, his own mission ground, farm rice which is flooded naturally each wet season. Once the grain is ready the natives harvest it from boats and then dry it, tread it, clean it, and store it in granaries. 271 

* However, it is not safe to go to these flooded fields without a boat, since there are many crocodiles and caymans. 

Describes how some ambassadors bearing threats from Japan were shipwrecked on Taiwan and killed by the local in 1592. 285

Says that there were 200,000 natives on Panay when the Spanish arrived, but famine and disease reduced their number to 30,000 by 1600. 

The Japanese ambassador Faranda succeeded in reaching Manila in 1593. Dasmarinas sent him back to Japan with gifts (including a Spanish horse) and an affirmation of friendship, but Faranda mistranslated the letter as an acknowledgement of sovereignty. In response, the “tyrant” “Taycosama” sent a letter offering “friendship” and demanding to meet the King of Castile. Luis Dasmarinas responded with a quite formal letter clarifying that the only lords of the Philippines were Jesus and Philip. 

In 1595 a galleon crashed in Japan. Taycosama then seized the cargo, and angrily ordered all Christian friars in Nagasaki and “Meaco” to be arrested. Soon, he had three executed, on the basis that the Spaniards used friars as their spies in every land they conquered. 

The Christian king of Siao (Tidore? Sulu?), an island between the Philippines and the Moluccas, came to Manila on June 28, 1593. Dasmarinas planned to join with him on his expedition to the Moluccas. (319-323)

The mutiny which killed Dasmarinas occurred on October 26, 1593. Apparently the Spaniards at Cavite were anxious about Dasmarinas choice to use paid Chinese rowers — was this an experiment on his part? 

* In any case 52 of the 70 Spaniards escorting the governor were killed. 
* After hearing of his father’s death, Luis Dasmarinas decided to return to Manila to take charge there, rather than continue the expedition to the Moluccas. 325
