Faroqhi

* \[In 1982\] "the history of Ottoman transportation as a whole still remains to be written;" previous works, e.g. by Cengiz Orhonlu, have looked only from the POV of the administration.
* Celali rebellions as time when merchant safety was compromised, lasting until the 18th century.
* “Although transportation by camel became an important feature of pre Ottoman and Ottoman Anatolia, wheeled transportation never disappeared, as it did for the most part in Syria and Egypt.” 524
* Basic research question is **the relative costs of camels vs. wheeled transport** at different times and places in Anatolia; suggestion of taking large chunks of time (100-150 years) to study discontinuities, rather than assuming that the use of these two forms of transport was uniform across the pre-rail period.
* Sources: the only sources dealing specifically with camels are government surveys of the camels available to either be levied from localities, or raised in royal stables. Camel-raising also comes up incidentally in documents dealing with land/housing sales (where camel stables might be included) as well as theft and rural banditry.
* Seasonality: nomads raised camels for their own needs, but mostly used them during the spring and early (?) summer; they tended to rent them out during the fall and winter in order to raise cash to pay taxes, and the urgency of this need sometimes led them to force their “services” on neighbors who did not really need them.
* “Tavernier remarks that caravans passing from the Ottoman Empire to Iran spent about three weeks in Erzurum, both for the purpose of customs inspection and to hire new camels.'4 But at the same time, merchants probably made use of the opportunity to sell off certain goods and buy others, so that transportation costs decreased relative to total turnover. From the camel drivers' point of view, this arrangement also possessed the advantage that they might more easily find a merchant who would require their services for the return trip.” 526
	* Major transport centers as places where camel caravans were dissolved and reformed, along with customs payments and transactions.
* When the Ottoman state needed camels for supply lines during war, they would either use camels from state stables, in which case rooms at provincial _han_ would be requisitioned for stabling, or they would hire camels, in which case the costs would be shouldered by provincial taxpayers. Camels could also be requisitioned for war at state-set prices via the _mübayaa_ system. Evidence suggests both that the prices paid by the state were below market rates, and that _mübayasir_s tended to abuse their authority in various ways.
* “even at the artificially lowered transportation prices paid by the Ottoman central administration, grain transportation of this type must have cost several times as much as the purchase price of the grain in question. Only extra-economic constraint, as exercised by the powerful Ottoman state, could make overland transportation of grain from Payas or from Manisa to Erzurum a reality.” 531
* In sixteenth century Anatolia, certain nomadic groups (some identified as “Tatars”) preferred to use (ox-drawn?) wagons as their main means of transportation; while wagons were not normally part of merchant caravans in Anatolia, they frequently appeared for this purpose in the Balkans, especially in the traffic with Poland, where Armenian merchants in Lwow organized large wagon caravans to the Ottoman Empire. 532
* In the early seventeenth century, there seem to have existed wagon trails linking Eastern Anatolia to Syria and Van, probably established for the transport of cannon to the front of the war with Persia; these were also available for non-military use, as in the case of a high functionary’s widow who was provided with horses for a wagon trip to Damascus.
* General conclusion: the Ottoman road system was probably in better shape around the mid-sixteenth century, and wagon transport was thus more common, than two hundred years later. The choice between camel or wheeled transport had to do with a number of factors related to cost, road maintenance, and ability to provision from local transport networks, that shifted over the course of Ottoman history, without a clear direction of “progress” or “decline.” 536
