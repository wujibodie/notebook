* Gives five reasons for the slow development of Xiamen’s urban structure in the early 1920s:

	* 2\. The amount of capital required for just the widening and paving of streets ran into the millions of yuan, which the government was not able to raise from any of its principal revenue streams (local taxes, bonds and donations, land sales, harbor fees, etc.), most of which were requisitioned by Zang’s forces;
	* 3\. * Claims that it is only around **1919** that the Japanese ambassadors in Fujian began to clearly make use of Taiwanese criminal elements.
* Seeks to periodize the relationship between the Japanese government and Taiwanese criminals on the mainland - when did this relationship begin, how did it change, what were the main phases?
* The period from 1919 to 1923 was the "golden age" of the Taiwanese "武力派," and there is direct evidence of the Japanese utilizing this group to counter anti-Japanese activism.
* Dates the beginning of popular resentment and street violence against the Taiwanese in Xiamen to August 01, 1913, the so-called 臺紀案, when a fight broke out between a Taiwanese ice vendor and a member of the Ji surname, leading to a brawl between the Taiwanese community and the Ji lineage involving hundreds of men on each side. The Japanese ambassador protested to the local government, and eventually both Chinese and Japanese soldiers were dispatched the protect the residences of the Taiwan jimin. In negotiations with provincial authorities, the Japanese ambassador demanded that local authorities protect Japanese imperial subjects 帝國臣民 and harshly punish crimes against them, and called for the public execution of ringleaders from the Ji family. Wang notes that this is the moment when Chinese newspapers began using the term 臺匪 and accusing the Japanese of protecting criminals.
	* The Ji surname happened to be among the traditional “3 secret society lineages” 幫會三姓 of Xiamen, along with the Wu and Chen lineages. As such it proved impossible for authorities to arrest the ringleaders or confiscate their firearms, especially as most of the people accused of involvement in the riot had fled to their home villages in Tong’an. As a result of Japanese diplomatic complaints in Beijing, the local officials responsible were dismissed, and after this Xiamen’s officials never dared to inspect any buildings hanging placards of Taiwanese registration, which helped the Taiwanese community take over the city’s underground.
* In 1916 there was a notorious wave of kidnappings and ransoming of property, with the criminals said to operate out of safe houses marked with Taiwan residency placards. Whenever the police captured a Taiwan resident accused of these crimes, the Japanese ambassador would request them to be freed for lack of evidence.
* After 1916 the Japanese ambassador （Kikuchi 菊池) in Xiamen began to be concerned about criminality in the Taiwanese population under his jurisdiction, and negotiated with local authorities to strip criminals of their registration status, but the Japanese found it impossible to do this in practice. A police inspector was sent from Taipei to Xiamen in May, and about 60 people were arrested in the investigation, with the 黑幫大哥 烏面瑞 being executed. After this there was a period of crackdown on Taiwanese criminals in Xiamen, with many leaving the city.
	* Succeeding attempts to tighten restrictions on who received ambassadorial protection and to regularize extradition of criminals back to Taiwan stalled in the face of disputes between Kikuchi in Xiamen and his counterparts in Taiwan. In the end, no progress was made on restricting or regulating the movements of criminals between Taiwan and Xiamen.
* In September 1917, Li Houji (appointed Fuzhou garrison commander by Yuan Shikai in 1913) took sole command of the Fujian army. After 1919, he began to face opposition from the local people, and eventually fled to Taiwan (!)
* A 1929 Japanese report found that Zang Zhiping’s era of rule was the “golden age” of criminal toughs, who made their money on gambling, opium, and prostitution.
* Wang sees a turn in Japanese policy after 1919, when they choose to utilize Taiwan jimin as a counterweight to anti-Japanese movements in the wake of the Treaty of Versailles and May 4. One 楊北辰 was awarded by the Japanese embassy in 1920 for his work to defuse anti-Japanese boycotts as the head of a “special Taiwanese collective” task force.
* On April 21, 1923, the merchants and government of Xiamen participated in a boycott of Japanese products, with many public speeches against the Japanese. The then-ambassador, Kono Saya 河野清, sent a report to his superiors saying that Zang Zhiping refused to get involved because he was preparing for war with Sun Chuanfang. The Japanese response heavily relied on the Taiwan Gonghui for manpower: for example, when the city’s **sailors and porters** refused to accept Japanese merchandise, the embassy organized a new shipping company to unload merchandise, with the majority of the workers being recruited via the Taiwan Gonghui. 312-313
	* While at first certain Japanese residents of Gulangyu lodged complaints about Kono’s favoring of the Taiwanese, eventually the Japanese community became favorable to the Taiwanese, in part because of the flexibility of the Taiwanese in selling Japanese products with stickers claiming that they were Chinese-produced.
* The Special Taiwanese Collective Task Force assembled c. 1920 by the Japanese embassy was split into 10 teams, most of which were headed by members of the infamous 18 Brothers of Xiamen’s criminal underground, including Lin Gun 林滾. Clear proof that the official Japanese-approved Taiwanese associations were intimately tied in with the structure of Taiwanese gangs
* The Taiwanese gangs, Lin Gun in particular, were importing hundreds if not thousands of firearms from Taiwan and Hong Kong annually based on customs seizures from 1929 and 1930. Most of these guns probably were sold to lineage or bandit armies in the countryside, but many stayed in Xiamen to equip the “self-defense forces” of the Taiwan Gonghui and individual gangs.
* In 1923/24 the Taiwan 武力派 had a series of confrontations with the traditional criminal gangs, first the Wu lineage and then with the gangster/police inspector Li Qingbo. At this point the armed element in the Taiwanese community was effectively beyond the control of the Japanese embassy, as letters back to Japan show. 317-318
	* The two main groups w/in the Wulipai were the Daodaocheng Pai led by Lin Gun, and the Wanhua Pai led by Xie (A)fa 謝阿發, both born in what by then was Taibei zhou.
* A 1932 Japanese report on Lin Gun listed him as having a fortune of at least 150,000 Yuan, earned through gambling operations, large hotels, and (primarily?) opium smuggling. He was also an 議員 of the Taiwan Gonghui. He was also a trustee of multiple schools and the head of the Board of Education of the Taiwan Gonghui.

* The chief of staff of the Qingdao garrison army, Morioka 森岡, wrote that the initial goal of the Japanese opium policy was to designate accredited Chinese sellers (preferably just one) and sell licenses to smokers, and crack down on private smuggling and manufacturing.  Meanwhile, while the German system of licensing smokers (at the high cost of 1 silver dollar per month) was maintained in principle, in practice poor smokers were almost always allowed to purchase opium at dens without registration; the license system was kept up as a way to “control wealthy smokers, set a basic floor on consumption, while abandoning the poor,” all with the goal of maximizing revenue.

清末地方省份的鸦片专卖
刘 增 合

* In 1906-1908, the provincial governments of Jiangsu, Fujian, and Shandong became interested in a scheme to monopolize sales of opium paste in government-run “官膏局”. The earliest version of this scheme was thought up in Suzhou, but abandoned there in favor of a simple escalating tax on opium paste due to merchant opposition; monopoly offices were actually opened in Nanjing, Xuzhou, and Yangzhou under the Jiangning Xunfu Duan Fang, but Duan was quickly forced to close the offices and shift to simple taxation due in this case to protests from the British ambassador, speaking on behalf of both the “old” and “new” Sassoon firms as well as local (Chaozhouese and Cantonese) resellers. The Qing court ordered Duan to comply out of a fear that perceived violations of British treaty rights could lead to a new war or to British demands for new concessions.
* The opium prohibition bureau and the finance ministry of Fujian showed an interest in following the Nanjing model in 1907, in this case with substantial support from unnamed wealthy merchants who wished to farm the rights to the opium paste monopoly, including in one case for as much as 500,000 yuan per year in Zhangzhou and Quanzhou alone (!). This proposal also included a tripartite pricing scheme.
	* Liu points out that Fujian imported most of its opium despite being a producer, and that slightly more than half of these imports came from outside China, so the question of an opium monopoly in the province was again a delicate one re: British interests.
* Although Fujian ultimately decided for the Suzhou model of registering and taxing private sellers, major local merchants continued to show a keen interest in buying opium paste monopoly rights. A Quanzhou native named Li Bingyi offered to found a “All-Fujian Western Medicine Monopoly Corporation,” with the local Mitsui branch promising to invest 500,000 to 1 million yuan, but the scheme was eventually abandoned following an investigation by the Fujian Shanghui into Li. The formula for how this project was meant to operate was “Official supervision, gentry cooperation, merchant handling” (官督绅协商办).
