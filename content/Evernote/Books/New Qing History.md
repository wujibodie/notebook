All three review essays of the "New Four Books" of New Qing History highlight a key shared feature of the works by Crossley, Elliott, Rawski, and Rhoades: the reimagining of "Manchu" and "Han" identity as shifting signifiers constantly remade on the basis of political prerogatives, rather than as generic ethnic term distinguishing fundamentally unalike groups. The history of the Qing Dynasty becomes, to a large extent, the history of the shaping of these categories and their connotations between the coining of the term "Manchu" in 1635 and the final collapse of the dynasty in 1912. The Qing rulers are no longer seen as either "foreign" despots or "sinified" emperors, but as political professionals who were capable of showing different faces and employing different concepts to legitimize their rule to different members of their highly diverse empire. As Sen points out, this situation can be fruitfully compared to the situations in other large multiethnic empires contemporary with the Qing, such as those of the Ottomans, Hapsburgs, or Mughals, without taking away from the unique features of Qing political culture. The terms of diachronic comparison seem harder to settle on: there is disagreement among our authors about whether the Qing ultimately derived more from previous "Chinese" traditions of rulership (especially the Qing), from other "hybrid" empires such as the Liao, Jin, and Yuan, or whether they were largely a _de novo_ product of the Liaodong frontier. I gave a quick skim to one of these four books, Crossley's _Translucent Mirror_, and found that her emphasis tended towards the latter of these interpretations: the Aisin Goro line as a class apart, which drew its basic political philosophy from the experience of uniting their linguistically fractured ("Chinese," "Mongol," and "Manchu") territories beyond the Wall, before applying those experiences creatively to the exigencies of their post-1644 empire. But this point strikes me as being up for debate even within the "New Qing School," which has been criticized more broadly both for being overly reliant on Western "theory" (as in the critiques listed by Ding and Elliott) and for being relatively _undertheorized_ and overly focused on the idea of top-down Manchu-Han or Manchu-Mongol relations rather than interactions between non-Manchu-identified groups (what I take to be one strand of Schluessel's project).

Comparing Schluessel to Crossley, I find that Schluessel's focus has shifted from the formation of identity groups to the comparison of concepts held to define different ethnolinguistic bodies. Little attempt is made to investigate the formation of such group boundaries as "Musulman" vs. "Hui," which are given as actor's categories based on spoken language. Instead, Schluessel's key focus in on ways in which the "Chinese" concept of _li_ was understood in contrast to local Musulman interpretations of Sharia law. I wonder to what extent this reflects a conscious choice on Schluessel's part to refocus on "civilizational" comparison after the work done problematizing ethnic categories by the earlier "New Qing History," or perhaps a necessity rising from the sources in a context (Muslim-majority East Turkistan) where such boundaries were more visible and less flexible than along the Great Wall frontier. I have to admit to being somewhat troubled, though, by Schluessel's consistent comparison between the concept of _li_ and that of "civilization," and his use of this comparison to suggest that the project of the Xiang Army occupiers of Xinjiang was fundamentally the same (a colonial "civilizing project") as that of the European imperial powers elsewhere. It seems to me that much of what might be fruitfully gained from the encounter between two different legal systems and languages which are rarely compared in depth in English-language scholarship is lost when those comparisons are brought back to such familiar categories as colonialism and reconstruction.
