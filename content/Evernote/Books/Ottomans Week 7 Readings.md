Peirce - The Court of Aintab

* In the Ottoman domains, court records begin to survive in large numbers from the late fifteenth century on. Peirce focuses on the challenges of interpreting this corpus of sources, with a focus on the local terrain and their placement in broader legal literature (especially the "merging" of Islamic law, sultanic law, and local customary law).
* In theory the only crimes required to be prosecuted were those listed in the Quran: theft, highway robbery, wine drinking, illicit sex, and slander. These crimes could not be settled by personal agreement as other crimes could.
* Collective responsibility: villages and neighborhoods could be held responsible for crimes committed in them if they did not produce a suspect; communities also had the right to expel members with criminal reputations. 4
* "In fact, the jurisdiction of a judge—the kaza, or judicial administrative unit—was by design congruent with the jurisdiction of the provincial governor—the sancak or liva, the basic unit of military-fiscal administration."
* The practice of keeping public records at courthouses became more widespread in Suleyman's reign, along with the shift towards central appointment of all judges. "The practice of keeping a public record was obviously linked to the push to define a fixed location for the court. It was also tied to another goal of Ottoman judicial administration: to standardize procedures across the empire so that legal transactions could be universally recognized. " 12
* "In other words, verbal testimony is recorded not randomly but rather in a manner integral to the nature of the matter at hand.67 Patterns of speech representation can thus signal issues that were chronic concerns for Aintabans."
* Tendency for judges to follow "separate justices" for individual members of a given case, with the goal of "social equity rather than a neat solution in which all relevant legal rules are brought into harmony and satisfied" (21).
* Perception of kanun and sharia as overlapping and complementary: "In truth, the long history of Muslim sovereigns fulfilling (or failing in) their duty to enforce religious law had drawn these two sources of law into symbiosis, at least in the eastern Mediterranean region and by the late medieval period. In Aintab, the “people of executive authority” who punished were the same individuals who prosecuted the Qur’anic crimes in the name of protecting the “claims of God.”" 26
* Customary law had authority over local prices, with a marked element of seasonality: "One arena where local leaders (ayan) set legally enforced standards was the pricing of goods sold in the markets of Aintab, which the court record informs us were established “according to traditional practice” (adet-i kadim üzere). For example, in the setting of meat prices, the record relates that “the ayan of Aintab gathered, came to the court, and gave the following testimony: ‘It has come about from old that **in the current season \[ June\]**, a batman of lamb is \[sold\] for nine Aleppo akçes and a batman of goat meat for seven Aleppo akçes.’”
* Similarities between kanun and Hanafi law: "Hanafi legal tradition shared with sultanic kanun a number of features that gave them an **epistemological kinship**. Both had strong loyalties to the founders of their respective legal traditions, yet both acknowledged from the beginning the notion that there could be differences of opinion among the founders. Both were moored to original texts while recognizing the need for continual commentary and practical flexibility. Both emphasized local knowledge, sharia with its caution to judges to recognize legitimate local practice and kanun with its separate law books for different provinces. Both would appear to have similarly viewed normative law as a statement of the limits of the tolerable rather than a set of inflexible rules to be imposed regardless of circumstances. Both had mechanisms for updating and modifying existing textual prescriptions." 28
* "Indeed, the guiding formula of din wa dawla, “religion and imperium,” translated in practice into a mutual reinforcement between the two sources of legislative authority. The division of labor between judgment and punishment in the court of Aintab is a concrete example of this symbiosis." 29
* "We might think of the local court as the meeting place between subjects and sovereign where the two negotiated a balance of control over the management of local society. "

Doumani - Fatima's Determination

* A dispute over a _waqf_ estate hinged on the question of the granter's precise wording, with the plaintiffs alleging that he had mistakenly used the language correct to a will rather than a waqf; determining this matter involved decades of legal fights. Difficulty of squaring the long-lasting legal consequences of_waqfs_ with the mostly oral nature of their granting.
* "The registration and governance of family waqf endowments through the shariʿa court were critical to the construction of “family” as a corporate unit in Ottoman times, especially in provincial cities, towns, and large villages." 279
* The estate in question consisted of seven properties: a house, an olive press, and parcels planted with mulberry trees, vines, olive groves, and citrus orchards. 281
	* These lands had been in the family for generations and were interspersed with those of Fatima's cousins; the major cause of dispute was that, as she was married to a non-relative, her children would belong to that man's family, and their inheritance would thus leave the extended family patrimony.
* In the initial legal decision in 1814, the judge of Tripoli ruled that Abd al-Qadir had stated that the _waqf_ was to be established after his death, which made it liable to Islamic inheritance laws, by which Fatima only was entitled to sole control of one-third of the estate; the other two-thirds were divided between her and her cousins.
* 23 years later, Fatima appeared in Tripoli to relitigate the case, and the qadi ruled that the entire estate should be returned to her. "The legal environment did not change in 1842. Nor did the basic social norms of the village in which she lived. What changed was Fatima’s ability to mobilize power brokers in both Tripoli and Qalamon, and she did so in ways consistent with the possibilities for economic and social power that already existed in Tripoli for women from propertied families. In the 1838 lawsuit, Fatima produced twice the number of witnesses needed. All hailed from Tripoli, not Qalamon, and all brazenly testified to what they could not possibly have seen or heard." 292
	* Property litigation as a ground where power struggles between propertied groups could be played out, with shifting coalitions leading to different outcomes in suits.

Grehan

* Case on one Abd al-Rahman, a defendant who won his case for lack of evidence, but was found guilty when he refused to swear his innocence on the Quran.
* "His hesitation, bordering on awe, suggests that profound beliefs and inhibitions were coloring his thoughts and holding him back. What made words (or at least particular kinds of words such as oaths) seem so threatening and portentous? If we really want to understand these powerful psychological reactions, we need to step outside the courthouse and look beyond its arcane rules and rituals. Wandering into the streets, we need to tum instead to language itself and set the use of oaths among prevailing habits of speech, along with the manners, gestures, and emotions which typically accompanied them and inflected their meaning. Only this broader sociolinguistic background, so obvious to contemporaries but largely invisible to us, can explain the unusual weight that oaths seemed to carry, the situations in which people might resort to them, and the responses that they would be likely to provoke." 992
* The elaborate etiquette observed by the ulema seems not to have made much of an impact on popular society, which "recognized an alternate code of ethics which accepted, and sometimes demanded, dramatic gestures, harsh words, and if necessary, full-blown act of violence," with an "obsession with 'saving face'" (997).
* Conclusion: "So it was only a short step from fleeting words to irrevocable deeds. "The common people say that the tongues of the people are the pens of fate (tuqulu al-'amma inna alsinat al-khalq aqlamal-haqq)," declared Ibn Kannan in confirma- tion. l 13 Or as sociolinguists might put it today, Damascenes viewed their pro- nouncements as "performative utterances", in which the distinction between words and deeds almost imperceptibly melted away. Much of this mentality was intimately bound up with an essentially magical view of the world, which en- dowed words with an incantatory quality." 1008

Burak - Second Formation of Islamic Law

* The conquest of Baghdad in 1535 brought with it the tomb of Abu Hanifa, "the greatest imam" and founder of the Hanafi school.
* Along with the Ottoman state's selection of the Hanafi school as the official madhhab (school of law) in its domain, it also regulated to some extent the content of that school.
* The madhhabs emerged in the 9th/10th centuries as legal canons around which groups of scholars, tracing descent from original founders, based their hermeneutic principles.
* 4 interlocking developments which led to the evolution of the state madhhab: 1. the rise of the imperial learned hierarchy; 2. the emergence of the practice of appointing muftis by the dynasty; 3. the dynasty's/state's regulation of the structure and doctrine of the school; and 4. **the rise of dynastic law in the post-Mongol eastern Islamic lands.**  10-11.
* Both the dynasty and the jurists which it encountered after its conquest of the Arab lands was faced with the challenge "posed by the encounter between the post-Mongol notion of dynastic law and the pre-Mongol notion of Islamic law." 16-17
* Disputes the periodization which sees the 19th century as the period of rupture when Islamic law is codified and reformed by state intervention; instead this process "is observable from the fifteenth century (and perhaps even earlier)." 20
* Shahrukh appointed a _sheyk al-Islam_ responsible for overseeing religious life in his domains in the early 1400s, while the Ottomans instituted the _seyhülislam_ around the same time. These officials particularly had authority over state-appointed muftis.
* The language of kanun emerged in the fifteenth century, as part of a "notion of dynastic law (framed in terms of _yasa_ or _töre_)... circulating at that time across the eastern Islamic lands" (216).
* The fifteenth/sixteenth centuries saw the encounter of Islamic and dynastic legal traditions, with tension and delineation of boundaries as a natural consequence. 218
	* "The official/dynastic school of law (or branch within the school) was the response that dynasts and state-affiliated jurists developed to cope with the challenges that the encounter between the Chinggisid and pre-Mongol Islamic (Sunni) notions of law posed." 220

Ergene - Qanun and Sharia

* “The Ottoman qānūnnāme, which is a compilation of qanuns, can be defined as a legal code that contained state directives on criminal penalties, urban and rural taxation, land use, market organization, manufacturing and artisanal production, and military matters.” Qanunname could be made for all reaya, for specific Sanjaks, or for particular groups.
* “Even if there is a historical connection between Ottoman qanun and Turco-Mongolian legislative practice (Heyd 1967: 3),4 the contents of the qanun codes appear to have been contextually determined and not brought from Central Asia. Colin Imber (1997: chap. 1; 2008) suggests that they were shaped by local, “feudal” customs and practices in tax collection, land use, and administrative matters that the Ottomans encountered in conquered territories.”
* Previous scholars (Barkan, Imber) had viewed Qanun as separate from Sharia in that the former was man-made and flexible, while the former was unchanging and kept more or less the same form from the eleventh to nineteenth centuries, and also of limited applicability to practical governance.
* Present-day scholars tend to view Qanun as rarely superseding sharia, but instead dealing with areas not discussed in religious jurisprudence, or where the burden of evidence required cannot be satisfied.
* “Furthermore, it has been pointed out that many criminal provisions of qanun had no counterparts in religious law. For example, while for the Hanafis murder and injury generated private claims, in the qanun they also gave rise to public claims.” What are the historical or ideological contexts that would give rise to this difference? Change in assumption that injured parties would be avenged?
* Finally, other scholars note that Qanun began to lose ground to Sharia in the seventeenth century, with the rise of “Kadizadeli puritanism” which stringently asserted the superiority of religious law and restricted the creation of new qanunname in recently conquered territories.
* Counterpoint to notion of Qanun as “secular” law: “Akgündüz (1990: 67) points out that qanunnames contained prescriptions that can be found in fiqh treatises. In fact, he argues, one of the functions of qanunnames was to implement, if selectively, relevant fiqh-based prescriptions in particular circumstances, and therefore these documents reflect Ottoman efforts to apply fiqh-based principles in their own context.”114
	* “Akgündüz interprets qanun legislation as a means to arrive at novel legal interpretations in Islamic law. Consistent with this interpretation, Akgündüz does not refer in his account to Inner Asian imperial traditions of government that recognized law-making as a basis of sovereignty. This is likely because he considers Islamic legal traditions as capable of producing new legislation without any outside help.” 115
	* Ergene approves of Akgündüz on qanun being intertwined with Quranic and Hadith-based jurisprudence, but worries that A. sees religious law as being perfect and unchanging, giving short shrift to the contingent and historically conditioned etc etc nature of all lawmaking. E demands historians answer “why the Ottomans themselves changed their thinking about qanun and its relationship to religious law over time, as is demonstrated by the fact that while the earliest qanunnames did not contain any references to the religious law, any reference to qanun in legal prescriptions was prohibited after the seventeenth century.” 117
* A call not to view qanun as determined entirely by the sovereign: “Results generated by this research have led students of Ottoman court records to hypothesize that local law was often constructed in a process of negotiation among multiple parties that took into consideration a multiplicity of factors, including fiqh-based principles, the imperial center’s interests and objectives, and provincial expectations of fairness and equity.”

Akiba - Farming Out Offices

* Over the 18th century, the growing dominance of kadiships by a small number of elite “Ulema families” occurred in connection with a growing outsourcing of actual judging to _naibs_, assistant judges. Akiba’s research goal: studying this development “not as a symptom of deterioration or corruption of the Ottoman ulema but as a result of the transformation of the ilmiye institution into a system of allocating sources of revenue.”
* The system of deputization grew out of _arpaliks_, nominal judgeships assigned to out-of-work kadis as “unemployment subsidies;” the subsidized individuals would be paid out of the fees given to their deputies in minor posts. Before the 18th century, most kadiships reserved for these sinecures were of medium-sized towns in the Balkans and Anatolia, but as time went on increasingly many and important provincial courts were reserved for arpaliks.
	* Is this system not essentially just a subsidy for senior judges directly out of the salary of younger/lower-ranking ones?
	* Other, smaller kadiships were reserved for _maisets,_ subsidies for early-career professors.
* In all, “more than 300 kadıship positions were earmarked as arpalıks and maişets to provide mevali, professors, and sons of ulema with sources of income,” which helped “financially support the ilmiye institution as a status group.” 8-9
* The long waits between kadi positions encouraged hopefuls to take on other lines of work, which they would often continue while farming out their nominal kadiships once they were appointed.
	* The naibs themselves could come from backgrounds other than the ulema; there were criticisms of naibs coming from the “guilds and marketplaces.” “Their accusations were not entirely groundless; we find booksellers, public bath operators (hamamcı), and a rice seller (pirinççi) among the kuzat members.48 We cannot be certain whether they were kadıs-turned-tradesmen or tradesmen-turned-kadıs; both patterns are probable.” 13
* Two means by which the financial transfers of naibships took place: commission, whereby the naib forward 4/5ths or so of his collected fees to the nominal kadi and kept the rest, or _iltizam_, whereby a kadiship was purchased for a fixed fee, partially paid in advance. The government attempted to ban _iltizam_ of kadiships and upheld 1/5th commissions as the standard, but in practice both methods were common, and could be combined.
* Intermediaries named _muhzirs_, officials tasked with communicating with kadis, were often entrusted with the actual transfer of funds and even the selection of naibs; they often took advantage of their position to extend loans to naibs, many of whom died in debt to these agents, and to broker the positions entrusted to them, with or without reference to qualities of the candidates besides their ability to pay.
* Moneylenders (_sarrafs)_ also frequently specialized in trading and arranging naibships; “with the introduction of lifetime tax farming (malikâne) in 1695, their role became particularly important because they provided tax farmers with loans for the advance payments and transferred the annual payments to the treasury,” and this relationship was extended to the _iltizam_ of kadiships.
* “Their \[muhzirs’ and sarrafs’\] intervention in the appointment of naibs and the related financial transactions also suggests that some office holders merely received payments from them without being involved in the nomination of their deputy judges, while others appointed naibs based on patron–client relationships.” 
* One of the important sources of income for kadis was the fees they levied as part of apportioning tax levies for their jurisdictions. Cites Ergene’s finding that there was “little correlation between the fees and the tax amounts.”
* “Because the appointment of naibs involved assigning sources of state revenue to individuals, it is conceivable that this process was based on a mechanism parallel to the tax farming system, as the use of the term “iltizam” clearly indicates—all the more so because after the mid-eighteenth century, a significant proportion of their income was derived from taxes collected from local taxpayers in the name of apportionment fees rather than from fees paid by court clients.”30
	* Arpalik also had a parallel in the military-administrative system: “it became increasingly common for provincial governors, high-ranking officials, and fortress commanders to be granted subprovince governor offices as additional sources of income, also called “arpalık”.”
* In general, in the 18th century, all posts were routinely subcontracted, “but it is important that they were operated through a mechanism of tax farming. Thus, during the eighteenth century, tax farming became a major instrument **not only for collecting state revenue but also for allocating it to state officials**.” Crucial importance of the tax farming system as the new means for distributing revenue among the elite.
	* Major difference between naib system and tax farming: “Whereas tax farming in the Ottoman Empire was adopted primarily as a means of raising state revenue, the revenue collected by naibs was not transferred directly to the state treasury but to the office holders who appointed them.” The system was eventually destroyed when the state began paying regular salaries to both kadis and naibs, while insisting on judgeship fees being routed to the treasury; this in turn “dealt a heavy blow to the power of the ilmiye institution, which lost its economic foundations.” 32-33
