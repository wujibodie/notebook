Rowe

* Opening point of US historiography of China: Fairbank's modernization thesis centered on "China's response to the West." Idea that even the PRC is a "People's Middle Kingdom" - deep historical continuity. Vietnam-era critique of this thesis essentially just flips it: Western entry as negative turning point from stability rather than positive entry from stagnation to modernity.
	* Naito Konan: "Precocious" transition to modernity in development of examination-based "middle class" in the Song Dynasty.
* "Third, intermediate, view": A coherent period from the 1550s to the 1930s characterized by "the urbanization of the lower Yangtze, the commutation of labor services into money payments, the development of certain kinds of regional trade, the growth of mass literacy and increase in the size of the gentry, the commercialization of local managerial activities..." These changes fall under the label of "early modern."
* Four major issues under debate by the three schools:
	* The agrarian regime. Major trends: shift from guantian to private land; segmentation of land rights and markets in partial land claims; development of separate rentier, managerial, and cultivator classes. Separation in landholding patterns between the north (mostly yeoman, few large landholders), Jiangnan (mostly absentee landlords and long-term tenant cultivators), South China (high rents, absentee landlordism, often managed by kinship-based corporate landlord associations), and "newly settled regions" liked Xiang valley, Taiwan plains, certain highlands  (cultivated on "commercial" basis for export, with older entrepreneurs cooperating to "open" the land, and new arrivals tending to be heavily exploited).
		* Three views of basic rural structure: Fei Xiaotong's idea of localized village societies with "network consciousness" rather than "group consciousness;" Japanese debates about rural "community" (kyodotai 共同体) as opposed to class consciousness; and the idea of separately "open" and "closed" village societies in response to political/economic conditions, based esp. on Skinner

von Glahn

* Chief distinction between Kyoto and Tokyo schools: Naito believed that China reached a precocious "modernity" characterized by autonomous local society, whereas the Tokyo school saw "the perpetuation of feudal social relations within the enveloping folds of _kyodotai_ as the chief obstacle to China's modernization." 41　The mainstream of Japanese sinology embraced Tokyo school conclusion that the Tang-Song transition saw beginnings of "feudal" society through autonomous local landholding relations.
* Question, starting in 50s, of whether late Ming/Qing commercial revolution constituted beginning of indigenous breakthrough towards capitalism. Common view that the "gentry" ruling class prevented any economic/social development by siphoning surplus production into leisure.
* "Early modern" paradigm grows out of Annales school's study of 1500-1800 period as one large "conjuncture" with unified trends. View of China as participating in many of these trends championed by Wakeman, Rowe. Challenges from Huang (sees arrested development in agriculture from Song to Qing) and Wong (similar economic dynamics bw China and Europe, but different state-society relations; state devolution of responsibilities to local elites vs state arrogation of all responsibilities in europe)
* Chugokushi kenkyukai 中國史研究會 group: focus on multiple loci of influence in local society, including: 1. consanguineal networks; 2. economic exchange, including landlord/tenants; 3. cultural hegemony of the _shidafu_; 4. the state. Sees the rise of an autocratic state along with a highly developed state of "petty commodity production" preceding the modern Chinese situation of a powerful state w/o corresponding civil society or representation

Farquhar and Hevia

* Problem of "culture concept": critiques of its ineffability and reliance on questionable generalizations have not killed it as a load-bearing part of historical theories.
* Recent attempts to rehabilitate new European historiographical modes (e.g. Habermasian "public sphere", and perhaps social history in general) risk turning back to a basic idea of "progress" which ties rather too closely to "state policy interests..."
* A too-easy borrowing of the ideas which have developed around the "public sphere" debate in the 20th century West for studies in a Chinese context
* "once the social is seen as heterogenous... neither textual hermeneutic nor objectivist description can clarify what we see as the central issue of contemporary cultural studies -- subject construction."

		(intros)
		These three articles each follow similar touchstones when discussing older historiography: the German sociologists serve as a shared influence on the separate traditions of the Harvard school of Fairbank, the Kyoto and Tokyo schools of inter- and post-war Japan, and 20th century mainland Chinese scholarship. Each of these mid-century traditions shares a broad emphasis on the features of "traditional" China which either precluded or facilitated an entry into a "modernity" characterized by European political-economic experience. For each of these authors, one break from this preoccupation with the "stagnancy" or otherwise of a broadly conceived pre-modern China came with the emergence of the "early modern" paradigm (which as Von Glahn shows was also initally borrowed from European theoreticians, this time particularly the Annales school), one of the early proponents of which was Fred Wakeman, although William Skinner appears as an important precursor in his focus on differentiating the cyclical dynamics of China's "macro-regions." Rowe, as a card-carrying proponent of this paradigm, emphasizes how the notion of "early modern" China provides a view of continuities stretching from the Ming to Republican periods, which put the lie to a picture of a stable China stimulated to change by the Western impact. Von Glahn, writing somewhat later than Rowe, summarizes the views of a number of scholars who accept the need to move past the traditional-modern dichotomy but take issue with Rowe's picture of a China moving independently towards a state basically similar to that of "early modern" Europe. Furquhar and Hevia, in their short discussion of another of Rowe's writings ("The Public Sphere in Modern China") draw this implicit critique out more forcefully when they point out that attempts to reapply universalist (and modish) European theories in the Chinese context risk tying the study of Chinese history rather too closely to an idea of "progress" which is difficult to separate from American foreign policy.
				Each of this week's authors shares the awareness
		that one of the key questions for any American historian of China needs to be how to contribute productively to discussions about a place which has a long, broad, and ongoing historiographical tradition of its own. The idea of writing a "history of China as seen from China" is a necessity, but it also brings up the obvious question: why should Americans (or Europeans) be writing these histories at all? As the American academy becomes (slowly) less insular and less able to claim priority in global knowledge production, it becomes ever more obvious how insufficient early attempts at incorporating "China" into the historical concepts current in the West have been, even from the standpoint of contributing to those same Western theories. The optimistic possibilities of a theoretical alliance between studies of Chinese history and the paradigms of early modernity, world history, and critical cultural studies do not entirely escape this intrinsic issue, although they certainly each provide much more satisfactory bases to work from than some of the theories our authors critique. To my mind this is not a reason to despair and give up on the research or writing of Chinese history (or the history of anywhere else), but it is also not something likely to ever be solved in an entirely satisfactory way. Instead, it should be held firmly in mind and used as a spur against complacency in the choice of questions, methods, and, most of all, theories.
