Prakash, “Cooperation and Conflict”

* “The coercive authority available to the English Company (and by extension to its servants engaged in trade on their private account) was used not only in respect of the rival European corporate enterprises and private European traders but also in relation to the Indian merchants engaged in intra-Asian trade. Apart from textiles, the most widespread misuse of this authority took place in the procurement of and trade in opium. Soon after the take-over of Bengal, English Company servants stationed in Bihar established private monopolies in the drug. From 1765 onwards, the factors at Patna decided to operate jointly and divide the profits from the venture on the basis of each person’s status in the hierarchy. In 1773, the Company decided to assume monopoly rights in the drug for itself. The arrangement was for the Company to organize the procurement of the drug on an exclusive basis and then arrange for its sale to prospective traders at public auctions held at Calcutta. The adverse effects that this had on the trade of the rivals including the Dutch East India Company — its servants engaged in trade in the drug on a clandestine basis— and the Indian merchants trading with the eastern sections of the Indian Ocean, was quite substantial.” 22

Richards, “The Opium Industry”

* Overall argument is that the large profit margins, large number of individual transactions, and remittance in the form of specie all made opium an outsized stimulant to the Indian economy during the heyday of the trade (1830-1890 or so)
* During the period of absolute highest volume of exports to China, from 1865-1890, when exports averaged around 90,000 chests of opium per year, Indian supplies could have sufficed for 13.3 million daily smokers in China. 58-59
* J.Y. Wong summarizing a debate in the House of Lords on the crucial role of opium in the flow of commerce, and the ability of the Raj to maintain tribute payments to London: “He said that the sale of Bengal opium to China was a great link in the chain of commerce with which Britain surrounded the world. The chain worked like this. The United Kingdom paid the United States for cotton by bills upon England. The Americans took some of these bills to Canton and swapped them for tea. The Chine s changed teh bills for Indian opium. Some of the bills were remitted to England as profit; others were taken to India to buy additional commodities, as well as to furnish the money remittance of private fortunes in India and the funds for carrying on the Indian government at home.” 59
* After 1820, the majority of bullion imports from China went to Bombay, rather than Calcutta. "This suggests that the greater part of private returns from the opium trade that returned to India went to the mercantile communities of western India, not Bengal." 64
* "From the days of Clive, British rule reversed the usual flow of precious metals in the world economy." Silver now flowing from New World -> China -> India -> London. The growth in silver imports from China also reversed India's early 19th century financial crisis, helping end the "wrenching period of structural adjustment to \[India's\] new dependent position in the world economy."
	* Especially important as the minting of millions of gold/silver rupees helped solve liquidity problems in an economy that still was not allowed to have a circulating paper currency
* The overall impact of bullion moving to Calcutta was limited, as it would largely remain in Company/Raj coffers or be remitted to London. But "Malwa opium was a different story. This was private enterprise and largely Indian enterprise. Opium was an economic mainstay for a large number of princely Indian states in Central India and Rajasthan... In these enclaves opium cultivation employed several hundred thousand peasant growers. Generating and collecting funds for opium advances to cultivators generated income for an array of village moneylenders, village headmen, and an ascending sequence of Indian bankers. Gathering raw opium, assembling, curing, packing and transporting it, employed thousands of labourers, guards, carters and other workers. As opium passed through a chain of transactions on its way to Bombay it offered reliable profits to numerous brokers, traders, and commission agents." 71 Not to mention giving a foot in the door to some of the first and most important Indian capitalists, Tatas, Sassoons, etc...
	* Estimates that the sale of Malwa opium domestically (i.e. before leaving Bombay) generated 31-32 million rupis per year (1850s-1880s) between "producers, processors, trad and creditors." 72
	* **"I suspect that much, if not most, of the capital accumulated by Indian investors, traders and industrialists manifest in nineteenth century Bombay was the result of the Malwa or western opium trade."** 77
