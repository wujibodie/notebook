Halil Inalicik, “Centralization and Decentralization,” _Studies in Eighteenth Century Islamic History._

* “The era of the Köprülüs (four reforming grand vizirs of the latter half of the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries) was a crucial period during which a vigor­ ous effort was made to curb the power of governors, to restore central authority in the provinces, and to ap­ ply systematically a rational policy of protecting the reaya (tax-paying subjects) from abuses of authority.” The conflict was initially forced by the trend of provincial governors forming private armies which they used to extort extralegal taxes from the populace.
	* “From the seventeenth century onward, the expansion of the practice of arpalik, the consequent administra­tion of sancaks by mtttesellims, and finally the appointment of mütesellims from among the local ayan, ap­pear to have been the most important operative factors which resulted in the rise to prominence of ayan in provincial administration.” 31
		* As the strongest of the _ayan_ (local notables) increasingly recognized _mütesellim_ (tax collector/farmer) status as the highest font of authority in their regions, violent strife between _ayan_ became increasingly common. Issue both of usurpation and of coalitions of other local elites against the _mütesellim_, due to conflicting interests
	* “The most significant development, which, with few exceptions in the seventeenth century, occurred in the eighteenth century, was the granting of the actual posts of bey and pasa to these aghas of reaya origin, that is, the direct delegation of the sultan's author­ ity.40 As a result, pasas of ayan origin and their fam­ilies rose to prominence in the provinces, while the centralized Ottoman regime, based on the sultan's abso­ lute authority, was breaking down.”
	* The _ayan_ were generally of religious or military background, but the primary condition for belong to that status group was personal wealth, and these groups had the best opportunities for accruing wealth. “Thus in the Ottoman city the religious and military upper class and the class of merchants and financiers consisted largely of the same persons.“

Bussow and Meier, “Ottoman Corporatism”

* B and M identify two key arguments:
	* 1\. “In this article we posit that we can much better understand a number of classical topics in the historiography of the modem Middle East- among them the so-called "millet system," tribe-state relations, and the politics of notables-if we take a broader perspective, which encompasses the entire range of group formation and group dynamics, and which we propose to call "Ottoman corporatism ."
	* 2\. Drawing from observations from the Syrian lands between the eighteenth and twentieth centuries, we argue that in the early-modem and modem Ottoman domains, social organisation and state formation are continuous and intricately linked processes that cannot be grasped if looked at only through the lens of "state-society " relations .
* Theoretical background: Joyce and Mukerjee’s notion of “logistical power,” backed up by Michael Mann’s “infrastructure power.” James S. Scott’s notion of states employing “deliberate abstraction” vis-a-vis the “facts on the ground.” Rudolf Schlogel’s idea of early modern societies as “agglomerations of groups” defined by the means of communication (personal/performative or primarily written) linking them.
* Early Western writings on Islamic law saw it as lacking a notion of the “corporation” or “legal personality” of collectives, but revisionist scholarship has shown that the “professional group” as a legal entity existed from the 11th century on.
	* “In the context of this essay, we define corporations (or corporate groups) as social groups that bear collective responsibility and have some capacity for collective action” (87, citing Johansen for this understanding)
* Villages (and urban neighborhoods?) acted collectively in negotiations with the state, and had a certain degree of autonomy in these dealings, but to an extent circumscribed by law and unequal power balances, including in disputes with other sorts of groups. “The position of village communities seems most disadvantageous in their relations with the endowments (waqf) that controlled the income of village lands. Meier (2005) has shown in several cases how changes in waqf legal theory and the way Ottoman judges applied it in their courts helped endowments (legal personalities in their own right) gain the upper hand over village communities.”
* Part 2 deals with the changing role of “tribes.” “In contrast to villages, where the sources suggest that liabilities became in- creasingly individualised during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth cen- turies, the role of tribal collectives and tribal representatives definitely grew during the same period, as the imperial government strove to gain control over peripheral lands.” Especially after 1800 and a series of migrations of Najdi Bedouins to the Syrian desert, the Ottoman state attempted to enroll tribes as tax collectors and rural police forces.
	* Description of the duties of an appointed tribal sheikh: “The shaykh received a letter of agreement (rey bu:yuruldusu)from the governor. He then met with the governor and was dressed in a robe of honour. Immediately afterwards, the shaykh went to the market and sat down in some place where merchants and artisans passing by would see him. From then on it was the shaykh's duty to supervise those tribes that approached the agricultural lands.” Some similarities to the tusi of Qing China; the Ottomans apparently also had “genealogical manuals” used to distinguish tribes and clans in the Arab provinces…
	* “an all-encompassing spatial order was devised for Syria, which rested on a conceptual separation between a zone of cultivation and settled life (ma'mura), where the govern- ment was to exercise sole control over the legitimate use of violence, and the "open country" (biidiya),where various Bedouin tribes enjoyed special privi- leges. In late Ottoman administrative discourse, the ma'mura was the realm of the individual landholder, the biidiyathat of the tribal collective.” The residents of the _ma’mura_ were banned from bearing arms, but all Bedouin tribesmen had the right to do so.
* Conclusions: “Following the infrastructural logic manifest in the role of corporate groups in various sites allows for insights into the everyday functioning of the empire and the ever-changing relationships between various corporate and non- corporate groups. Our findings do not confirm what is often conferred by the image of the "mosaic society," that is, the peaceful coexistence of autonomous and self- governing entities.” Need for better study of the internal dynamics of corporate groups (how opinion was shaped, how various actors came to decisions within groups), and also for studies of the larger (hierarchical) groups held together by writing rather than by face-to-face interaction.

Faroqhi, “Ottoman Crafstmen”

* The _malikane_ system was seen as negatively impacting artisans, contrary to the state’s intentions: “When life-time tax farms were first instituted, the idea had been to protect taxpayers from the exactions of rapidly changing tax farmers. Appointed for a few years or months, these men typically had been unwilling to invest in mines, dye houses or bridges, but were only interested in collecting a maximum of revenue during their brief tenures. Life-time tax farmers, who paid a fixed rate every year for the tax farm, were expected to think of long-term perspectives and therefore to avoid killing the goose that laid the golden eggs.” In fact, the subcontractors who did most actual tax collection faced the same incentives towards short-term extraction that the previous class of tax farmers had.
	* Result: general movement of artisans from cities to rural environment, where they could more easily avoid tax agents.
	* “One might even say that the integration of provincial elites into the Ottoman system, which the grants of life-time tax farms seem to have quite effectively secured, was achieved at the expense of artisans and artisan production.”
	* “This line of reasoning may also link up with the argument, much discussed in the 1960s and 1970s, that the crisis in Ottoman craft production was the effect of European demand for raw materials. As producers were unable to pass on increased costs to their customers, this demand led not to an expansion of raw material production, but merely to ‘profit squeezes’.41 When examining the reasons why costs could not be passed on and the reasons why, in spite of Naima’s injunctions, the Ottoman bureaucracy avoided protectionism, we are concerned with a political issue, namely, the functioning of the Ottoman state apparatus.42 We can thus reformulate the ‘profit squeeze’ hypothesis by stating that Ottoman artisans were especially vulnerable to pressures originating from the European world economy. This vulnerability was due to the manner in which the Ottoman state operated and to the peculiar manner in which artisans and the Ottoman political class related to one another.” 94

Quataert, “Clothing Laws”

* In 1829, Sultan Mahmud II eliminated old sumptuary codes distinguishing communities and occupations in favor of a uniform mandate to wear the fez, a symbol solely of loyalty to the state. Part of Mahmud II’s attack not only on the elites (janissaries), but also on workers’ guilds.
