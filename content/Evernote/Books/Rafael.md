Opens with two critiques of the preexisting historiography on Catholicism's spread in the Philippines (De la Costa and Phelan): first, older works "take the structure of Christianity for granted... and once taken for granted, it is seen ultimately to have exercised a mitigating influence;" to Rafael, Christianity both "sought to define the boundaries of native culture while at the same time claiming an attachment to an origin outside of those boundaries," and therefore was structurally ambiguous, having elements foreign to both Spaniards and native converts. Second, both De la Costa and Phelan ignore the question of Christianity's transmission in Philippine vernaculars. Focus on the inherently _random_ and _fragmented_ nature of native Filipinos' interpretation of the Catholic message.

"The penchant for hooking onto discrete words in the friar's sermon results in some kind of native submission. But it is a submission purchased at the expense of marginalizing the meaning and intent behind the discourse of authority. What is depicted here is therefore a social order premised not on consensus between ruler and ruled but on the fragmentation and hermeneutic displacement of the very basis of consensus: language." 7

Uses the example of an interview with the former peasant rebel leader Pedro Calosa to give an example of  "recontextualization" of fragments into the "historical particularity" of the "local" culture which Rafael believes characterizes Tagalog translation. To Rafael, Tagalog political history (in common w the rest of Southeast Asia) makes "localization" and constant renegotiation of loyalties/genealogies habitual.

Comment that in Mexico and Peru the Spanish friars seized on the largest or most prestigious indigenous languages and turned them into the dominant "_indio_" languages across a formerly more diverse region, while in the Philippines, with fewer coercive means at their disposal, they found themselves learning a greater variety of languages, compounding the problem of vernacular proselytization.

Second chapter: starts with Antonio de Nebrija's 1492 grammar of the Castilian language. The foundational role of Latin grammar in defining the Castilian language. Francisco Blancas de San José's _Arte_ of the Tagalog language as modelled on  Castilian grammar; importation of Latin grammatical concepts and eventual replacement of the written language by Roman characters. Need to reconstruct vernacular language to come closer to divine origin of language as such. This goal fits together with making the Tagalog language more familiar and easier to learn for Spaniards. Ultimately: the Spaniards sought a precise relation b/w speech and writing, with language ultimately subservient to meaning, while the Tagalog writing system focused on evoking potential sounds, to be interpreted according to the reader's "sensations" rather than transmitting "sense."

My comment: I can't help but notice that the passage which Rafael quotes from _Noli me tangere_ uses two metaphors about conflict with foreign groups: first, when the priest says "Ye great sinners, captives of the Moro pirates of the spirit...," and second, in the listeners' interpretation of the sermon, where they recall a painting of San Francisco "fending off  Chinese invasion during the first years of discovery."

* Two cases where Christianity makes itself legible by way of defining sides: the Christians (including Spaniards and Tagals) vs. enemy invaders. Mobilization of memories of past conflict to define group identity even where some of the "content" of the group's belief might not be shared. If the goal of these sorts of sermons is ultimately to create and maintain political "submission," then perhaps the native audiences of Father Damaso's sermon "fished out" more or less the meaning they were intended to.
* Comparison to past weeks' discussions of confessionalization and "internal missions" in Europe, particularly the discussion of belief vs. behavior in Gregory, _Salvation at Stake_. To what extent can we be sure that "content" really mattered at all times to all missionaries?

Gruzinski: "Everything led to confusion and misunderstanding: the Nahua _Mictlan_ chosen to render Christian hell was but one of the abodes of the dead and what is more a freezing place; the Christian heaven designated by the term ilhuicatl had little in common with the indigenous Empyrean realm and its 13 levels; _In tloque in nahuaque_ \- "the master of the near and the far" - that the friars took to signify God originally described Ometeotl, the Lord of the Duality of whome Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl were two of many manifestations. _Tonantzin_, chosen as the name for the Virgin Mary, had once served to designate one of the forms of the mother goddess, and so on... was it necessary to use a European terminology completely impenetrable to the Indians or to build bridges by extracting equivalences, sources of infinite misunderstanding?" 186
