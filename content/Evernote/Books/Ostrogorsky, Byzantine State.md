Continuous struggle beginning c. 9th century to minimize large landholdings in favor of a “smallholding” peasantry which was seen as the state’s key economic/military, and which was increasingly subordinated to that state. Ongoing tug-of-war between imperial state and landed aristocracy; “natural” movement was for the wealthy to pick up more and more land as peasants failed, especially during times of crisis; state had to intervene, at first by favoring relatives/neighbors in land transfers, then by simply forbidding such transfers and forcibly resettling tenants as “free” farmers, in order to safeguard unmediated access to taxes and manpower. Strong parallels with Chinese states from Three Kingdoms on — a worthwhile topic of discussion is why this structural problem emerged in these two regions at a similar time, how/why it was recognized as a problem by one group of elites, and how it was handled. First millennium AD sees growth and decline of aristocracy in both empires (e..g Tackett) 

* Story really begins from Heraclius’ organization of themes, which was elaborated for next 2/3 centuries: settlements of soldier-farmers which doubled as administrative districts. Emperor’s direct control of each region was really over the _stratiotai_ of each themes. This change made possible due to destruction of the old aristocracy brought about by 7th century warfare: 
	* “The large estates which had dominated the early Byznatine period were considerably reduced in number after the crisis at the end of the sixth and the beginning of the seventh centuries and they suffered severely under the hostile attacks which followed. It is scarcely conceivable that the old landed estates could have survived to any significant extent the assaults of the Avars and Slavs on the one hand, and the Persians and then the Arabs on the other. As far as can be seen, they did in fact disappear, and were replaced by smallholders, i.e. free peasants who took possession of the fallow land and the _stratiotai_ who formed the new army of the themes.” 134 Dominant story of Heraclian dynasty is depopulation + destruction of great families, followed by period of reclamation + resettlement. Next phase waits for “natural" regeneration of new landholding elite
* Nicephorus’ reforms largely aimed at increasing empire’s power of taxation (esp. over those (poor farmers) previously exempt, e.g. ecclesiastical tenants and foreign slaves) and colonization — sign that land was still abundant and largely held by small owners in his reign (802-811). 186-191
* The beginning of the Macedonian Dynasty sees transition period: “The legislation of Leo VI reflects not only the establishment of imperial omnipotence, but also the growing strength of the Byzantine aristocracy, a process which in its later stages was to shake the absolutism of the Emperor and undermine the whole edifice of the state. The beginnings of this development reach back into the eighth century, for by that time a number of dynasties of magnates had already made their appearance. But by the time of Leo VI, the aristocracy already possessed such power, and had already advanced so far towards becoming a class apart, having enforced acceptance of their privileges, that the _Tactica_ of Leo VI expressly recommend that the office of _strategus_ and the higher commisisions should be given only to persons of rank and wealth. An increasingly marked social differentiation thus made itself felt, so much so that even the imperial government had to take it into account. The danger this represented to the life of the state went unrecognized by Leo’s administration…” 255. At first the higher tiers of state are drawn in by the charisma and power of the landholders, without foreseeing that they will drain the state of resources and ultimately provide an alternate power base capable of threatening Constantinople… 
* First emperor to challenge aristocracy was Romanus Lecapenus (of Armenian peasant origin). In his own words: “The small-holding is of great benefit by reason of payment of state taxes and the duty of military service; this advantage would be completely lost if the number of small-hldrs were to be diminished.” 272
	* In 922, Romanus reintatet lost restrictions on land transfer from Leo Vi’s time, and gives rights of pre-emption to five groups ahead of any purchasers from outside family/village. However, even the harsh punishments meted out to violators of this policy did not stop the magnates from gaining ground during the famine of 928, especially due to hostility of local officials. “The landed proprietors and the officials together formed a caste. Just as it was the natural ambition of a favorably placed official to acquire property in his province, so it was the constant ambition of a wealthy land-owner to break into the official class, and thus to create for himself the necessary social importance and useful connections either by acceptation an official position or through the acquisition of an official title. As a rule, the ‘powerful’ was both a landed proprietor and an official. The will of the central government was opposed by the solid determination of the Economically most powerful and socially most respected sections of the community. Those who were responsible for executing the orders of the Emperor were largely interested in frustrating them.” 275
	* Also an interesting discussion of why the small-holders might prefer the “patronage” of great families over direct submission to the state; “In actual fact the state was not defending the rights and independence of small property-holders as the imperial novels try to claim. It was defending its own rights over the alienation and direction of the small-holders — rights which were being challenged by the great land-owning aristocracy.” _Manpower,_ not land, is the major bone of contention in this period, during which population seems to have been roughly in sync with carrying limit of land
	* This struggle occurs exactly as Byzantine military power reaches its apogee, especially vis-a-vis Arabs — another sign that population was high but not yet enough so to truly immiserate the populace
* Constantine Porphyrogenitus’s councilor Theophilus extended Romanus’ work to immediately return all property recently purchased by the rich to the peasantry, without compensation (280-281).
* Nicephorus Phocas, an aristocrat himself, turns back his predecessors’ policy, but increased the property limit for soldiers from four to twelve pounds of gold, in order to ensure they could afford more expensive equipment. Army to be based more on lesser aristocracy than on truly “poor” smallholders. Also restricted growth of church land, which was a major competitor to aristocrats + the state. 
* Aristocracy starting to reach a limit of own growth/wealth leads to pressure for external expansion (internal to external game): “The movement to acquire more land worked itself out in two ways. One form it took was the absorption of small-holdings in the Byzantine provinces, undermining the existing social structure of the Byzantine state, the other reached out beyond the frontiers of the Empire, contriving to win over land from the enemies of the empire. The acquisition of territory by the state in the East in the tenth century was above all the work of the aristocracy of Asia Minor.” 288 Outside expansion must have somewhat reduced pressure of internal conflict.
* John Tzimisces: “As a result, the formerly independent small land owners became _paroikoi_ of the state, in that the state deprived them not only of the right to dispose freely of their own land, but also of their freedom of movement.” 295
* Basil II’s emperorship was contested early on by great houses, especially  that of Phocas; after the magnates lost the civil wars, Basil attacked the base of their power, directly confiscating as much land as possible from these families, with no grace period on its acquisition. Made aristocrats responsible for paying the taxes of peasants who had gone into arrears. Also limited growth of large monasteries. (305-307 discusses all this.) bgOverall great expansion of state treasury at expense of landholders at Asia Minor; I wonder what effect his would have on wars in the East… 
* Side argument that land was so important for elites partially because private economic enterprise was entirely restricted, especially in Constantinople, by imperial control. No other profitable use for capital but investment in land. 
	* As a result this was also the only avenue by which officials could enrich themselves and ensure continued good fortune for their families, especially from a unified power base 

Fascinated by the Bogomils, apparently a Manichaean and ascetic sect which rejected outward wohip, ritual, and the church order. Developed out of Paulinians and served as roots for the Cathars and Albigenses. 268-269.

* “The heresy gained ground most rapidly in times of crisis and oppression, for it was in such periods that its basically pessimistic outlook, which rejected not only definite order, but the terrestrial world as such, found richest nourishment, and in which its protest made its most effective impression.” Link between world-negating beliefs (infinite negative freedom) and temporal “crisis”
