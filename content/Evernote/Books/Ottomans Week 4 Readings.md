Stefan Winter - History of the Alawis

Chapter 4
While medieval sources on the ‘Alawis almost exclusively focus on religious figures, documentation from the Ottoman period provides much more detail on mundane economic and political negotiations. Example again that perception of “early modern” rise in importance of merchants, petty bourgeoise and bureaucrats is a function of increased source availability?

* “the growing exposure of the ‘Alawi community to the Ottoman state and its bureaucracy has bequeathed us a rich archive of negotiation, contestation, and co-optation that can serve as the basis for a new prosopography of the ‘Alawis in the early modern period.”

After the mid-seventeenth century, the Ottoman administration in Syria shifted from _timar_ land grants to commercial tax farming (_iltizam_). A growth in both governor-tax farmer “dynasties” and a resilient local elite of tax farming intermediaries, including a number of ‘Alawi families, including the Shamshins and the Bayt al-Shillif. These families became for the first time a “local gentry” with both autonomy vis-a-vis Istanbul and great power over their immediate neighbors; their wealth expanded greatly during the tobacco boom of the 18th/19th centuries, while local inequality led to migration of poorer ‘Alawis to a broader range.
Affirms that local hierarchies were not “organic” products of age-old tribal organization, but developed top-down in response to assignment of bureaucratic positions by the Ottoman state.

* What was new in the sixteenth century was the rise of iltizam tax farming. If state office had previously been tied to military service, the rapid monetization of the Ottoman and eastern Mediterranean economy and the central government’s growing need for cash revenues meant that the authorities increasingly sought to “farm out” or subcontract the collection of taxes in many provinces to local or locally rooted urban notables, financiers, or rural community leaders.”
* By the time of the earliest court records (1667), a bevy of specific taxes (even including the _jizya_) had been combined into one lump sum levied on an entire tax district, to be collected by the tax farmer according to his own methods, though with certain guarantees not to harm the people etc. “In any event, all these taxes, most of which had never been itemized in the Tahrir registers, accrued to the provincial governor’s reserve (havass-ı mirmiran) and were now assessed on the district in a single yearly lump-sum payment, rather than on individual villages or communities.” 125
* In Safita district, the Shamshin family contracted the _iltizam_ for much of the 17th and 18th centuries.
* The Shillif family which controlled tax farms in the Latakia region (northwest) also became notorious for brigandage and for their cooperation with Bedouin and Kurdish tribes in resisting Ottoman authority.

The city of Latakia began to develop as a port around the turn of the 18th century, and by the 1750s it was coming to eclipse Tripoli as the major local port city, with the French consulate relocating there in 1788. Latakia’s rise had to do with the decline of the silk trade in favor of tobacco, which was cultivated in the highlands, as well as with the migration of ‘Alawis northward and the city’s favor among Greek and Armenian Christians.

* A tobacco tax was introduced in 1690, and thereafter Latakia accounted for a majority of the entire Ottoman empire’s tobacco customs revenue. 139
* The most favored local “brand” of tobacco, called Abu Riha, was distinguished by being hung and dried through the winter, making it one of few crops to be taxed in spring rather than fall. The coordination and marketing of this cash crop favored the emergence of a commercial-agricultural elite. “If tobacco cultivation doubtless employed a growing number of ‘Alawi peasants and may help explain the community’s expansion in the northern districts of Latakia, it also produced an increasing concentration of wealth, as described by David Sneath, that was likely the very basis for the emergence of a local gentry in the region. Whereas traditional subsistence farming was organized on an individual family basis and taxes collected by a simple muqaddam, often in kind, the introduction of a new, export-oriented cash crop that could furthermore only be sold a full year after planting required an unprecedented level of investment and coordination by a new class of rural entrepreneurs such as the Rustum Aghas and the Shillifs.” 140
* The same phenomenon led to the emergence of complaints about “usury,” as many peasants became indebted to (mostly Christian) moneylenders, which further caused difficulties in tax collection.
* “The situation of the ‘Alawis vis-à-vis the rise of the tobacco industry was therefore complex but by no means unique: the introduction of commercial agriculture in economically marginal areas, the cycle of increasing indebtedness and vulnerability of peasant families, and the overuse of land and breakdown of traditional rural institutions have indeed been described as an early crisis of modernization on a worldwide scale.” (Citing Osterhammel’s _Verwandlung_ for the latter point)

"It thus appears that the economic displacements of the eighteenth century both reinforced the role of parochial leaderships in the high mountains and encouraged emigration toward the coastal and interior plains, where ‘Alawi peasants would increasingly find work as day laborers on large, Muslim-owned commercial estates." 144
Increasingly unique local administration in ‘Alawi areas, perhaps in recognition of the arrangements preferred by local tax farming elites: “Around the same time a new administrative unit was introduced in the Safita contracts that does not appear to have been applied in any other part of Syria, the hilla (literally an “encampment” or “hamlet”) as an offi- cial subdivision or township of the larger nahiye. Between 1751 and the reincorporation of a single tax farm under Saqr ibn Mahfuz al-Shibli’s care in 1782, these hillas served as the basis for the rise a new ‘Alawi elite in the region.”

* “Saqr ibn Mahfuz al-Shibli Shamsin was arguably the most prominent figure in ‘Alawi history between the death of Makzun al-Sinjari in the thirteenth century and the rise of Hafiz al-Asad between 1966 and 1970. His career, which spanned six decades before his death at age eighty in 1813,121 marks the high point of ‘Alawi feudal autonomy under Ottoman rule.” 152
* Equation of “decentralization” and “privatization”: “If the Ottoman state had already shown little interest in their religious identity and social organization at the time of Syria’s integration into the empire, the decentralization or more properly the privatization of provincial government and tax collection offices beginning in the seventeenth century brought to the fore a new class of local or localized petty gentry who henceforth dominated the rural hinterland.” 158
* “If the Ottomans had taken little issue with the ‘Alawis as a group in previous centuries and occasionally even deplored the absence of an autochthonous leadership, the rise of Shibli Shamsin,‘Ali al-Shillif, or Saqr Mahfuz as lords of multiple tax districts, capable of raising large peasant armies, negotiating with the authorities in court, conspiring with the Shihabi emirs, or assisting the governor of Damascus, destined them to play an active part in the ever more contentious struggle for power in the Syrian provinces in the nineteenth century.” 159
* ‘Alawis increasingly came to be seen as a united political force defined by sectarian identity and capable of organizing substantial resistance to the imperial state, though they largely were left to their own devices as long as tax collection proceeded smoothly.

Tezcan

* “The Second Empire came to be marked administratively by an early modern state, as opposed to a medieval dynastic institution; culturally by an early modern sensibility; economically by a more market-oriented economy; legally by a more unified legal system that came to exert some authority over the dynasty; monetarily by a more unified currency system; politically by the development of a type of limited government that grew out of the interaction between the legal developments of the time and such processes as “civilization” and “proto-democratization;” and socially by a relatively less stratified society. I use the term “proto-democratization” to refer to the process through which a much larger segment of the imperial administration came to consist of men whose social origins were among the commoners, the very people who used to be known as “outsiders” to the previous ruling elite whose leadership was dominated by the military slaves of the emperor. Thus more and more men whose backgrounds were in finance or trade came to occupy significant positions in the government of the empire, replacing those military slaves and civilizing the imperial polity.” 10
* “The first chapter explores the rela­tionship between the development of a monetary economy in the sixteenth century and the rising political profile of jurists’ law. I argue that the sixteenth ccntury was marked by the monetization of the Ottoman economy and the political empowerment of socioeconomic groups that benefited from this pro­ cess, such as merchants and financiers. These changes led to the expansion of the “political nation;” that is, the body of people who could influence or play a legitimate role in the government.” Increasing importance of _sheriya_ over _kanun_.

Salzmann

* Sees the 17th-18th centuries as a period of “decentralization” and “privatization” which laid the foundations for renewed “centralization” after 1812. Suggests that the Ottoman trajectory, while different from the traditional model of “ideal” state development, may have been necessary or at least desirable in the Ottoman case; in particular the practice of lifetime tax farming grants (_malikane_) “created diffused but interrelated loci of state power.” Call to study ancien regimes not only from the perspective of the “central” state, but with reference to the “constellation of discrete but interlocking institutions,” the “micro economies of state power,” that made up the actual functioning of the empire. 395
* Starting point: the early modern Ottoman state was characterized by sultanic control of most land, social mobility within the military, low degree of monetization, and difficult overland transport with heavy state oversight of commerce, which inhibited the formation of concentrated capital or hereditary landed elites.
	* This situation began to change in the sixteenth century, with increasing monetization of the countryside related to international silver flows
* The demands of war on 2 fronts in the early seventeenth century, and the rise of gunpowder, led first to the eclipse of the service cavalry by the salaried janissaries, then to the use of peasant mercenaries; overall effect was to reduce the imperial patrimony and arm potential resistance to the throne; increase in peasant rebellions linked to demobilized soldier-peasantry, as well as shift to **lump-sum monetary taxation** collected by fiscal professionals (1593-1620; later than the fiscalization of the bureaucracy noted in week 3…)
* The war against Austria in 1683-7 led to a severe fiscal crisis, which the state was unable to resolve by expropriating or taxing the wealth of newly privileged classes (attempts at which led to overthrows of 3 sultans); only alternative was move to larger (longer-term) tax farms and loans on the same. _Malikane_ contracts were introduced in 1695 to deal with this situation.
	* _Malikane_ contracts were normally divided into fractions, which could be privately traded, and lasted until the death of the original contractor.
	* The most lucrative _malikane_ auctions were reserved for “fiscal insiders” from the palace, the military, and the Istanbul ulema, who could benefit from access to credit and information. Less profitable contracts were auctioned off in the provinces. Holders had to be Muslim, but the extension of credit and remittance of funds relied heavily on Armenian banking guilds, who thus developed greatly during this period.
	* Even in the provinces, tax contracts were usually awarded not only on the basis of wealth, but also of relationships with elites; contracts were often kept quasi-hereditary as long as a given family maintained good relationships with government officials. Consolidation of local elite families, but with strong ties of obligation to central bureacucracy.
* Selim III attempted to solve the fiscal crisis caused by the French Revolution and war with Russia by reasserting state control over taxation; but he was overthrown and his successor, Mahmoud II, was forced to promise to leave the accumulated privileges of the elite untouched. Mahmud thereafter turned towards campaigns against the largest magnates, leaving the small and medium _malikane_ contracts relatively free of interference; the “medium” local elites continued to serve as an important base of social support for the state through the tanzimat period.
* Conclusion: Privatization was “not only the product of fiscal shortfalls but was also part of a process of renegotiating rights and reorganizing provincial administration in changing socioeconomic and political conditions.” 408

Green - Patriarch’s Victory

* Historians have persisted in seeing the end of the _devsirme_ as a watershed (citing Hathaway and Tezcan). According to this view, the seventeenth century saw the _reaya-askeri_ division shift to a simple nonmuslim-Muslim split, as any freeborn Muslim male was theoretically entitled to participation in the state. The resulting “bleak assessment” of Christian prospects in this time, however, “sits rather awkwardly” with evidence of Greek prosperity.  Greek merchants and office-holders (the Phanariots dominated the transdanubian empire) funded the “Greek Enlightenment” based on revived Greek-language education and book production.
* Greek merchants were noted for pursuing _beratli_ status, as formal employees of European embassies, allowing them to benefit from extraterritoriality within the empire. Historians have seen this as a sign of Orthodox antipathy to the Ottomans as well as of the counterproductive laws of the empire, which alienated a “nascent bourgeoisie,” and paved the way to the Greek revolt in the 1820s.  
* The 18th century also saw the establishment of the Patriarch of the Greek Orthodox Church as the official representative of the Greek community; the Church achieved a measure of centralized authority and influence within the empire that it had never held before.
* The major Balkan cities became increasingly “Christian” in character and population due especially to Greek urbanization, driven in the 18th C by unrest in the countryside.
* “Enough scholarship has now accumulated to allow us to state with confidence that, in the eighteenth century, the community became an essential unit of Ottoman provincial governance. By “community” we do not mean simply a group of people residing in a particular territory, but rather groups that had acquired “a legal persona as self-governing bodies” (Yaycioğlu 2008: 143).” This process was a result of the replacement of _timar_ by local elite collection of monetary taxes. The establishment of _ayan,_ local tax representatives, also implied the solidification of “community” character.
	* The figure of the _varos_, the tax collector responsible for Christians over a large area, emerged in the late 17th century; during this time, Muslim-Christian conflicts over tax payment became frequent, as Christians might be “claimed” for either neighborhood, guild, or “community” (religious) tax collectors. The important members of the Christian tax collection bodies were normally drawn from local clergy; increasing overlap of secular and religious institutions.
	* Guilds tended to be split between Muslim and Christian, with Christian guilds in particular flourishing during the 18th C.
	* Christian culture was also increasingly “Hellenized,” as ambitious Orthodox men and women acquired the Greek language in their Christian education, and a self-consciously Hellenizing “aristocracy” developed along the Aegean coast, sometimes adopting old Byzantine titles.
* In the seventeenth century, the Greek Patriarch could not punish Christians or have audiences with the sultan; by the mid 18th C he was both a frequent attendee at court in Istanbul and empowered to punish Orthodox criminals on his own authority.

Hamadeh

* "As in contemporary Europe, China, Japan, and India, the Ottoman state’s deepest concern was to preserve, or recreate, an established system of social hierarchies; at the same time, the boundaries between different social groups were becoming ever more porous." 4
* "Ottoman architecture in the eigh- teenth century was hardly, as many would have it, the expression of an overarching Westernizing inclination among the ruling elite. It was, in fact, profoundly hybrid and remarkably innovative. First and foremost, it was the dominant venue for the visual enactment of the changing social and cultural landscape of Istanbul, the material realization of what I have called the _décloisonnement,_ or “open- ing up,” of the city’s social and cultural fabric." 4
* "As crucial as external pressures may have been, deep-rooted internal changes were far more significant in provoking a crisis in imperial self-representation. Transformations in the social and eco- nomic structure in the last hundred and fifty years signaled the gradual erosion of the system of hier- archies that had exemplified the Ottoman world order. Shifting professional boundaries, the rising power of certain groups (most notably the central bureaucracy), the growing integration of the janis- saries in the social fabric, and, generally, the contraction of the _devîirme_ system all meant, for many at the time, the breakdown of order and stability in the empire.7 By the eighteenth century, these transformations had become integral to the social landscape of the capital. As the boundaries between ruling elite and tax-paying society were becoming increasingly fluid, so too the contours of social and professional groups were becoming increasingly permeable. And as the political arena expanded, social and financial power was becoming accessible to more and more individuals, both inside and outside the ruling circle.8 If for contemporary critics these developments heralded the empire’s decline, they were also symptomatic of what, in Europe, characterized the early modern period. In eighteenth-century Istanbul, this study contends, urban middle classes were becoming more visible in the public sphere and permeated visual and literary culture. Greater social, financial, and professional mobility revealed noticeable changes in consumption patterns,9 as well as in recreational and cultural practices. The diffusion of power began to have a visible impact on the decentralization of building, literary, and artistic patronage. Gradually, as a result, formerly stable signs of status began to wear out." 6
