Trade on the West Coast

* The most important trading families of Surat c. 1700 were the Mullas, local Bohras, and the Chellabies, a Turkish family. Cites Alexander Hamilton's travel account on Mulla Abdul Ghafur as the largest trader of Surat in his time, who single-handedly "drove a trade equal to the English East India Company." 25
* Three basic ways for a ship-owning merchant to operate: "He could hire his ship  out to more than a single merchant by taking on a cargo of goods on commenda: the ship-owner guaranteed to pay the shippers the value of the goods (principal) and the agreed ratio of profits, unless the goods were not sold for some reason. Alternatively, the ship-owner could become a merchant in his own right by borrowing money on bottomry; the ship itself became the security for the loan and whatever money was made after paying the loan and interest was the ship-owner's earnings. Finally, the ship-owner could borrow in respondentia; agreed to sell goods on board as in commenda, returning the value of the loan plus the interest only if the goods arrived safely at their destination." 25
* By 1740, Bombay had emerged as the preferred destination for the Surat Banias’ transportation of bullion from their Red Sea ventures, due to the protection afforded by British convoys, and this bullion in turn was cited in a British merchants’ petition as vital to the financing of the city’s trade (especially the country trade). 44-46
* The chief brokers for the English at Bombay were the Parsi family of Rustamji Manek, and this family was the first case of Parsis becoming important commercially through association with the British; a number of other Parsi families followed them to Bombay in the early-mid 18th century.
	* The Parsi Panchayat of Bombay was founded in 1728, and the early Parsi community was chiefly engaged in small trading and the provisioning of the Company, especially with timber for the docks. 51
	* Citing D.L. White, “Parsis as Entrepreneurs in West India,” a UVA PhD dissertation from 1981.
* After the British takeover of Bengal, exports of silver to fund EIC trading ventures led to severe deflation. In the 1760s-80s, Bombay was particularly dependent on this source of bullion, and received more in stipends (160,000 pounds per year) than was sent to the China trade. Along with this, the “money market” linking Bengal to West India increasingly fell apart. 61
* In 1683, the British noted that “whenever the demand for hundis on Bengal increased in Agra, the bankers in Bengal would promptly advance liquid funds against bills of exchange… such long distance movements regulated entirely by commercial considerations would have been inconceivable in the reign of Muhammad Shah.”
	* “When the movement of money from coast to coast resumed again in the late eighteenth century, the motive force was no longer the market, but financial operations conducted by the Surat and Benaras shroffs between the Bengal and Bombay presidencies in response to the political requirements of the English East India Company.” 64

Mid-century English Victories

* In the period between 1750 and 1770 the British successfully established maritime supremacy over the west coast of India, defeating local navies and establishing the security of ships holding British passes, which became “the exclusive trade permit in operation along the west coast.” 82
* During the nawabship of Safdar Khan from 1751-1758, the \[English\] Surat Council increasingly cooperated with local Banias to negotiate exemptions from tolls and taxes for its allies, putting it into frequent conflict with the local government, but also winning it a reputation for being able to protect merchants which worked with the Company. After Safdar Khan’s death, the “shroffs” of the city supposedly urged the English to “seize the Castle, become the city’s qiladar and prevent a further deterioration of the situation,” and offered financial backing for the venture. 93
	* However, the English decided not to directly intervene in the succession dispute, and as a result the clique of Ali Navas Khan and Sidi Mas’ud retained power in the city, and immediately began to put pressure on the British position as shippers.

Challenge and Consolidation

* In the early 1790s, at a time when the Bombay Presidency found its accustomed banking partners unwilling to offer good rates on transfers from Bombay, the Company was equally unable to draw substantial funds from the Surat shroffs, because most of their capital was invested in the cotton trade with China. 193-196
	* Specifically, the shroffs “advanced considerable funds for the purchase of raw cotton” (196), which was however shipped to Canton by British (and Parsi?) merchants. 196
