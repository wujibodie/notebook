Introduction

* Starting point: Monetary phenomena are a signal of deeper economic shifts. Marc Bloch says they are "like a seismograph that not only registers earth tremors, but sometimes brings them about." 17
* Disagrees with Cipolla (and Keynes) that the late medieval depression was _unilaterally_ caused by monetary deflation; instead, money supply and economic activity are mutually influencing. Fischer's Equation of P=(MV)/T or PT=MV is a "relation," i.e. a change in any variable changes the others; priority of causation cannot be determined by theory, only "history."

Chapter 3

* Constantine introduced the most "modern" currency system of antiquity, with the solid gold "solidus" stamped with Constantine's image, along with 1/3 solidii (triens), and silver and copper coins. Later "barbarian" kings copied this system, but coined smaller and smaller amounts with larger admixtures of base metal, showing that the need for large coins decreased while gold became scarcer. Charlemagne did away with circulating gold and made the silver penny the basic currency; but even this underwent steady debasement after his death.
* In the later Roman empire, gold tended to flow eastwards, to Asia Minor, Egypt, and Persia (India as well?). The Byzantines thus retained a reasonable circulation of gold until the Arab invasions, which cut them off from the main stores of gold.
	* Similarly, Spain was the first part of Christian Europe where the minting of gold resumed, as part of the process of reconquista, wherein huge amounts of Muslim gold were looted. Between 1250 and 1300, Florence, Genoa, Lucca, Perugia, Venice, and Milan all began successfully minting their own coins, while France and England failed to do so until the 14th century.
	* Nicholas Oresme, adviser to Charles V of France, was the "originator" of monetary theory.
* The 14th century crisis in Europe saw a reduction in the price of gold, but the recovery in the 1400s saw a corresponding rise in value (or: a fall in prices _relative_ to gold) which made "the search for gold extremely profitable." 37

Chapter 8

* Argues that the crucial changes in the European economy were already starting by 1450, and the overall tendency towards greater activity and higher prices would likely have been a result of trends like population growth, technological development, and more sophisticated financial instruments even without the (relatively modest) influx of African and Caribbean gold.
* Especially interesting is the rise in Tyrolian, Saxon, Bohemian mining from the 1450s to 1520s; a silver influx simultaneous with the gold influx via Iberia, which perhaps supports the argument that deeper economic trends incentivizing the search for precious metals were the more decisive factor in the eventual "price revolution."
	* The Fuggers in particular made their fortune in silver, financed new mines across the German-dominated world, and pioneered new techniques of credit/debt, including the bonds which paid off the electors who made Charles V emperor of Germany.

Chapter 9

* On East African gold, most of which went to Goa to be coined and traded for Indian goods: "In the 16th and 17th centuries the two main areas of India, Hindustan and the Deccan, paid very high prices for gold and even more for silver. "Silver hunger" attracted merchants, and Portuguese _bandeirantes_ ransacked the African jungle for deposits of silver, without success. **The lesson here is the same \[as in the case of the Mina\]: the demand might be far away, in India and China, but price differentials were what determined trading routes and prospecting activities.** Although trade depends on precious metals, the production of precious metals also depends on trade. Coins are, in the last analysis, commodities."
* Claims that after the Portuguese reached Malacca and the Spanish founded Manila, there existed "a world market for cotton cloth, silk, porcelain, gold and silver, which made precious metals the medium of direct exchange with the three main export products of India and China." 96
	* Also claims that in the Gujarati countryside, tribute was paid in gold and silver. Investigate
* "In the first half of the 16th century, the biggest profits in trade with the East came from exporting, in the following order: coral, vermillion, mercury, copper, silver, and only after that, gold." Venice for example had largely replaced gold with commodities in its trade for Eastern goods by 1500, as a result of gold's relatively higher value relative to other goods in Europe.

16: Gold and Silver in Castile

* "According to Hamilton's lists, 447 million pesos were imported between 1503 and 1660, 117 million for the King and 330 million for private owners. Rather over a quarter was therefore the King's. But although it was a smaller share the King's treasure had greater international influence, since it spread rapidly throughout Europe to pay the Crown debts."
* Vilar emphasizes that c**redit was being employed at huge scale even before the entry of American gold and silver; "Spanish and European wealth _preceded_ the influx of American gold."** However, the massive debts of Castile in particular are attributable to the security afforded it by American metals; loans from Hamburg alone rose from 50,000 Flemish pounds in 1515 to 7,000,000 in 1556!
	* Thus, "**the King's share \[of silver\] hardly affected Spain, as it was already committed elsewhere;"** thus the need for ever-rising taxation at home which eventually contributed to strangling production.
