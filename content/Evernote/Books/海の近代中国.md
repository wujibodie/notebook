* Introduction
	* Briefly discusses the historiography on “local order” in Chinese cities, summarizing their arguments as showing how local elites, lineage organizations, and local-place solidarity provided means of unifying the disparate groups that made up cities such as Hankou, Shanghai, Guangzhou, or Tianjin in the 19th century. However, Murakami views these studies as not sufficiently describing the diversity of Chinese urban experience, and considers Minnan a region where there was no “elite stratum” focused on providing order, as up until the 20th century foreign trade was scattered in many hands rather than concentrating and forming the basis for a dominant merchant-gentry. 19-20

Chapter One - Southern Coastal Populace and the Qing Dynasty

* The system of coastal governance set up by the Qing after 1683 was shaken by two sorts of changes in the later Qing: shifts in the underlying geography of trade, and problems of public order.
	* Minnan traders, operating out of Xiamen, were an increasingly large presence in Guangzhou during the Qianlong reign, by the end of which there were 8 洋行 and numerous 商行 in the city, which also organized illegal trading ventures directly to foreign countries. By the early 1810s there was only one Yanghang, and by 1821 even this was bankrupt. After this there were small ventures from minor ports (like Zhaoan in Zhangzhou), but in general foreign goods had to be procured via Guangzhou. Murakami cites the Xiamen Zhi. 50-51
		* M dismisses piracy as a cause, because the largest pirate organizations of the period were defeated by 1810, and also dismisses the possibility of a wider economic decline, because the early 1800s were a flourishing period for East Asian maritime commerce in general.
		* Instead, sees the basic cause of Xiamen’s decline as competition from Guangdong and Zhangzhou; specifically, the direct trade routes from Xiamen to Southeast Asia controlled by large firms were replaced by smaller-scale but numerous ventures departing with foreign goods (European and SEAsian) from minor port towns near Guangzhou, to similarly upstart port towns in Zhang-Quan, bypassing the central customs office at Xiamen. A smaller but related piece of the decline was the rise of smuggling from minor, unofficial ports on Taiwan to various mainland destinations, bypassing the official 5 harbors with their legal endpoint in Xiamen. More ships called at Aomen and Jiangmen from Zhangzhou than Xiamen, and Xiamen was only a minor destination for junks leaving Singapore; also, tribute ships from Sulu began to be routed through Manila rather than directly heading for Xiamen, and Manila’s trade (English and Spanish) started to orient itself more to the PRD in this time (53-55)
	* During this period Minnanese traders were highly active and successful in foreign trade, but they increasingly based themselves in Guangzhou, Chaozhou, Ningbo, or (soon) Shanghai; “abandonment” of Xiamen by Minnan merchants. 58
	* The shift of trade away from Xiamen did not cause the Qing state perceptible fiscal problems, but it did make control of ocean trade far more difficult, as the major organs of state oversight (the _hang_) withered away
* After 1810, although the major pirate fleets were largely broken up, a phenomenon emerged of smaller pirate groups using small, nimble warships which shifted between smuggling and banditry, while posing as ordinary merchants. These ships began to include opium in their smuggling portfolios, and this habit entered government circles after the pirate Zhang Bao 張保 surrendered and was made vice-admiral at Penghu in 1820. 63
	* So very many parallels to the 1600s! Similar actors, events, and locations, with one very new element…
* One of the earliest government reports on the Guangdong opium trade, from 1822, already lists several known traders in the PRD from Zhangzhou (including Zhao’an) as well as Chaozhou. 65
* The Qing court attempted to task the 閩粵 and 閩潮 _huiguan_ with controlling sojourners from those areas in Tianjin and Guangzhou respectively, but at least in Tianjin, the head of the Min-Yue huiguan knowingly withheld information about opium use and sales among residents in the huiguan from authorities; the affinity and responsibility felt by huiguan leaders towards local-place compatriots often overruled call to prevent their illegal activities… 120-121
* A Quan-Zhang huiguan was founded in Shanghai in 1831, and soon came to represent a very numerous population of sojourners, some of whom were considered upstanding citizens, but others of whom were known to carry on illegal trade (although at first authorities were more concerned with salt smuggling than opium). “玉石混雜” 122
	* In 1840, when sojourners from Chaozhou and Huizhou were being suspected of involvement in opium smuggling, a joint 潮惠會館 was established in Shanghai for the first time… 122

Chapter 7 - The Losers of Asian Internal Competition

* After its opening as a treaty port, Xiamen proved uncompetitive with other ports due to the lack of English-speaking mediators and warehouses, which made it difficult for foreigners to make large purchases or retail in the countryside, and the combination of unusually high likin rates and unsettled public order around the Small Swords and Taiping rebellions disadvantaged it vis-a-vis Shantou, Fuzhou, or the small unofficial ports nearby. 371-372
* The gap between Xiamen’s imports and exports began to grow in the 1870s, with an annual trade deficit (on paper) of ~15 million taels (w/ only 3 million taels in exports!) by the end of the Qing. 373
	* How to fit this fact with the end of opium imports around the same time?
* In 1880, of 4080 chests of opium imported into Xiamen, 840 were consumed in Xiamen, 840 went to Quanzhou and Tong’an, while over half, 2400 chests, were reexported up the Jiulongjiang to Zhangzhou and to the hill regions of west Fujian. The majority of tea exported via Taiwan similarly came down the Beixi from the hilly regions around Longyanzhou. Thus in the 19th century Zhangzhou (with the rural counties beyond it) was a far more important trade partner for Xiamen than Quanzhou. **The replacement of foreign opium with local poppies, and eclipse of the oolong trade, would have represented a serious shift in Xiamen’s orientation from Zhangzhou to Quanzhou/Tong’an.** 377
* The tea industry in northern Taiwan began to take off in the 1860s and 1870s due in large part to foreign investment, with HSBC a major source of capital. The 媽振館 was an important local intermediary in these investments 378
	* "Mazhen" comes from the English "Merchant"
* An attempt to modernize Minnan's sugar industry through the erection of a western-style machinized factory by a Taiwanese citizen with business experience in Java, Guo Zhenxiang 郭禎祥, floundered against opposition from local officials and sugar producers.  Similar attempts failed for related reasons, and the sugar export sector of southern Fujian eventually died out entirely.
* Tobacco was a major export to Taiwan, and after 1895 this business was largely in the hand of small-time smugglers with Taiwanese passports, who were able to avoid customs duties on both sides of the Strait, stimulated by the chance to manufacture cigarettes for duty-free sale to Japan; but after Taiwan enacted a tobacco monopoly system in 1905, centralizing cigarette production in a few licensed factories and encouraging local tobacco cultivation, the profitability of this market largely died out. 403-404
* At the end of the 19th century Xiamen was center of a sizable trade in iron pots and pans, with old iron implements being shipped to Xiamen from Singapore and Hong Kong to be melted and recast in Zhangzhou or Tong'an for export to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. However, this trade began to die out in the early 20th century as local production and higher-quality Shanghai manufactures outcompeted Minnan's recycled pots

* Fujianese bricks/tiles were also driven off the Southeast Asian markets by cheaper (industrialized) German wares
* South Fujianese porcelain was still exported from Xiamen, but its overall market share in Southeast Asia was reduced by competition from Shantou, Japan, Singapore, and Vietnam
* After 1904, the last Western steamship company running Xiamen-Taiwan ceased operating in Taiwan, being outcompeted by the Osaka firm OSK Lines 大阪商船會社. 408
* In the early years after Taiwan's annexation by Japan, most of the tea sold at Danshui went to Xiamen merchants, and a number of Fujianese tea workers were given special dispensations to migrate to Taiwan; a number of people from these groups applied to the Japanese embassy for Taiwan passports after returning to Xiamen, according to a Shenbao report from 1898. 411
	* After 1899, Japan raised export duties on Taiwanese tea, and exports from north Taiwan began to be oriented towards the Japanese market (without tariffs) rather than the American one via Xiamen
	* After 1900, the  Xiamen branch of Taiwan Bank began to cease offering loans to tea merchants
* Ei disagrees with the British consular opinion that Huaqiao remittances mostly went towards the purchase of foreign luxuries. **Instead, they in large measure helped sustain the massive domestic trade in daily necessities, such as sugar, beans, tea, grains, seafood, which was largely in the hands of Fujianese merchants** (split into those of Chinese, Taiwanese/Japanese, and Southeast Asian/European nationalities) 435-436
* Of Xiamen shops dealing in Taiwanese tea in 1912, about half were registered to Qing and Japanese citizens each; there was also one run by a Spanish citizen, presumably a Fujianese merchant who had gotten citizenship in Manila before 1899. There were also 2 Spanish merchant houses dealing in sea products.
