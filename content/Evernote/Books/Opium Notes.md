* Perhaps the most important contribution overseas Chinese made to improv- ing living conditions in Xiamen was investing in the provision of clean running water. Being a small island with no river system, Xiamen had always suffered from a dearth of potable water. The notorious “seven ponds, eight rivers, and thirteen streams” on the island were heavily polluted, and wells dug into the reclaimed area of the urban sector produced only brackish and hard water, not suitable for human consumption. For decades, the majority of Xiamen’s residents had relied on “water boats” (_shui chuan_) to ferry water from inland areas. Fresh water thus was a rare and expensive commodity in Xiamen, costing 1 to 1.85 yuan per ton for water from the Jiulong River or as much as 10 to 15 cents per dan for superior spring water that porters shouldered in buckets from the White-Deer Cave on the island.92 Despite paying premium prices for drinking water, Xiamen’s residents were not guaranteed delivery as storms and winds often disrupted shipment. And because water was transported in barrels and had to pass through many hands before reaching the consumer, it was not just dirty but also a means for communicable diseases to spread.
* In 1923, Huang Yizhu founded the Commercial Xiamen Water PLC (Shang- ban Xiamen zilaishui gufen youxian gongsi) with initial capital of 1.1 million yuan_._ Huang provided 400,000 yuan himself, while the rest was raised among overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia. As the company’s largest shareholder, Huang set a very high standard for his project—when looking for a company to build a reservoir and a water-processing plant, Huang gave the job to the German Siemens Company, believing it was better equipped to do the job even though a Japanese company had come in with a lower bid. When construction was completed in 1927, the Commercial Xiamen Water PLC boasted a one-million-ton (lifang mi) main reservoir in the southeast corner of the island and a water-processing plant capable of purifying five thousand tons of water daily near the island’s famous Nanputuo Temple." 151-152
* "While overseas Chinese might have salvaged Xiamen from decline, the port’s overdependence on its emigrants had unforeseen and unfavorable consequences as well. Chen Zhongping - Origins of Chinese Chambers of Commerce

* "Guandu-shangban" enterprises emerging out of the self-strengthening movement;  model for the monopoly bureau? "These enterprises were "modern" because they adopted the technology recently developed in the West and used a Western-style joint-stock company to attract merchant investment; however, they operated not only under merchant manag- ers but also under official supervisors such as Sheng." 159
* "Given the basic characteristics of the late Qing chambers of commerce in the Lower Yangzi region, it is not fruitful to search for their origins only in some organizational evolution from guilds to Wester style chambers of commerce. Rather, **the key to their rise was t emergence of elite merchants as a distinct social group** and especial their interactions with late Qing officials under the Western challenge and in the context of guilds and other business institutions, including guandu-shangban enterprises and bureaus of commerce." 168
* "ina. To resist foreign intrusion, late Qing officials and local elites perceived a com- mon necessity to promote Chinese chambers of commerce to unite merchants and advance government-business cooperation. **At the same time, local elites, especially elite merchants, also promoted chambers of commerce to expand their influence over local business and politics, and officials instead planned to use them to extend gov- ernmental control over Chinese businesses, especially modem busi- ness ventures.** This difference between elite and official interests had already become evident in the development of the guandu-shangban enterprises and bureaus of commerce from the late nineteenth century." 181
* The first guandu-shangban enterprise was the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company, founded in 1872 and sponsored by Li Hongzhang; it was seized from its erstwhile merchant managers by Sheng Xuanhuai in 1885, after which it stagnated. 182
* In 1896-97 Sheng Xuanhai proposed the foundation of an Imperial Bank of China, styled a "Shanghui," to be based on merchant capital with official supervision, with a Chamber of Commerce for all merchants in general to be attached under it; the latter proposal was rejected by the court, but the majority of the first directors and managers of the bank participated when the actual first Chamber of Commerce was organized in 1902.
* "Guilds served as an organizational basis for the chambers of com- merce because they were affiliated with them and contributed to them by providing funds, premises, and especially elite merchant leaders. However, the guilds did not directly federate or expand into chambers; indeed, they remained ordinary merchant organizations under elite merchant dominance even after chambers of commerce had appeared as exclusively elite merchant associations. But guilds paved the way for chambers of commerce by providing an institutional arena for elite merchants to rise, act, and interact with officials in local businesses and communities. In return, the elite merchant leadership and its rela- tions with local governments enabled guilds to get the social recogni- tion and official favor they needed to acquire legitimacy. The elite merchants' cooperation with local officials around guild and commu- nity affairs heralded their joint efforts to establish chambers of com- merce later on." 192
* "The chambers of commerce indeed acquired legitimacy from the Qing state as social associations beyond direct bureaucratic control, but their legitimacy did not always ensure that they could operate as elements of a civil society representing a domain of private and collec- tive activity autonomous from the state (Duara, 1995: 148). In fact, the legitimacy of chambers of commerce derived primarily from, and relied continuously on, the efforts of elite merchants to expand their power in collusion and contention with officials. Ironically, such legitimacy resulted partially from intragovernmental competition for control of business and the merchant community." 193-194
* "For a more accurate analysis of the Lower Yangzi chambers o commerce and similar elitist organizations in the late imperial early Republican periods, it is better to adopt the more concrete concepts of "local elite public sphere" and "local elite civil society,"1 Habermas (1989) does in the European case.
