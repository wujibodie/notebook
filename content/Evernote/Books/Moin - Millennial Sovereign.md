Intro

* The foundational rulers of the Mughal and Safavid empires (Akbar and Ismail) both modeled themselves on Sufi saints and adopted a messianic posture, linking the person of the king with millennial expectations common across the Muslim world of the 16th/17th centuries. Moin argues that both drew on earlier precedents from Timurid times.
	* “**The imperial projects of the Mughals and the Safavids in the first half of the sixteenth century had competed for the same set of material resources, patronage and kinship networks, and cultural symbols.** Akbar’s Timurid father and grandfather, Humayun and Babur, had both sought refuge and military assistance from the Safavids at low points in their royal careers and had witnessed the workings of the Safavid court and Sufi organization up close. The Safavids, in turn, had adopted the highly stylized forms and fashions of the latter-era Timurid courts as they evolved from a Sufi order into an imperial dynasty. The two nascent sixteenth-century empires had drawn upon a shared cultural context and learned from the other’s modes and methods.”
	* The relationship between kingship and sainthood did not originate with the Timurids per se, but developed as an expectation across the Iranian plateau and Central Asia following the destruction of the old urban network by the Mongol invasions, and the growing importance of Sufi shrines and travelers in the reconstituted social order thereafter.
* The relationship between saintliness and kingship has often been ignored in scholarship due to the separation of “sacred” and “profane” realms, and focus on the textual aspects of Islamic belief to the neglect of “heterodox” practices.
* The “complex science of the millennium” combined a number of expectations about a coming rectification of the religiopolitical world order, which were knitted together in the body of messianic figures modeled most closely on the popular Sufi saints who were an ordinary presence in religious life.
	* “This correction was expected to take place, moreover, not primarily by doctrinal intervention or revival of religious law—although this was often claimed in messianic apologia—but rather by the sheer physical presence, the thaumaturgical body, of the messianic being.”
* An important aspect of messianism was transmigration of the messianic soul - although decried by many orthodox Muslim writers, the idea that the messiah shared in an eternal, recirculating salvific soul was widespread and linked to ideas about the particular potency and abilities of the Sufi saints.
* Millennialism was linked to the literal millennium of 1000 year cycles following key dates in early Islam (1622 (Hijra 1000) being the most important year), but permutations and “fractions” of this date were also important; vital role of astrology, the other side of history (familiar!).
	* “An astrological-cyclical view of time, then, is critical for understanding the institution of sacred kinship in this milieu. It brings to light a “millennial” sovereignty that was not bound by a single religious tradition but universally extended to all the communal constituencies of early modern Islamic empires, whether Muslims, Christians, Jews, Mongols, Hindus, Buddhists, or others. Moreover, it points to an important continuity with cosmological knowledge from the pre-Islamic traditions of India, Iran, and Greece and the even more ancient ones of Sumeria and Akkadia.”
* Need to look at popular depictions and practices of kingship, not just prescriptive texts: the “social personality of kings kings developed via constant circulation through the realm in an ongoing dialectic with the social ideals and popular myths of their diverse subject populations. Premodern kingship, in other words, had a strong performative element to it that cannot be recovered just from prescriptive texts.” 28-29
* Timur’s title: Sahib Qiran, “Lord of the Conjunction…”

2 - THE LORD OF THE CONJUNCTION

* Shah Jahan called himself “Second Lord of the Conjunction,” and compiled a massive astrological register under that title.
* “Using a table that broke down the numerological value of the Persian letters of the words “Lord of Conjunction” (Sahib Qiran), “Timur,” and “Shah Jahan,” the Mughal astronomer showed how these names were intimately linked in a series of resemblances with one another and with the number 365, the number of days in the annual cycle of the sun, the “King of the Heavenly Spheres” and the planet of kings.“ 37
* When Ibn Khaldun met Timur at the siege of Damascus, he gave a lengthy account of the astrological predictions presaging Timur’s arrival; perhaps only to flatter a ruler who emerged from a milieu steeped in millenarianism, but also reflecting Ibn Khaldun’s own abiding interest in astrology (again the other side of history). “Because of all this I, too, had been watching for the event; so now, on account of my fears, it occurred to me to tell him something of it by which he would be diverted and might become kindly disposed toward me.”
* Khaldun’s “skeptical” judgement of Timur: “This king Timur is one of the greatest and mightiest of kings. Some attribute to him knowledge (al-   ilm ), others attribute to him heresy (i   tiqad alrafd ) because they note his preference for the “members of the House” \[of Ali\]; still others attribute to him the employment of magic and sorcery ( ala intihal al-sihr ), but in all this there is nothing; it is simply that he is highly intelligent and very perspicacious, addicted to debate and argumentation about what he knows and also about what he does not know.”
* Sharaf al-Din Yazdi: chronicler-astrologer who crafted a cosmological connection between Timur and Alexander, and plausibly carved the inscription on Timur’s tomb proclaiming him to be descended of the mythical founding goddess of the Mongols. Both wrote the most authoritative Timurid chronicle and served as an astronomer in Ulugh Beg’s observatory in Samarkand.
* During Timur’s lifetime, he emphasized his kinship with Chinggis Khan, but after his death those links were deemphasized and he was increasingly viewed as being a devotee and even relative of Ali. “To summarize, before Timur could become a Lord of Conjunction in his own right, his charisma had depended on how he ritually and symbolically engaged with the memory of Chinggis Khan and Ali. On the plane of Islamic history, as we understand it, it is difficult to see the equivalence between these two men. Indeed, they could not be farther apart. Chinggis was a cruel “pagan” conqueror who uprooted Islam and imposed his own law in its place. Ali, on the other hand, was a foundational figure of Islam—first cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet, the fourth caliph of Islam, revered by his partisans (shi   a ) as the first leader (imam ) of the Muslims after the Prophet. Indeed, Timur’s juggling of these two symbols behind the modest facade of being an amir upholding Sunni Islam while slaughtering and plundering on an unimaginable scale is what makes him so difficult to characterize today. However, the differences between Ali and Chinggis Khan fade away when we realize that both figures were Lords of Conjunction of the highest order, men destined to inaugurate new epochs and dispensations.”
* Timur’s successors in the 1420s had a messianic rival in the form of one Nurbakhsh, who claimed to be a “reincarnation” of Ali, not via metempsychosis, but via the “filling” of his perfected body with Ali’s soul from above, in a picture similar to the claims of Yazdi about Timur’s provenance from an Alid born of Alanquva after her impregnation by a beam of light. “In his case, the phenomenon of buruz —the descent of the messianic soul into Nurbakhsh’s body—was witnessed by one of his followers, who saw “in Irbil in the year 827 \[1423–1424\], that one day people gathered together to wait for Jesus to descend from the sky. He saw that he descended in the form of light rather than body, and flowed toward me \[i.e., Nurbakhsh\] and held me. The same night I saw that I was present in the sky and in a human body on earth in the same instant.””
	* Similarities between this “descent” of the messianic soul into a perfected body and the reveries of Sabbatai Sevi?
* Conclusions: “Timurid notions of sovereignty were shaped by the messianic myth of the Lord of Conjunction. This was a time of transition. The existing Mongol order was receding. Its symbol was Chinggis Khan. Another Islamic order was arising. Its symbol was Ali. The sacred myth that could explain this grand change in world affairs was the rise of a messianic figure who would inaugurate the new era. Timur inhabited this myth and performed it with relish. What everyone knew but could not say was that he was the Lord of Conjunction. Ibn Khaldun, an outsider, let it slip in court and recorded for posterity Timur’s public denial that he was the Lord of Conjunction. Other sources tell us that a group of Timur’s own soldiers had worshipped him, as a saint, messiah, or divinity, much as the followers of Nurbakhsh, Musha sha , and Fazlallah Astarabadi had venerated these men. The milieu of the military camp, with its oral epic traditions and heroic ideals, encouraged enthusiastic and concrete modes of sacrality over textual religious doctrines. In such a setting, the fact of charismatic sovereignty was mostly what mattered, an embodied sovereignty that could be transmitted through blood or milk. Timur’s successors openly proclaimed this fact upon Timur’s death. The machinery of imperial tradition making began its work, and sages of the realm used esoteric lore to express what had popularly been known—Timur was the Lord of Conjunction, the descendant of Ali, the awaited messiah.”

3 - Crown of Dreams

* “Indeed, one can argue that in the fifteenth century, Timurid courts and princely retinues, concentrated in eastern Iran and present-day Afghanistan, were the main centers of the long-acting “civilizing process”—the cultivated manners, habits, and tastes—that shaped elite Persianate “social personality” across large swaths of Asia.” Both Ismail and Babur came out of this milieu.
* Babur repeatedly narrated dreams involving the intercession of Naqshbandi saints (dead and living), who promised aid in his journey towards sovereignty; this in part reflected the real power of that order around Samarkand. By contrast, Shah Ismail moved from priesthood to sovereignty by himself, and in opposition to other Sufi orders such as the Naqshbandis. “To understand the nature of Babur’s relationship with Shah Ismail, it is important to develop an appreciation for the popular image and political stature of the latter. For, in the first decade of the sixteenth century, when Babur was scraping together a living by raiding Afghan villages and keeping his sovereign ambitions alive by writing down his dreams in a diary, Shah Isma il was enacting the myth of being a Lord of Conjunction, and news of his invincibility and miraculous victories was echoing across Asia and Europe.”
* The Safavid order began building a hierarchical network of priest-missionaries in the Southern Caucasus, Anatolia, and the Caspian region starting two generations before Shah Ismail, who thus was “born a messiah.” By the time Ismail inherited his position at age 12, he thus already had a large base of enormously devoted followers to whom he uniquely served as a symbol of the coming rectification of the world. The Bektashi Sufi order which ministered to the Ottoman janissaries was highly susceptible to this message.
	* The Safavid order emerged among a backdrop of a growing movement of “deviant renunciation” in the form of itinerant dervishes who distinguished themselves from “princely” Sufis such as the Naqshbandis. “It was not necessarily class, birth, or even education that divided the conformist (ba shar   ) from the deviant (bi shar   ) mystics. Rather, it was how they lived, what they consumed, and the way they adorned their bodies, deliberately breaking as many of society’s taboos as possible.” These dervishes were fervent worshippers of Ali, and kept alive a messianic vision of Ali’s return, and thus set much of the imagery into which Shah Ismail stepped as a reincarnation of Ali.
* The antinomian or deliberately transgressive practices of the bi’shar dervishes were a part of Ismail’s inheritance, and he made use of **purposeful transgression** at crucial moments in establishing his kingship: “Another more grisly way of demonstrating loyalty with the shah consisted of the frenzied devouring of the body of his enemy. Reportedly, one victim of this ritual act was the Uzbek ruler Shaybani Khan, whose muddied and bloodied corpse was eaten by a stampeding crowd of Qizilbash soldiers when Shah Isma il said “whoever among our sincere soldiers (qurchiyan-i kathir al-ikhlas ) and special servants (mulaziman-i kathir al- ikhtisas ) loves our imperial head (sar-i navab-i humayun-i ma ) should partake of the flesh of this enemy.” 114 It has been observed that this transgressive act of cannibalism was a demonstration of the Qizilbash disciples’ loyalty to the shah in a deeply affirmative sense—by the consuming together of tabooed flesh—as well as in a negative sense, by the corporeal destruction of all other possibilities of sovereignty.”
* Babur retook Samarkand for the 3rd time, in 1511, only as a vassal of the Safavids, and had to publicly show his belief in the “heresy” of Shah Ismail’s messianism, while standing by as the Qizilbash committed abuses against the local population; this ended in his losing the support of the local people, and the memories of Babur’s humiliating subjection to the Safavids remained a stain on Timurid prestige which Babur’s heirs had to expend significant effort to overcome.
* Ismail claimed reincarnation from pre-Islamic figures as well as Ali: “I am Faridun, **Khusraw**, Jamshid, and Zahhak; I am Zal’s son (i.e., Rustam) and Alexander.”
