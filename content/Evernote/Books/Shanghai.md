Yeh

* Two major lines of inquiry on modern shanghai: one, more sociological, focused on guilds/native-place associations and state/society relations, and another more economically focused studying the businesses of early Republican Shanghai, along with banking and commercial networks in "a new kind of urban economy as a concrete expression of relationships between China and the West."
	* Meanwhile, studies in literary and art history have increasingly come to focus on the institutional/material foundations of early 20th c Shanghai culture, leading to a convergence towards a focus on the "phenomenon of modernity" in Shanghai.
* Overall, these lines of study combine to draw a picture of Shanghai as the seat of an emerging form of Chinese modernity, and so as the key point for the evolution of a new form of Chinese identity.
* Yeh's project: to point out the different conceptual frameworks that animate the study of state/society on one hand, and culture/commerce on the other.
* Bergere: Shanghai bourgeois moment was politically sterile, bc the Chinese middle classes lacked a strong state to latch on to, and failed to articulate a successful vision leading to the creation of such a state
* "**These newly shaped phenomena reflected the working not so much of national factors such as politics and institutions as of global forces of commerce and finance, which resulted not only in the commodification of culture but also in the rise of new social interests and occupational classes that radically redefined what it meant to be "Chinese."** It was inevitable, then, for the "national" to acquire a new set of meanings within the context of a dialectical relationship with the "global" - meanings that exceeded a simple stark contradiction between identities and values. " 385
	* Skeptical that the Shanghai bourgeoisie actually possessed the potential to enact political change; sees them instead of convincing themselves that they had been (and wanted to be) modern, rather than actually walking through the steps taken by the Western bourgeoisie in their takeover of their own societies.
* Ends up with a long study of advertising, arguing that the notion of Shanghai's modernity was as much a product of the images which circulated depicting this modernity as of any concrete aspect of life in the city.
	

1. Please write out a brief account of the ways that Lee and Yue create their senses of what constitutes “cosmopolitan” in the Shanghai. In what ways can they be juxtaposed as alternatives, complements or otherwise be evaluated?

Both Lee and Yue agree on the need to separate the idea of Shanghai's "cosmopolitan" "modernity" from previous sociological or economic reductionist accounts, and to bring the focus back to the cultural response of Chinese actors to the new images, ideas, and challenges brought by Shanghai's encounter with the West and Japan. Where they differ is principally in where they look for an explanation for Shanghai's particular "cosmopolitan" character: for Lee, early 20th century Shanghai saw the introduction of new kinds of "public structures and spaces for urban cultural production and consumption," which were obviously modeled on those of the West even if they were reappropriated by Chinese consumers and thinkers, while for Yue Shanghai is principally remarkable for its inheritance of a tradition of boisterous "festive urbanism" from the broader region of the Jiangnan canal cities. In keeping with these different emphases, the two authors also give quite different pictures of the Chinese intellectuals engaged in translating Western modernity to their Shanghai context.
In Yue’s case, the focus is more on literary/dramatic productions and translation. Her focus on "countersemiology" and the reinterpretation of foreign political events in light of a growing discourse of imperialism/revolution both serve to show how the information and concepts coming from abroad, rather than replacing older forms of knowledge, were creatively incorporated into older frameworks by an artists and intellectuals still fluent in late imperial forms. Meanwhile, her insistence on Shanghai culture's continuity with Yangzhou, Suzhou, and other nearby cities, as well as her study of the fanhua/yin dichotomy are means of de-emphasizing the "newness" of turn-of-the-century Shanghai's culture, by showing how the commercial flourishing which is often taken as a moment of linear progress was also conceived as the high point of a cyclical process extending many centuries into the past. The overall thrust of her work is to focus attention on the people who produced the (Chinese-language) discourse on Shanghai, as opposed to the sociological or economic phenomena which Yeh rightly points out are the more common explanations cited for Shanghai's particular prominence in the 20th century.
By contrast, Lee does spend time looking at the concrete changes which the Western encounter brought to Shanghai's cityscape, but not without losing sight of the urban populace and the way they made use of new elements. Looking at both the "substance" (skyscrapers, public parks, dance halls, coffee houses, etc) and "appearance" (the "forms of cultural activity and expression" made possible in these places) allows Lee to problematize the word "cosmopolitan" and work out the ways in which it can be productively applied to Shanghai in particular. Throughout Lee's essay, the constant contrast between "Western" and "Chinese" is seen both as an unavoidable reminder of Shanghai's place in a colonial hierarchy, and as an opportunity for productive reappropriations as a model for the imagination. In the end, Lee rejects the obvious comparison cases: Shanghai is neither fully like Paris, the imperial center, nor like the metropolises of India and Africa, where political dominance extended further and included wholescale exclusion of indigenous languages. Ironically, while focuses on the urban spaces which he claims made Shanghai's "cosmopolitan" image possible, Lee ends up affirming the importance of production _in Chinese_ of writing about these places; in other words, he reaches a point where exactly the kinds of translation Yue writes about are the most crucial elements of a specifically Shanghainese modernity.
