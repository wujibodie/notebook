Introduction

* Focuses on the writings of one 嚴書開, a lesser known author of the Ming-Qing transition who studied the question of when silver became the chief circulating currency and measure of value. His answer: it was largely considered a non-monetary treasure up to the Song, although there were brief experiments in monetizing it (e.g. Wang Mang); after being being heavily mined in the Song as an important source of _external_ account-settling (merchants and foreign tribute), it became a key circulating currency first in the Liao and Jin territories, and even in the Southern Song it was only a supplementary means of payment, rather than the basic measure of value and foundation of the tax system. For Yan, the increasing use of silver in taxes during the Yuan Dynasty reflected a lack of circulating coin, and the simultaneous reliance on paper money during this time underlines the inability of silver to take over as chief currency. The early Ming rulers did not use silver at all; but by the Zhengtong reign the state began to accept the conversion of in-kind taxes to silver, because it profited both the state and taxpayers by reducing transportation costs to the new capital in Beijing. (Silverization as long-term consequence of the Yongle usurpation!) The _xunfu_ [周忱] is singled out as especially important in this process; while in charge of “Wu,” he set special tax grades for 南浙蘇松五郡 and 4 categories of land (rice tax fully converted to silver; 50% converted; 30% converted; 10% converted). Direct line from this early policy to the extension of a single rate of silver taxation to all land in the empire in the _yitiaobian fa._ Following the implementation of the single whip, silver taxes steadily rose, increasing by three or fourfold from 1588 to 1644.
	* Yan ends by affirming that taxes should be based on things produced by nature and/or human effort, rather than goods of uncertain provenance like silver; Qiu characterizes this as a 重本輕銀的邏輯 typical of 明末 thinking.

Chapter 1

* 4 research topics for history of silver:
	* How did the various prerequisites for silverization, particularly the move away from labor levies, the collapse of paper money, the persistently low supply of copper coins, and the increasing measure of values in terms of silver, come about over the long term?
	* What were the reasons for the various ruptures in Ming monetary/fiscal policy? Particularly, what explains the transformation of the Ming _baochao_ into a form of money and consequent collapse, and why did the end of the “three centuries of paper money” not cause wider social turmoil? Fundamentally, what are the deeper causes for the failures of the early Ming currency system?
	* How to relate and rank the importance of the various scales at which silverization appeared? Was the government’s adoption of silver initiated primarily by officials looking for private profit, a natural product of internal developments within the institutions themselves, or was it a response to the requirements of commercial development? What was the detailed process by which influence moved between the realms of fiscal policy, purely monetary institutions, and private exchange? These questions call for a very precise analysis of how and why exactly the _fuyi_ levies came to rely more and more on silver as time went on.
	* How to explain the precise geography of silver flows? How did it come about that, by the middle of the Ming, there were regions where cash circulated and regions where it did not? What did _local_ silverization(s) look like in detail, and how did they differ? What were the respective geographies of silver and coin flows, and how might they be interrelated? Is it correct to generalize that the south and east tended to have surfeits of silver due to foreign trade, while the north and west tended to see shortages?
		* “把握货币的实际流通状况，一直是货币史研究领域的重难点问题.” 6
	* Regarding the debate between whether silverization was driven by the state or by "society" (top down or bottom up), Qiu sees an autocratic "洪武型經濟體制" which eventually collapses simultaneously with a general expansion of commerce; silverization of finance is driven by its adoption in the marketplace, but this line of causality is only possible due to the failure of the previous, centralized monetary system. 25
		

Chapter 3 - The Establishment of the “Hongwu Currency System” and the Diversity of Commodity Exchange in the Early Ming

* At first, Ming Hongwu implemented Baochao only in relatively large denominations, while minting copper coins intended for daily use. However, as minting was never enough to cover the demand for coinage, counterfeit coins were widespread, and in general coins were preferred to paper notes. In Hongwu 8, the Daming Baochao was introduced, with its values expressed in terms of copper (smallest note = 100 _wen_). Simultaneously, in order to support the new baochao, the use of gold, silver, and other valuable goods in private exchange was banned.
* It was only in Hongwu 27 that Hongwu banned copper coins and closed all imperial mints. The explicit reasoning behind this policy was to combat the preference for coins over baochao in the most commercialized regions (可見，其是針對兩浙，福建，兩廣，江西等處民眾重錢輕鈔，鈔價貶值的市場行為而發。）89
* Eventually baochao were introduced at quantities as low as 10 _wen_, but no lower, leaving a substantial gap in the system for minimally sized legal currency.
* Qiu sees two basic reasons for Hongwu’s adoption of such a seemingly obviously flawed currency system: 1, his belief in the idea common among the gentry that money was a fundamentally worthless substance that always depended on 主權 for its “creditworthiness.” (Cites 劉基 as one proponent of this understanding). 2,  a desire to strike at the wealthy merchants and powerful families of Jiangnan, Fujian, etc, while raising up the commodity economy.
	* What is Zhang Juzheng’s conception re: the former of these points?
* Proclamations against private circulation of coins were repeatedly issued up through the Xuande reign, as a result of frequent reports that coins were in common use in large regions.
* In an edict of Hongwu 5 and elsewhere, Ming Taizu drew a direct connection between fiscal policy (pro-_chao_ and against gold/silver) and the reconstruction of customs and morality after Mongol rule (particularly singling out the rich of Liangguang and Fujian, as well as Buddhists): “中國衣冠壞於胡俗，已嘗考定品官，命婦冠服及士庶人衣巾，婦女服飾，行之中外… 福建，兩廣等處豪強之家，多以他人子閹割役使，名曰“火者”，今後有犯者，以閹罪抵之，沒官為奴.” 108, quoting 明太祖實錄
	* Claims that early Ming officials and _shidafu_ almost all praised Hongwu’s economic thinking, in part because it was compatible with traditional Ru thought, and in part because of the power of the first emperor’s example. Officials who thought otherwise (Qiu mentions one 黃福, of the Nanjing 戶部尚書, who memorialized arguing that the baochao system could be saved (after a drop in relative value to silver of 3-5 guan per liang to over 1000 guan per liang) by buying up old baochao with silver at face value, and then releasing new notes in exchange for silver at market prices, thus stabilizing the price ratio) had a difficult time convincing others to go against this precedent. (Richard mentions the exact same official and quote in Fountain of Fortune!)
	* When taxation in silver began to become widespread, it had to be justified using the few examples where Hongwu himself had allowed (temporary, small-scale!) remission of taxes in silver. 109-110
	* Thinks (borrowing an argument from Ge Zhaoguang) that the immense cultural prestige of Hongwu and his immediate successors, who were seen as having stabilized a chaotic situation and renewed correct customs, were the major factor explaining why so many attempts were made to save a failing _baochao_ system.
* During the Hongwu and Yongle reigns, the Ming state attempted to cover its massive expenses entirely by spending _baochao_ on a very small market, without successfully recovering them or making them the base of taxation. Qiu sees this as the crux of the declining purchasing power of the baochao. Beginning late in Yongle’s reign, and increasing for the next few decades, the state began to attempt a correction by demanding certain payments in _baochao,_ most importantly the _鹽課._ By this time, however, there was little market interest in the baochao, and private transactions almost entirely used silver (or coins, silk, etc). 116
* Argues, with Kuroda, that the real situation of the Ming economy was not defined by official policy, but by the conflict between this policy and the very diverse circulation of different currencies in the real markets. In the early Ming, the “shriveling” of markets and restriction of commerce due to the dislocation of the Yuan collapse and the harsh policies of Ming Taizu caused the currency system to become very unstable. 121
	* By contrast to the Southern Song, when private circulation of state-issued paper money became commonplace in conditions of robust commercial growth and ever-increasing demand for currency, the early Ming saw massive emission of paper notes into shrunken and uncertain markets where stability, security, and durability, rather than convenience of circulation, were at a premium.
* Copper coins: in general, excavations of hoards found in China are largely Tang-Song, with a large component of Hongwu era coins. Many of these hoards were probably buried during the bans on copper money of the late Hongwu reign. By contrast, hoards found in Southeast Asia and Japan are dominated by Yongle coins, which are barely ever found in China proper. Clear suggestion that the Yongle reign saw a massive outflow of copper coins in exchange for tributary gifts, and this purpose was probably the main one for which Yongle mints were run at all. 120-123.
* The value of _baochao_ was fairly stable only for the first few years of their circulation, and by Hongwu 19 (about 10 years after they were introduced) their market value was about 1/5th of face value. By the Yongle and Xuande reigns they were worth around 1% of their face value, with a brief spike in late Yongle.
* In the Hongwu and Yongle reigns, Huizhou land contracts were predominantly valued in terms of baochao, but a fairly large percent were already using rice, silver, or silk to measure value. In the Xuande reign only a quarter of these contracts used baochao, and the majority used silk. “Commodity” money was never absent in these large-scale, legally notarized transactions, and it naturally came to dominate when trust in baochao dropped.
* During this period currencies differed greatly by region. In general Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Fujian, and Liangguang preferred coin, especially Tang-Song coins, although more isolated areas often used commodities for exchange. In Sichuan and Yunnan, copper coins were rare, and gold, cowries, silver, salt, tea, and cattle all found use. Pan Jixun mentioned that (in the Jiajing era) peasants at Jiangxi markets usually used grain to trade, and coins were never found (131).
	* During this time, grain (specifically 石) remained the habitual measure of value in most government sources.

Chapter 4 - Monetary Silverization 1 - Fiscal system reforms and the process of state revenues silverization

* Begins with a study of fiscal silverization, as a result of which silver went from illegal to legally sanctioned status, and which had a mutually encouraging relationship with market silverization.
* Basic idea: emergence of a “dual track 雙軌” silver system, with remittance of taxes into silver on the top, and increasing (illegal) use of silver in commerce (and gradual dominance of silver as measure of value) on the bottom.
* Early anti-silver measures specifically targeted the wealthy of Zhejiang, who were said to measure all prices in terms of silver (but not necessarily to physically exchange silver!). Later on the preservation of the baochao system became another strong motivating factor for anti-silver policy. 134-135
	* “當時，金銀主要是通行於江南富商，勢豪之中，禁金銀也是明太祖彈控浙西商人，豪強大戶，收歸貨幣權柄的重要舉措.” 137
* In **Xuande 5, the court learned that in Suzhou, the local garrison officers had collected taxes in silver and silk in order to pay for anti-wokou warships.** Other examples of officials requesting permission to commute or directly collecting silver taxes proliferated in the Xuande reign. 137 Around the same time, it became clear that stagnant commerce had led to concentration of silver with only a few wealthy households.
	* Qiu believes that by the end of the Xuande reign, the court was more or less resigned to private use of silver, although it did not yet officially sanction its use. 138 However, private circulation of silver was only part of a diverse set of “commodity monies,” not yet the dominant currency as such: “在明初貨幣經濟極度萎縮環境下，當時社會實際應是一個實物本位居主流的多元通貨並存時期.” 139
* Commutation of land tax into silver (as well as silk, copper coins, etc) was permitted from Hongwu 9 in exceptional circumstances. In Hongwu 17, 蘇松嘉潮 were allowed to pay their 田租 in gold. Certain border areas, e.g. in Yunnan and Sichuan, where special taxes were levied in horses, skins, or wool, had these taxes converted to gold and silver payments. Most Annamese taxes were also converted to gold and silver due to distance and poverty.
* Yongle greatly expanded the scale of silver mining, which went from producing about 30,000 liang annually in Hongwu’s time to an average of 220,000 liang during Yongle’s 22 year reign. 142
* Even while silver was banned, it was always accepted in payment of 課程 taxes on wine, commerce, etc. In Xuande’s reign, criminal fines were accepted in silver as well.
* Hongwu set a precedent of using gold and silver to pay soldiers and princes (蕃王), which continued afterwards. 144-145
* In general, during the early Ming the government consistently used precious metals to pay for a portion of its expenses, and also took certain forms of tax payments in gold or silver (among other valuable commodities). During this time, then, there was substantial circulation of silver within the government as well as in private markets, but there was little room for contact between these circuits, and what contact there was was mainly government siphoning of silver via commercial taxes or land tax commutations, etc.
	* Consequence: “貨幣白銀化將在兩條軌道上演進：一是政府制度層面的潛行，突破和展開；二是在民間交易市場中突破使用領域，使用人群，地域範圍等限度，在更廣層面流通.” 146
* Section 2: Beginning of silver commutations in the Zheng-Jing reigns and the Expansion of Public and Private Use of Silver
	* Zali payments: In Hongwu’s time, while higher officials in the capital were paid in rice,  provincial officials (and yamen clerks, etc) were paid in _baochao_, leading to a rapid decline in their real incomes. In Yongle’s time even capital officials were paid partially in _baochao_. This situation induced many officials to siphon off those taxes which were collected in money, and thus incentivized them to seek silver commutation (!) (149) One particularly prominent case was that of the _zali_, initially the servants assigned to officials or yamens via _lichai_, but the officials increasingly accepted silver payments in lieu of service in a “private agreement” which came to be very widespread.
	* “宣德皇帝在獲悉此事詳委之後，為避免底下貪官污吏隨意敲詐勤索，於是決定將其制度化。“ 150 In the Xuande and Zhengtong reigns, every grade of official was assigned a fixed number of zali, which really meant that they were entitled to a certain fixed annual silver payment called 柴薪銀. This was done in order to avoid officials inflating the number of men they called up in lichai and then pressing them for large quantities to commute their labor service. Points out that this system meant that Ming official salaries after Zhengtong were in practice quite high, in contrast to what most modern historians believe.
		* Because this was the first major case of labor services being systematically commuted to silver, it means that the officials’ pursuit of their own interests, not any impulse from the commercial stratum, was the motor behind the earliest examples of silver commutation. “其出現顯然來源於官僚階層對自身利益的強烈訴求，以及對銀禁法令和寶鈔體制的突破，而非商品經濟發展的結果.” 151
		* Bc the total value of the 柴薪銀 was multiple times greater than that of the 金花銀, Qiu sees it as the early Ming’s largest single step into silverization.
	* Southern tax commutation: After Yongle’s move to Beijing, the tax burden on southern regions grew dramatically due to transport expenses. Commutation of land taxes into baochao and silk became familiar methods of lessening this problem, and although gold/silver commutation was less common, it did occasionally occur. Late in the Xuande reign, when attempts to revive the baochao were mostly admitted to have failed, officials began to consider officially establishing use of silver for this purpose. [周忱] commuted portions of the Su-Song land tax into silver and cloth in Xuande 8, while Huizhou was allowed to pay its entire grain tax in silver in Xuande 10 (paying 32000 taels). In the Zhengtong reign, this practice became more widespread, and the silver went from covering deficits in official salaries to being stored in the central treasuries.
		* Qiu believes that the Zhengtong move towards silverized land taxation: 1, occurred in order to cover the shortfall in official salaries caused by the shift from baochao to silver; 2. Was supported by powerful officials like 胡濙 and 黃福 selectively citing Taizu’s precedent, and was not formalized in state accounts until the end of the Chenghua reign (i.e., remained theoretically an exceptional measure); 3. Although the court repeatedly claimed silver commutation was a temporary measure for inaccessible districts, the above-market rate of exchange for rice:silver commutation meant that taxpayers rarely wished to go back, and it tended to become stabilized in Nan Zhili, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Huguang, Liangguang, Fujian, with the central state becoming accustomed to accounting with a fairly stable yearly silver income; 4, this process of commutation was not the same as the 金花銀, which as a formal and fixed variety of land tax in silver for one specific region only came about later.
	* Expansion in public and private use of silver: by the 1440s, the baochao was irrevocably undervalued, copper coins were illegal, and at the same time the state’s revenue in silver was growing year over year. This situation made it natural that the state would lean heavily on silver to cover the expenses of wars in the north after the Tumu crisis. In particular, silver came to be one of the main means of covering military expenditures on the northern border (158-160). This further pushed the court towards accepting more taxes in silver. Silver also came to be used to assist commoners in areas suffering from natural disaster or famine.
	* During the Jingtai reign in the 1450s, most commercial taxes 商課 were converted to silver from baochao.
	* Between Zhengtong and Chenghua reigns, land contracts went from being about 70% expressed in silver, with remainder written mainly in terms of rice or silk, to 100% valued in terms of silver. 163 (Cites Wan Ming)
	* Ends by quoting Fountain of Fortune saying that the period between the 1430s to 1450 were the start of the true silver economy in the Ming
* In general, the prefectures where silver commutation was first practiced were the exact same ones which were singled out to carry the heaviest tax burden by Zhu Yuanzhang (clustered around Su-Song).

Chapter 5 - 貨幣制度領域於民間市場上的白銀替代

* During the Zhengtong reign, the government seized on signs of a slight increase in baochao values (from near-zero at the start of the reign) to reaffirm the ban on private use of copper coins; the successful deflation of the Xuande era encouraged the state to maintain this ban, but by the 1450s officials more or less recognized that both the circulation of baochao and the restriction of copper/silver had failed in the marketplace, and one sign of this recognition was increasing government willingness to use silver and silk as a part of famine relief funds in the Tianshun reign.
	* The Tianshun reign (1457-1464) is also when a number of other government expenses tended to be converted from baochao to silver (192).
* By the Chenghua reign baochao largely disappeared from the marketplace (cit. Qiu Jun).
* In Chenghua 1 (1465), the court resolved to accept certain payments in half coin and half baochao, the first step towards legally recognizing the circulation of copper currency. However, the exchange rate for baochao to coin was unrealistically high, and so Qiu sees this as a way for the state to increase tax revenues above all, and only secondarily to encourage use of coin. Later, one Tu Yong 屠鏞 from Zhejiang proposed using silver to buy up stocks of copper coin and then use them for government expenses, thus expanding the circulation of coin and encouraging price stability; although this policy was not enacted, it showed that the Chenghua court did take the problem of copper coin circulation seriously, that private circulation of copper coin in Zhejiang was not negligible, and that silver was already serving as a relatively stable medium of exchange by which prices of other commodities/currencies could be measured. In the end, however, Chenghua policies as enacted did not put control of coin supply or prices in the government’s hands, and only encouraged private counterfeit minting. 195-197
