Feygin and Leusder, in “The Class Politics of the Dollar System,” write: "To go beyond managing the status quo, we need to first recognize that the dollar system evolved not as a tool of imperial statecraft, but as the project of a transnational elite that has effectively usurped control of an international public good. “
To some extent this is correct; but if the dollar system was not purposefully crafted as a “tool” of empire, the world’s most powerful country (and its apparatus of “statecraft" at least did not inhibit that system’s existence. Why, when the results were so clearly damaging to large sectors of the American populace? What was the logic of those who architected the “dollar system”’s diplomatic side?  

Peterson, America in a Changing World Economy (1971)

* Begins with the perspective that America’s structural dominance is at an end, and that Nixon’s NEP is a late response to this concrete fact.
* Specifies these structural changes as largely involving the growing importance of the EC and its “preferential trading arrangements,” vague “problems of the world’s system of trade in agriculture, to the point where market forces are seriously compromised,” and imbalances in the international trade of Japan. 
* 2 basic suggestions: wide-ranging revisions of trading rights to construct a “new, open and fair world trading system,” and a new international monetary system with flexible exchange rates. 
* “This new system must be characterized by shared leadership shared responsibility and shared burdens, to which at least all the free world’s strongest nations commit themselves. It will be a system which fully recognizes, and is solidly rooted in, the growing reality of a genuinely interdependent and increasingly competitive world economy whose goal is mutual, shared prosperity — not artificial, temporary advantage."
* Basis of US weakness is slow growth in manufacturing exports — in 1960s, US exports grew 110%, compared to 200% in W. Germany and 400% in Japan. P blames this on discriminatory trading agreements abroad, weak federal investment in export growth, and rising labor costs.
