* Starts by emphasizing the internal dynamics of British capitalism; explanation for how the East India Company came to dominate India is to be found in the relationship between the company's agents and their homeland; London and Madras together.
	* On "Indocentric" explanations: "Even though these hypotheses contribute to an understanding of the British private sector in Asia, they fail to see private merchants as an independent dynamic commercial structure and do not link the development in India with the rest of Great Britain’s overseas activities." Trend towards explanation of early modern India in terms of Indian economic and political actors improves on eurocentric idea of a "static" India taken over by "modern" Europeans, but mischaracterizes the British agents of the EIC by deemphasizing their interactions with England itself.
* Believes 17th/18th C Britain was characterized by a "gentlemanly capitalism" in which the aristocracy and bourgeoise fused to produce a shared set of values for all British elites. Emphasizes the extensive private trade carried on in Madras by British merchants whose personal connections in the City supplied them with funds and agents. These ties also maintained the cultural continuity of the British "diaspora" community in Madras, which in turn made it easier for people to circulate back and forth between Madras and London.

Chapter One

* After the foundation of Madras, the EIC's annual investments in India totaled ~500,000-700,000 pounds per year, mostly in gold and silver coins. 18
	* "An appointment in India was much coveted" because young men "regarded Asia and the Indian subcontinent in particular as the land of opportunities where an enterprising merchant could build up a fortune within a few years and return home to a pleasant life in England."
* Most observers, from the 1610s to now, have seen private trade as detrimental to the EIC's growth; but Mentz believes that it was the most dynamic sector of English trade and helped expand the overall capacities of the English network
* One stream of historiography: the centralized Mughal state had such high revenues from taxing agriculture that it did not need to bargain with merchants by giving them social rights in exchange for a share of their profits as the European states did; Indian trade thus never developed the same role in the political order as in England. To some, this meant that India failed to develop "trade capitalism" and was thus doomed to become peripheral in the European world system; to others (Chaudhuri) "trading capitalism" did objectively exist, but as profits could not be reinvested into other areas (such as land), there were no guarantees of continued prosperity for individual merchants and no room for capitalists to affect social life more broadly.
* Revisionist stream:
	* Burton Stein: The Indian state was "segmentary," with power held at provincial level, rather than "centralized."
	* Bayly: Mughals as dominant "third party" who mediated between local _zamindars_ and kept conflict/accumulation of power to a minimum. The "export boom" of the 17th C. led to an increase in power of the _zamindars_, whose income did rely on control of trade, and the strongest created breakaway states (Hyderabad, Bengal), which were then vulnerable to takeover by the British
	* Subrahmanyam: By the early 1600s, Golconda was home to multiple Iranian "portfolio capitalists" who dominated the commercial and political life of the towns through their sponsorship of overseas trade. Muhammad Sayyid: Iranian merchant in Masulipatnam who gained a grant of the Kolluru diamond mine, and in 1623 was made governor. Eventually amassed a guard of 5,000 men and rivaled the Sultan; forced to flee Golconda and work for Aurangzeb. His exit left a "vacuum that was slowly filled by English private traders," who tapped into the same capital markets he had worked in. But why the English and not other local merchants?
	* Arasaratnam: Early 17th C South India did see strong political merchants who negotiated privileges w/ the rulers of "segmentary" states dependent on trade, including control of land + direct management of production. This equilibrium was broken up by the constant wars following Aurangzeb's conquest of the Deccan sultanates, after which the most stable partners for these merchants were the European companies.
	* Summary: "Both Subrahmanyam and Arasaratnam regard the commercial growth of the English private sector as the consequence of a general weakening of the local Indian trading network. This viewpoint is central to Indocentric historical research. The private merchants did not participate actively in changing conditions to their own advantage. They are described as being either totally dependent upon collaboration with Indian merchants or as appendices to the Company. The years from 1600 to 1750 are described either as an age in which collaboration and partnership left their mark on relationships between Indians and Englishmen or as a period of armed conflict in which the trading companies consolidated their position by means of brute force at the expense of local merchants. The use of violence helped the private traders within the Asiatic trading world, eliminating competition from indigenous merchants."
* 1600-1750: Age of "cooperation" or "competition" between European and Indian merchants?
* Sees the eventual conquest of India (starting w. Battle of Plassey) as growing out of the search for individual profit typical of the English private trade; growth in "plundering" by merchants was a way to bring fortunes back home, and the Company attempted to stop it.
	* Mention of Danish East India Company as neutral vessel which brought back illicit/private gains on behalf of English private traders

Chapter 2: Madras and the City of London - a Diaspora?

* Sees the nature of interaction between metropole and diasporic communities as one in which developments in the metropole influence the beliefs and capacities of actors in the colonies. For this reason, Mentz expects that events in Madras parallel those in other colonies of the British Empire - particularly the Caribbean and mainland North American colonies.
	* Uses Curtin's concept of "cross-cultural brokers" to define merchant diasporas, as well as Abner Cohen's notion of ethnic solidarity producing trust which is vital for long-distance trade.
	* "Diaspora merchants tried to monopolize trade in certain goods, in order to control various trade routes and the performance of services in competition with rival networks. In order to maintain a competitive edge, members of a commercial network were forced to organize their activities. It was of particular importance to co-ordinate internal mechanisms of credit, develop a commercial system, and establish lines of communication operating over long distances. By doing this, merchants would have reliable commercial partners at their disposal, whom they did not know personally, but whom they still trusted. Such partners were dispersed over a wide geographical area thus promoting the activities of the network." 42
* English context: the power of overseas merchants depended on metropolitan developments bw 1660 and 1688, namely the improvement of credit and capital mobility in the City, English successes in wars with the Dutch, state sponsorship of merchants, and the steady accumulation of capital by the wealthiest merchants and firms.
	* Most merchants were involved in moneylending, and bills of exchange between merchant houses (w/ long-lasting lines of credit up to 2 years) were the primary means of exchange financing overseas voyages. Therefore, bankruptcies could ripple across the chain of merchants --> trust and reputation highly important.
* The Glorious Revolution and subsequent wars with France made the monarchy dependent on financial capital for funding -> higher position for merchants (strengthened House of Commons), foundation of the Bank of England, huge increase in national debt and credit supply
* Social side of the growth of credit/trust: coming-together of the landed aristocracy and the commercial class produces an ideal of the "gentleman," which is based in part on having the wealth to enjoy a cultivated leisure, and partially on moral metrics including honesty and trustworthiness. This ideal was shared among the elite of London and the merchants of Madras, making it easier for them to do business w/ one another.

Chapter 3: Transfer of Capital from England to Private Merchants

* Historiography has tended to focus on the official activities of the EIC, seen as a forerunner of modern corporations; but how did the scale of sanctioned trade compare to that of private trade by Englishmen in India?
	* Key questions: "Did the connection with the City of London play an important role in establishing an English commercial sector in Madras, what were the economic dimensions of the transactions conducted by Company servants in co-operation with commissioners in the City of London and did this branch of private trade play a part in creating a dynamic British country trade sector in India before the age of conquest?"
* Means of private trade: the EIC chartered ships rather than owning them, and thus did not have complete control over their cargoes; bc Madras traders came from the London milieu, it was easy for them to establish informal trading relationships and find both middlemen and creditors in England. Usually remittances were in the form of diamonds or bills of exchange.
* Yogev: Jewish merchants transferred silver and coral (!) from London to Madras and sent back diamonds to the tune of £100,000 per year. Mentz sees this as an "alternate private trading network" parallel to the EIC.
* Since letters involving clandestine trade were often destroyed, there are only fragments left; need to focus on repetitive structures rather than individual cases to draw general conclusions.
* Again, major sources of capital shipped to Madras were newly-minted Spanish dollars and Livorno coral. Not uncommon for young merchants to bring initial capital of £1-2000 to Madras.
* "Success in Asia was to a large extent dependent on individual contacts to the City of London," specifically either initial capital or personal contacts/letters of introduction.
* Many traders brought silver to China (rather than Madras) and traded it there for gold to make dividends of 40% or so; this gold would then be brought directly back to England. The profits could then be brought back in the form of silver for reinvestment by the merchant in the country trade
	* Wine (to be sold to Englishmen abroad) was also considered a good and safe investment
	* Many servants of the Company acted as commissioners for London merchants in this trade, keeping a portion of the dividend for themselves, while also benefitting from their demonstrated liquidity and connections
* Contemporary estimate of total bullion exports to India averaged around £500,000 per year in the late 17th C; Mentz estimates that ~£100,000 of this was destined for private traders.
* In general Madras merchants raised loans from others in India, but for lower interest and longer credit times they might seek credit from London
* It was the wealthiest merchants who brought the most private capital from England, and also they who tapped into the most profitable of the country trades; Mentz argues that part of the explanation for why more money did not flow from London to Madras is the desire to maintain control of the private trade by the most well-connected merchants.

Chapter Four: Private Trading between Madras and London

* The most important part of the private trade was that in diamonds, because it was discreet, produced high profits, and was never controlled by the Company.
* Nathaniel Chomley earned £50,000 in 19 years by working as a commissioner for London diamond merchants in Madras, while the same merchants looked over the money he sent back in (diamond) remissions to England.
	* because diamonds were purchased uncut, it required great skill and experience to make a reliable profit buying them: involvement was restricted to a few agents and their personal contacts.
* A company trader made a personal purchase of diamonds as early as 1639; the Company permitted private trading of diamonds to London after 1660, institutionalizing what before then had been scattered and clandestine
* Although diamonds were actually cut in Amsterdam, Dutch cutters preferred to purchase through English commissioners, with whom they could develop personal relationships, than through wholesale VOC cargoes
* Thomas Pitt, one of the wealthiest Madras merchants, had his son smuggle a massive diamond purchased for £20,000 back to England; it was eventually sold for £135,000. The highest profits were made by merchants rich enough to afford tying their capital up for a year or more before finding the best buyer
* Respondentia market: Many merchants sold high-interest loans on ship cargoes which did not have to be paid back if the cargo was lost.
* Ship captains and crews were allowed to use a total of about 10% of the ship's hold for their own personal trading goods; they often acquired capital for these cargoes by taking out respondentia loans in Madras; they would usually also rely on locally-based merchants to make purchases for them.
	* The relationships developed between captains and merchants also made room for captains to assist merchants in smuggling private goods, by claiming them as their own and then bringing them to the merchant's commissioner in London.
* Thomas Pitt and Edward Harrison both sent goods and letters between England and India on Danish ships, but this was relatively rare. After the founding of the Ostende East India Company of the Austrian Netherlands, a number of English merchants remitted goods back home on Ostende ships, some being fined by the EIC for doing so.

Chapter Five: The Significance of the Diaspora

* Comparison with Indian merchants: Metz concludes that neither the Iranian nor any Indian merchant groups had enough state support to develop long-lasting structures; they were fundamentally dependent on individual merchants' successes. They were thus also more vulnerable to political changes. By contrast, "The core of the \[English\] diaspora was not single persons but a set of commercial rules, a moral codex for accepted behaviour making it easy to establish personal business contacts with merchants living far away in a foreign country." Importance of cultural ties and shared set of mutually intelligible rules in maintaining Madras' consistent growth over the decades.

Chapter Six: Social Elements of the Diaspora

* The continuity from above was partially achieved via the establishment of "English," "gentlemanly" culture in Madras itself. Necessity of building a network of trustworthy individuals whose reputations could easily checked, in order that distant people could act as each others' commissioners.
* "Commercial rivalry was normal among merchants but these cases did not affect the solidarity among the English inhabitants because mutual dependency was a cultural necessity and even commercial rivals did not want to destroy English social habits."
* The tight social community in Madras, sustained by the few dozen full families present at any time, ensured that commercial information and practices could be communicated to new entrants; the private sector thrived despite any individual merchant leaving. This was important in compounding gains to capital
* Circulation of people b/w Madras and London, including the education of children in England, ensured that Madras did not develop a distinct "creole" culture despite a certain amount of acculturation

Chapter Seven: Private trade, companies, and the British Empire

* Comparison to the rest of the British empire; EIC is able to maintain its role, by contrast to the companies which operated in the Atlantic, by its embrace of private trade rather than its suppression of it.
* "That the English merchant diaspora in India managed to create a vital commercial sector which stood its ground in competition withlocal trading structures is best explained by pointing out the ties which connected private merchants to the City of London and their exploitation of the economic potential in the metropolis. No other merchant network ever received such strong economic support from the mother country.

Conclusion

* Emphasis of the English merchant networks' connections to the British empire as a whole; its social construction and continuity; "The private English diaspora represented an independent commercial structure with regard to Indian merchants. Private trade was not founded solely on Indian capital input but represented an independent, solvent and economically strong unit, representing moreover an independent body within the structure of the East India Company."  Connection to London is the best explanation for the eventual dominance of English traders in India in the 18th C.

Questions

* What does Mentz believe is **the nature of the interaction between “Madras and London,” or the metropole and merchant diaspora?** What are the stakes of emphasizing this interaction instead of emphasizing the interaction between the English merchants and the Indian regional context? 
* In a number of places, Mentz emphasizes the importance of “continuity” in the success of English trade. Individual merchants beginning with a small capital needed to spend decades developing their fortunes before they became truly rich, and for the same reason the most successful families (such as the Pitts) spent three or more generations climbing from merchant beginnings to high political office. A number of practices served to maintain cultural continuity between Madras and London, and Mentz considers this maintenance of continuity to separate the English from other groups (e.g. the Iranians represented by Mohammad Sayyid). **How does Mentz use his concept of cultural continuity to explain English success in the Indian Ocean trade?** Why were English merchants especially successful in maintaining this continuity as opposed to other **“diasporas”** in India?
* What was the **role of trust in long-distance English trade?** What was the role of culture in establishing and maintaining networks of trust? **Is “trust” the right way to think about the sorts of relationships Mentz describes?**
* How did the **goods traded privately differ from those which tended to be monopolized by the Company,** and which actors benefitted the most from the trade in these goods? How did social relationships among Madras and London merchants, seamen, and even different East India Companies function to keep this trade flowing despite the Company’s ambivalence?
* Does **Mentz’s argument that the English were able to tap into particularly extensive resources from the metropole due to the private trade based on mutual trust between commissioners give a plausible reason for the expansion of English trade in India before 1750?** Which parts of his overall argument seem more important to the success of English traders, and which less so?
* How does **Kuiters’ picture of an “unplanned” EIC conquest of Bengal fit with Mentz’s depiction of private English trade in India?** Did English involvement in the politics of Bengal (as power brokers, tax farmers, and eventually rulers) grow out of the **logic of “gentlemanly capitalism?” What is the role of private profit in explaining the conquest of Bengal?**
* Finally, what was **most unique about gentlemanly capitalism in the Madras-London circuit:** the complex of cultural traits associated with it, or the way capital itself functioned?
