Lack of historiographical attention to relations between Jews and Muslims in Spain

Week Two

Kamen

* The expulsion of the Jews of Spain has been seen as the reason for Spain's lacking a bourgeoisie and failing to develop capitalism.
* There has been an inflation in the figures of people expelled, from Andres Bernaldez's initial estimate of 170,000 "souls" to 170,000 families, or in most modern accounts 400,000-800,000 people. Yitzhak Baer affirmed the number of 170,000 people.
	* However, even 170,000 people would represent over 3% of the Spanish population, when Jews could not have made up more than .5-1.5% of the total population at the time; i.e. at very most 80,000 people could have been expelled from Castille, and perhaps about 10,000 from Aragon
* Even among this reduced estimate of the total Jewish population, probably the majority either converted or migrated to Portugal or Navarre, rather than leaving Iberia. Of those who left, many went to Provence or Italy, not all to North Africa/Turkey.  In all, about 10,000 departed from Aragonese ports (many of them Castilian), mainly on privately chartered ships.
* After Portugal also banned Jews in 1497, many returned to Castille as _conversos_, perhaps up to one half of total emigres.
* Not just Iberia: "the last decade of the 15th century was a time of crisis for the Jews of the entire Western Mediterranean... Jews were 'expelled' from Parma in 1488, from Castile and Aragon in 1492, from Milan in the same year, from Portugal in 1497, from Navarre in 1498, from Sicily in 1492, from 1496, from Marseille after 1500." 45
* The Spanish crown attempted to introduce the Inquisition to Naples in order to deal with _conversos_, but was repeatedly stymied, not least because Charles V needed Jewish loans for his campaigns in Africa.
* Despite the relatively small number of Castilian Sephardim in Muslim countries, Jewish communities in the Maghreb and Turkey adopted "Spanish" customs and the Castilian language; Kamen sees this as a result of "cultural superiority" of the Castilian Jews, who counted a number of the wealthiest families of Spain among their emigres; these sophisticates "imposed" their language and customs among their more rustic cousins.
	* The Spanish expulsees, despite their smaller-than-expected numbers, "firmly and decisively... penetrated the fabric of international Jewry at many points and changed its character permanently, infusing their language, their ritual, their customs, their mystical philosophy and their historical consciousness."
* Argues that migrations of _conversos_ fleeing the Inquisition may have rivaled those of Jews fleeing conversion. One account gives 5000 families leaving Andalucia in 1480. This emigration was more economically impactful than that of the Jews proper, because the latter were so legally disadvantaged that they had little capital and were certainly not a "nascent middle class;" in fact "there were no big Jewish merchants in late fifteenth-century Spain."

Meyerson

* The Christian kingdoms of Iberia followed a policy of _convivencia_ which appeared to be borrowed from the _dhimmah_ contract, with one crucial difference: the _dhimmah_ was divinely ordained, whereas the practice of _convivencia_ was based on surrender treaties and individual negotiations with specific non-Christian communities, and thus more subject to change. 3
	* Sees the protection afforded to minorities in Christian Spain as also a means of separating them and maintaining a mutual sense of strangeness, which could boil over into hatred when politically necessary, between the religious groups. _Latent antagonism_ as crucial aspect of coexistence.
* The Muslims of Castile and Granada were forced to choose between conversion and expulsion during Isabel's reign; by 1516, then, only the Muslims of Aragon retained the right to practice their religion as _mudejars_. This complicates attempt to explain the motivations of the Catholic Monarchs as purely concerned with religious purity in other cases (e.g. the expulsion of Jews and conversion of Granada).
* The Mudejars of Valencia rose in revolt immediately after conquest, in the 1240s, 50s, 60s, and 70s; there were also corresponding Christian pogroms against Muslims in the 1270s. Further uprisings and riots recurred throughout the 14th C.
	* 1455: A riot against the _moreria_ of Valencia followed threats that the Sultan of Granada was preparing to invade, and even after the Muslim quarter had been thoroughly sacked, "the cry 'the Moors are coming!' sent hundreds of men scrambling to the city's walls." 64
* In 1487, Fernando received reports that the Mudejars of Valencia had contacted the Ottoman Sultan offering to rise up in support if he sent aid to Granada; it appears no conclusive evidence was ever found, but the Nasrids certainly did appeal to the Ottomans and Mamluks, and it is possible the Mudejars could have been involved.
* In peacetime there was a continuous flow of trade between Valencia and Almeria (in Granada), with the Valencian side mostly carried on by Mudejars, though Christians were also involved.
* In 1481, Jews and Muslims of Valencia were made to wear symbols to distinguish them from Christians, to avoid infiltration by Granadans and prevent illicit sexual intercourse with Christian women (!).
* Contact with the states of the Maghreb went both ways: Valencian Mudejars settled in North Africa, but North African Muslims were also accepted into Mudejar communities. Mudejars acted as informants for or even sailed with corsairs, but they could also provide the same service to their Christian liege lords. Mudejars attempted to assist, free, and adopt Muslim prisoners in Spain just as Castilians attempted to ransom Christians taken to North Africa.
* Explanation for slow decline in Mudejar restiveness: on one hand, the Mudejars were sensitive to power shifts, as they became a minority in Valencia and as the Christian kingdoms became ascendant over those of Western Islam; on the other, centuries of cohabitation slowly made the situation more tolerable: "As Valencia's society evolved, with Muslim-Christian interaction at its core, the original relationship of colonialist Christian conqueror to vanquished Muslim subject was transformed into the more tolerable, albeit highly imperfect, one of social majority to minority." 81

* Mudejar resistance in the late 15th century was mostly informal: guidance of raiders and pirates, prison and brothel breaks, and even a network of guides for escaped slaves across _morerias_.
* Last question: to what extent was popular Christian violence against Mudejars inspired by or connected to the royal rhetoric of crusade?
	* Constant preaching of crusade against Islam frequently overspilled into persecution of Mudejars, even in the rhetoric of overzealous priests and friars, despite Fernando's orders to the contrary.
	* In Valencia, anti-Muslim violence was usually visited on Maghrebis, not Mudejars -- locals saw these groups as distinct.
	* Constant presence of alms-seekers for the ransom of captives kept the image of Muslim atrocities fresh in the minds of Christian Spaniards, and could easily have inspired fear and hate towards their neighbors.
	* No serious massacres of Mudejars in Valencia during Fernando's reign, though they did occur in 1455 and 1521;  prevented by royal policy? Meyerson sees such violence as mostly linked to socio-economic stress: "As members of Valencian society the Mudejars occupied particular socioeconomic niches that linked them by bonds of interest to specific Christian social groups. When economic stress placed the latter in conflict with other Christian groups, not only were the Mudejars affected  by virtue of their special alliances, they were also singled out on account of their religious difference.
		* 1455: A plague in 1450 followed by drought and high food prices in 1455 led to a riot of the economically most marginal Christians ("vagabonds, apprentices of artisans as men foreign to the city, of poor and minor condition"), who looted the Muslims' homes. 89
		* 1521: Demographic growth from the 1490s on had sparked inflation of food prices, again burdening the poor; the _Germanias_ (brotherhoods of artisans) rose in revolt in 1519, at first seeking to overthrow the privileged merchants and nobles. Meyerson believes that when, in 1521, they turned to massacring Mudejars, it was meant as an attack on the revenues of the signeurs. Later they turned agaisnt the "wealthier Muslims of the irrigated zones who were competing with Christians for free lands;" thus this uprising was precipitated by social and economic tensions distinct from religious antagonism, but which brought that antagonism "to the fore".
	* Stronger pressure came during 1500-1503, as Isabel was forcibly converting the Muslims of her domains. Fernando was forced to order officials to gainsay rumors that the Aragonese Muslims would also be pressured, and even threatened penalties against anyone disturbing the Mudejars; during this period he enforced a 1488 ban on emigration of Muslims. During this time the nobles of Valencia urged the king to allay the fears of their Mudejars, who were supposedly abandoning their fields and preparing to either emigrate or resist. The huge rise in corsair raiding in 1502-1503 was seen as being connected.

Nirenberg

* In medieval Spanish theology, God was variously presented as both the begetter of Christians, giving him the rights of a father, and as their spiritual spouse, giving him the rights of a husband. --> sexual behavior had to have the approval of God as paterfamilias. In particular, no "kinship of the flesh" with the unbaptized.
* Sexual honor analogizes the claims of the Christian community to the claims individuals made within their own families. Simmel saw honor as functioning to stabilize social groups and separate them from others even while it made individuals feel that this group honor constituted their "most intrinsic, most profound, and most personal self-interest."
* Late 14th century altarpiece depicts a woman having her throat slit by the communion wafer after taking a Muslim lover (!)
* Analogies of corruption and contagion used to bridge the gap between individual sexual honor and the welfare of the group; sense that sin would redound against the whole group via plague, famine, etc.
* Frequently mentions that Jews and Muslims policed their communities' sexual boundaries in an analogous manner to that of Christians (e.g. on 1073); but what is the proof that they did? If they did, how does that change our understanding of the reasons for this policing - surely the cause can't be found any longer in theology, so is it something in the ground of Spanish society ("honor" more primary than faith)? Or is there a real need felt to prevent behaviors that experience has shown are destabilizing? If so, why guard against inter-community sex in particular?
	* What comes first: bans on interfaith sex, or interfaith marriage?
* Points out that literary and legal sources from medieval Spain do not show either many concrete cases of adultery between Christians and Muslims, nor of horror of the concept. Alfonso the Wise wrote dirty poems about a Christian woman fighting "sexual duels" with a Muslim knight!
	* The one case where people were highly concerned with interfaith sex was that of Christian prostitutes, who faced death for taking non-Christians as clients. (A hygiene anxiety?) Burden lay on prostitutes to distinguish non-Christians by inspecting for circumcision(!)
* The anti-Jewish massacres of 1391 led to mass conversions, and consequently to the beginning of anxiety about _converso_ insincerity and purity of blood; the need to separate "natural Christians" from "new Christians" took on a sexual/reproductive dimension, along with being the cause for the Inquisition.
	* Particular concern w/ the intermixing of conversos and Jews, with the resulting consequence that Jews were able to pass as Christians and have sex with Christian women.
	* "Such letters make clear that concern with sexual boundaries rose to the forefront of Christian consciousness in the very first moments of mass conversion." 1080
	* However, this "concern" did not become a justification for spatial segregation, and Jewish and Christian populations still tended to intermingle in most Spanish cities.
* "This still relatively permissive religious topography was transformed by the preaching of St. Vincent," who exhorted the full conversion of the Jews of Spain; he particularly argued for segregation as a way to avoid "conversation," i.e. intercourse, between Christians and non-Christians.
* ![[./_resources/Subrahmanyam,_Religion.resources/Screen Shot 2022-01-09 at 1.12.27 PM.png]]
* "What is most striking about the generation of 1391 is its relative disinterest in _converso_ orthodoxy and orthopraxis, the very issues that would come to characterize the later period." In other words, the increase in rhetoric about Jewish and Muslim sexuality cannot be seen as strategically intended to pressure more conversions
* "The perception of crisis was provoked not by the converts' "Jewish" practices but by a much more complex phenomenon: the mass conversion's destabilization of an oppositional process of identification by which generations of Christians had defined themselves theologically and sociologically against Jews and Judaism."
* Finishes by arguing that, starting in the 1430s/40s, a "sexual logic" was replaced by a "reproductive" one more concerned with the danger to the "breeding stock" represented by Jewish "blood" in the Christian social body

The Problem of Unbelief in Rabelais

* Conflict between finite (Greek) and infinite (Christian) conceptualizations of harmonious universe. Issue that the Latin language, which was considered necessary for novel or rigorous thought, was locked in antique aesthetic incompatible with Christian notion of infinity, and that additions to it brought it further away from the idealized classical language of Cicero. To go beyond the Greek "universe" required an assimilation of their knowledge into living languages; a process which was not complete until the 17th century (Descartes as paradigm)
	* Impossibility of seeing the "unbelief" of people in Rabelais' time as being comparable or ancestral to "rationalism" and "freethinking," which depend at a basic level on developments in language and natural science which did not mature until the 17th and 18th centuries.
