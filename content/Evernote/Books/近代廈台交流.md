廈門的台灣籍民問題 (2-7)
台灣風物，第37卷第1期，1926/09

* This report was prepared by the Japanese ambassador in Xiamen, Inoue Kojirou 井上庚二郎 for a conference in Tokyo on trade with the Nanyang in 1926; he mentions that certain details touch upon state secrets and not to be divulged to foreigners.
* Includes a table of population figures according to which the Taiwan jimin population in Xiamen has grown from 2883 in 1917 to 6832 in 1926, an average increase of 400 people per year. However, the ambassador admits that a large number of immigrants from Taiwan, as well as marriages and births within the Taiwanese population, are not actually registered with the embassy, while on the other hand, a number of Xiamen locals have been granted Japanese passports purely because they want to enjoy the protection of the Japanese government, or because they hope to improve their quality of life. The majority of these Xiamen "Taiwanese" are politically or economically weighty figures.           
* Inoue estimates that about one quarter of Taiji households (a total of over 2051 individuals) are involved in "special professions 特殊營業," i.e. prostitution or opium, while another quarter are unemployed or have no fixed career. Of the other half of households, the largest categories of professions are General Goods stores (168 households), Restaurants (90), Medical herbs (26), Ocean products (22),  Money changers (19), Clothing stores (19), Tea shops (17), Tobacco (16), Grain merchants (13), Doctors (12). The number of households registered as running opium dens alone is **195\.**                   
* Inoue explains the preponderance of opium in Xiamen-Taiwanese society as a combination of the Taiwan jimin's legal protections, and the importance of Xiamen as a distribution center for the substantial opium yields of Minnan. “Due to the desire of partaking in these bounteous resources, Xiamen has become the distribution center for inland opium, and the increase in the number of opium merchants is an unavoidable consequence. Taking advantage of this trend, in recent years those who have come to Xiamen have seen opium trafficking as the best source of income, and in order to preserve this income, they have established something similar to a monopoly organization (類似專賣制度之機關). This organization on the surface refers to its mission as enacting opium prohibition, but in reality its direct objective is the restriction of private opium sales and the levying of opium taxes."
* Claims that a number of Taiwanese names registered as part owners of opium dens are in fact fronts: poor Taiwanese who are paid a small sum in exchange for serving as a shield for the non-Taiwanese investors actually profiting from their shares in these dens.
* Much of the "opium prohibition office," including many of the inspectors and tax collectors, are in fact Taiwanese citizens as well. Of course collection of the poppy tax on the mainland is also partially in the hands of Taiwanese (Zeng Houkun is not mentioned by name...)                                      
* Ends the report by decrying the obvious hypocrisy involved in, on one hand, holding up the Taiwan Opium Monopoly Bureau as a model for opium prohibition to the world at a recent "International Opium Conference," while at the same time it is Taiwanese who openly dominate opium trafficking in Xiamen. Urges for the suppression of this trade, while admitting that it will be very difficult to convince the Taiwan jimin in Xiamen to turn to any other business... 

Continuation from the same journal on 152-154:

* Claims that the protection of the Taiwanese from military justice in Xiamen is a great joy, as the naval administration executes suspected criminals on the slightest shred of evidence, and imprisons petty criminals in inhumane conditions. Further, the police are notably abusive, and it is only the understanding of the police chief himself, who spent time studying in Japan and therefore understands how to treat foreigners, which partially protects the Taiwanese residents of Xiamen.
* The editor admits that "from a certain point of view" the immunity of Japanese passport-holders from local taxes is indeed unfair, and suggests that KMT protests against extraterritoriality have made it impossible to maintain this privilege.
* Now, with the increasing Chinese protests against unequal treaties, there are risks that the Taiwanese in China will lose their special privileges; the editor beseeches the ambassador at Xiamen to protect them, and affirms that it is unlikely that the Japanese empire can accept the reversion of people who, under its banner, "have tasted the full fruits of a civilized nation," to a state of life they left 30 years ago.
* Admits that there are difficulties with Taiwanese criminals, but explains that the solution is impossible to solve, as 1. the ambassador cannot allow Japanese citizens to be tried in Chinese courts, where they might be executed without evidence; 2. the embassy does not have sufficient facilities to try and punish criminals; 3. criminals deported to Taiwan are likely to find ways back to Xiamen, having shaken off surveillance and their criminal record; 4. they cannot be punished by the Taiwan government for crimes outside the country, and knowing this, many criminals remain on their best behavior in Taiwan until they can find a way back to Xiamen.                                                                                                          

廈門台灣公會會長辭職 (p. 300)
（廈門日報）1908/02/06

* Reports that the inaugural head of the Taiwan Gonghui, "Mr. Shi," who also established the association, has resigned his post due to the heavy burdens involved, especially following certain negotiations with banks brought on Shi by actions of his "弟." The paper expresses doubts that his successor will be as capable as Shi Fanqi.

施范其創辦廈門台灣公會 (p. 300)
（廈門日報）1908/02/19

* Shi Fanqi is a descendent of Marquis Jinghai (Shi Lang), who has carried on the family tradition of commerce. He was a valued collaborator with the Japanese regime on Taiwan (為日政府所器重). Despite being a pillar of the Xiamen community, he was forced to leave (for Southeast Asia?) to seek new business opportunities after taking on the vast debt of his younger brother. Although he was able to pay hundreds of thousands after fund-raising among Xiamen's Japanese merchants, he lost all his capital and had to seek to rebuild his fortune elsewhere.

301 - 廈門台民加入台灣公會情形
(廈門台灣公會報，第43號，1926/06/30)

* According to Japanese statistics, of over 6,000 Taiwanese in Xiamen, only around 900 are members of the Taiwan Gonghui. The three reasons people give for not joining the Gonghui are 1. They see no personal benefit; 2. They don't want to pay the monthly fee; 3. They resent some perceived failure of the Gonghui to solve a past problem. The Gonghui Bao denounces each of these reasons as petty and small minded, and mounts a vigorous defense of the need for collective organizations in a competitive world. "宜損除成見，從遠大處著眼，試觀各省府縣之人，不論旅居何處，皆設有會館。且各姓亦組織有各姓之自治會。。。”

廈門總商會與台吳事件
Article from the 1993 book 廈門商會檔案史料選編

* Explains that, since the police were unable to touch the Taiwanese langmin, the Shanghai had to form militias out of the local toughs, especially from among the three surnames, of whom the Wu clan was strongest. ”由於日籍台灣浪人憑借日本駐廈領事館為靠山，地方軍警干涉乏力，於是商會出面召集地方各大姓，組織團練義勇，稱為“保衛團”，負責維持社會秩序。” 154
* The Wu-Tai incident occurred on 1923/09/17, when a patrol organized by the Wu family encountered a Taiwanese named Lin Rucai shaking down a local family for a predatory loan; an argument ensued, ending with Lin Rucai shooting one of the Wu men dead. Both sides called for help, and a shootout between the Wu family and the Taiwanese "Self-protection squad" 自衛隊 occurred in which a bystander was killed as well. In the immediate aftermath, the Japanese ambassador called for military assistance, and 4 steamships entered Xiamen harbor, with marines landing on 09/20. The Taiwanese began seeking out and killing or capturing Wu men, while the Wus were supported by the Shanghui, the Construction Guild, numerous civilians, and (secretly) by material assistance from Zang's army.
* Realizing the situation was untenable, the Japanese ambassador asked the Taiwan Gonghui to negotiate with the Shanghui. The actual negotations were carried out primarily by Zang and his administration on one hand, and the Japanese ambassadors, police, and naval captain on the other, though Cai Yucun, Zeng Houkun, and Liao Qibu (廖启埔 vice-head of the Taiwan Gonghui) are listed as participating. The groups decided to form two joint committees to investigate matters. During the investigations, a corpse was found floating in the waters near Gulangyu, and the Japanese ambassadors demanded compensation from the Shanghui for the death of someone they said was a Japanese citizen, without providing any evidence.
* In the end, the Shanghui agreed to apologize to the Taiwan Gonghui, while Zang's administration formally apologized to the Japanese ambassador, and Cai Yucun personally paid 1100 yuan in compensation for the corpse found by the Japanese. In early 1924, a feast was held in which both sides toasted to peace.
