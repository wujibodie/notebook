Zhu Yuanzhang deported the entirety of Nanjing’s original population to Yunnan, and replaced it in part by bringing in 45,000 “wealthy households” from Suzhou etc., who were registered as common laborers, though many of them seemingly held on to property in their home regions, and were able to buy their way out by hiring replacement laborers (using cash? Silver?). 15,000 wealthy households were brought from elsewhere to serve as 福戶 in the new capital, along with 20,000 specialized artisan households. 144-146

* The new population of Nanjing was organized into _fang, xiang, xiang_, with the population w/in the walls organized by _fang_ usually separated by place of origin or occupation.
* Many of these households (esp. artisans) were shortly thereafter resettled in Beijing during the Yongle reign, with damaging results for the local fang-xiang / lijia systems.
* Gu Qiyuan noted that in the middle of the fourteenth century “the governor of the imperial prefecture noted that the corvée exactions tended to fall heavily on the rural _shiang_ units; the urban _fang_ and _hsiang_ commoners were exempt but paid an annual cash fee. He proposed that the three kinds of units be integrated and the burden rationalized.” Could this be Zhou Chen？

Later Ming Nanjing became seen as the center of an alternate course of life for officials who wanted to avoid the dogfighting and onerous responsibilities of Beijing politics; it developed a reputation for leisure and elegance, and attracted many officials of literary talent. The existence of a secondary capital with a different set of duties for high-ranking officials was unprecedented, and might have offered political possibilities had things gone otherwise; “the rich life of the great city came close to being politically subversive as well as morally scandalous in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.” 152
