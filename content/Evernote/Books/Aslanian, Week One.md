**Bishara**
First round of Omani expansion into East Africa came under the Ya'rubis, who expelled Portugal from Muscat in 1650; they were overthrown and eventually replaced by their former subjects, the **Busa'idis** between 1720-1800.
Case study of Sewji Topan, Kutchi merchant who arrived in Muscat in the 1780s and bankrolled a number of the Sultan's military campaigns in East Africa, them became hereditary customs farmer in Zanzibar and East Africa (as a whole?). Topan and similar merchants represent the focus of recent historiography on merchant worlds of Indian Ocean, esp. w focus on the accounts of trust, reputation, and credit in regional trade networks.

* "As revealing as they have been, discussions of networks and trust have been perhaps too narrowly focused. With only some exceptions, most studies of networks in economic history have told us little about how members of these networks interacted with actors outside the trust circle. The emphasis on trust within the network has also tended to obscure as much as they have revealed. As astute as these new Indian Ocean eco- nomic histories have been in their analysis of the internal legal dynamics of merchant networks, they have generally ignored the pivotal question of how these internal dynamics intersected with an external institutional world \- courts, tribunals, and the state. "  Necessity to bring in the **law**. 9

The Jurist's perspective on Indian Ocean trade (in this case Nasser bin Abi Nabhan, son of a foundational Ibadhi legal scholar and companion of the Busa'id sultan): "For a jurist like him, law \- especially the _shari'a,_ that complex of social, political, moral, and legal discourses given expression in a range of different texts \- under\- pinned every aspect of political and economic life in the Indian Ocean. Where historians see trade networks populated by members of distinct ethnic or religious groups, he would have seen matrices of rights and obligations held together by contracts - intersections of nominate categories in Islamic jurisprudence and the conglomerations of commercial and juridical actors (himself included) and institutions that shaped them. Their circulation around the Indian Ocean was what gave the region its structure \- not networks. To the jurist, the notion that abstract bonds of trust made the commercial world go round would have been mislead- ing: if actors trusted one another at all, it was because they had a deep well of law to draw from \- one which structured their exchanges with one another, shaped their expectations of one another, and furnished them with a set of institutions that they could fall back on when trust gave way to suspicion." 10-11

_waraqa_s - deeds to debt based on "hypothecated" property which individuals could put up to finance ventures such as plantations. Bishara sees _waraqas_ as the crucial tool which connected Indian financiers, Omani merchants/settlers, and the East African coast; without the regularized debt/credit relationship they represented, and the legal system backing them, people would not have been able to give up their property in Oman or Gujarat to move to Mombasa or Zanzibar. Move to _obligation_ rather than just trust in understanding IO commercial networks.

**Hofmeyr**
Commentators on geopolitics, such as Robert Kaplan, increasingly see the Indian Ocean as a scene for coming great power conflict in the post-US-hegemony world. From academic perspective, Indian Ocean is seen as a "post-national" space displaying "lateral networks that fall within the Third World or Global South." Hofmeyr reminds of the sheer variety of connections across the sea, from the early modern period to now, which defy easy categorizations (for example, African slaves, Indian indentured servants, and white settlers) and perhaps trouble even the more promising forms of Africa-Asia solidarity beyond the nation state.

**Pearson**
Chaunu claimed that the Indian Ocean was not an "autonomous universe," but rather just an extension of the Eastern Mediterranean. Pearson points out that the Indian Ocean is the "oldest sea," and has been densely navigated for perhaps 5000 years.
Difference b/w Indian Ocean and Mediterranean: almost all of the Mediterranean is in sight of land, and it was dominated by related peoples along its whole littoral, whereas the Indian is largely open ocean and thus connected very distinct groups.
Question of ultimate "unity" of the Indian Ocean; most historians have seen long-distance maritime trade as relatively marginal, and connections across it to tenuous to create a common "space."

* Rene Barendse argued that there was a single maritime world: "There was the garland of harbours along the coasts: échelles where maritime trade met land-routes; " these harbors led to synchronicity in crops, coins, texts.

"Along the southeast Arabian and Somali coasts when the strong winds of the southwest monsoon blow coastal water away from the shore, one gets an upwelling of nutrient rich cold water This may have ten or even twenty times the nutrients of normal surface water. " This makes the monsoon period ideal for fishing in the region, but some years leads to plankton blooms that kill off the region's fish altogether.
