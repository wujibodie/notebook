Introduction

* Basic narrative: the Tang and Song Dynasties shared a conviction that the return to and consistent rejuvenation of the literary tradition of antiquity -- "This Culture" -- would provide models for action based on the patterns of nature and the examples of ancient history. The end of the Tang Dynasty and start of the Tang saw a shift towards the human side of this equation: "scholars supposed that they could infer values for the present from the sages' actions and writings, that they could manifest these values through writing in an ancient style, and that they could put these values into practice through government." However, in the Southern Song this focus on human affairs was challenged and replaced by the _daoxue_ focus on natural patterns 天理 as the foundation of moral life. This shift also implied a shift from belief that truth was contained in the "cumulative cultural tradition," to belief that the individual mind was capable of rationally deriving true ideas on its own through reflection and investigation.
	* Sees this shift as tracking changes in the "corporate identity" of the 士 from the great clans of the early Tang to the more numerous educated elite of the Southern Song.
	* Northern Song intellectual culture played out a tension between "individual cultivation" and "sociopolitical responsibility" inherent in the notion of _guwen_. 5
	* Comparison of familial advice books from early Tang and late Song: shift from "literary-historical" model of _shih_ identity to an "ethical-philosophical" one.
* Crisis-driven model of intellectual change: "I suspect that the intellectual interchange \[between the "three schools"\] was not constant over time, that moments of general social and political crisis affected all who benefited from the patronage that came with stability and prosperity, and that people were more likely to seek answers outside what they already knew when they were confused about what they should do." 19
* Ends introduction by rejecting the chronology of daoxue thinkers proposed by Zhu Xi, and instead argues for a study of the multiple strands of intellectual life in the Northern Song, placed in historical context and order; his final citation is of Qian Mu!
