Preface by Lupercio Leonardo de Argensola, brother of Bartolomé:

* Defends Argensola's work against critics on numerous counts; first, against the charge that the "Conquest of the Moluccas" is only treated in the last of the book's 10 chapters, Lupercio counters that Argensola needs to explain how the islands were lost to Dutch "heresy" in order to explain how they were won back; against the charge that Argensola includes too many romantic anecdotes, Lupercio points out that Livy does the same; against the charge that the book "is full of translations and metaphors, more becoming of a poet than a historian," Lupercio says that "they do not reflect that these terms are related, and not distant: and that passing from one to the other with modesty is a virtue, not a vice," again bringing up classical examples (Thucydides, Sallust, Tacitus, Herodotus, Xenophon, Livy). CLXI-CLXII

1: "victoria digna... no tanto por la raro fertilidad de aquellas provincias, como por haber quitado con ella á las armadas septentrionales una gran causa de navegar nuestros mares, porque no inficionen con herejía la pureza de la fe reciente de los indios asiáticos y de los habitadores de nuestras colonias que tratan con ellos."
12: Given the diversity of languages in the islands of the Moluccas, Argensola supposes that they must have been settled by distinct groups; some claim that the Chinese were the first to bring sailing to those parts, while others say that most Moluccans descend from the "Yaos." In any case, cloves have been cultivated and traded from these islands since classical times, being carried to the Persian Gulf and Red Sea, and with the natives being rich in gems and silks as a result. A. claims that the Roman conquest of Egypt was partially motivated by the greed for the spices which passed through the Red Sea; the Genoese and Venetians controlled the import into Europe from Asia until the Turks took Constantinople and then the trade shifted to "Barito," Aleppo, Damascus, and Alexandria, until the Portuguese seized the trade after discovering the Cape Route. In the success of the Portuguese blockade of Egyptian sea traffic, Argensola sees proof of Themistocles' saying that "todas las cosas posee quien posee los mares." 13
15-16: Magellan was in Malacca at the time of Serrano's wars on behalf of Ternate against Tidore, and received estimates of the islands' latitude from correspondence with Serrano; after his return to Portugal, he felt slighted by that court, and in revenge presented a "Planisferio" to Charles V on which the Moluccas were drawn on the Castilian side of the Tordesillas Line.
28: The Portuguese began to lose their prestige in the region after a mutiny of their soldiers following the failure of the galleon with their pay to arrive on time ending in their attempting to sack the island of Tobana, with a large number being killed or injured by the natives. The consequences of this event turned many of the natives against the Portuguese.
33-34: Argensola imagines a "harangue" by the Queen of Ternate against the Portuguese at the moment of a general mutiny against them. She inveighs against Portuguese offenses  "á vuestro Rey, á vuestra Patria, y á vuestra Religión." A. cites "the Latin history of Maffeo" for this speech. The mutiny ends with the murder of the Portuguese captain and a truce between the Ternatans and the surviving Portuguese, who were happy enough to see the tyrannical captain Pereira killed, especially as he had attempted to ban the _casados_ from privately trading in cloves.
36-37: The Portuguese attempted to hold the Ternatan prince hostage, but were forced to give him up after a coordinated siege of their fortress brought them close to starvation. The prince was crowned King Dayalo, and immediately made shows of severity. He was deposed and fled to Tidore after an episode in which several Portuguese soldiers were killed by locals in revenge for certain robberies and rapes, which the Portuguese (with the encouragement of Ternatan dissidents) saw as the work of the King. Argensola's comment: "Oh cuánto deben procurar los Príncipes asegurarse en el amor de sus súbditos!" Dayalo was replaced by his younger brother Tabarija, supported by the Portuguese.

* The Portuguese Captain Vicente de Fonseca was bitterly criticized by certain Portuguese who thought this adventure was reminiscent of Fonseca's own mutiny against his predecessor Pereira; "y el mismo Fonseca andaba mal compuesto con su conciencia, y tan peligroso y poco seguro, que nunca desamparaba las armas, triste, melancólico, y con todos los accidentes que cría en lo interior la memoria del delito, y deseara verse descargado de la obligación en que se puso." But at the same time, Tabarija attempted to avoid his brother's fate by openly favoring the Portuguese, and this in turn was the cause for war with Tidore. Fonseca was relieved of his duties by the next carrack, and returned to Goa to live out his retirement with a heavy conscience.

40: Tabarija was ultimately sent to Goa to be tried on charges of conspiring against the Portuguese, and was found innocent. He died in Malacca on his way back to Ternate, "showing signs of a true Christian," and was said to have willed his kingdom to King Juan III of Portugal. When the news was sent to Ternate, where the Portuguese garrison took control of the entire island with consent of the principal local leaders and lifted the Portuguese royal standard. The news reached Lisbon in 1549.
42: Cites the Decades of Diego de Couto for a story about the mother of the last king of Ternate, who attempts to hide him from the Portuguese in a garden of night-flowering jasmines (which the Portuguese call _arbol triste_). The forcible Portuguese enthronement of the boy-king Aerio, achieved only by the murder of his mother, and the  is said to be the last straw for a confederation of rulers of nearby islands to form against the Portuguese.

* Argensola ends the first book with the vignette of Queen Putriz, aunt of Aerio, tearfully cursing the Portuguese at the grave of her sister.

43: "Las Armadas de Castilla y de Portugal continuaban en estos tiempos sus navigeciones, por las derrotas sabidas para el Archipiélago. Castilla por Nueva España a Filipinas, y Portugal costeando el Africa, hasta la punta de Malaca. Por ambas partes se ejerctaba el dominio, el trato con ambición, y las relaciones todas dicen que con crueldadñ pero el teatro más sangriento de perpetuas tragedias era Ternate y todo el Maluco: en él peleaban ambas naciones Españolas con armas, y sus Reyes en Europa con sutilezas de derecho y de Cosmografia."
52-53:  The confederation of Moluccan kings agrees to burn all the cloves of the islands in order to spite the Portuguese, not realizing that this act will actually increase the fertility of the soil rather than render it sterile. This episode provides A with an excuse to give a description of cloves, along with a history of their discovery (by the Chinese) and use.

* A sees the desperation of the Moluccans as a  cause to reflect on the greed which causes people to fight and to make journeys to the remotest parts of the world to win spices "más por lascivia de sus manjares que para adquirir robusticidad en la salud." 58

59: Argensola cites certain Jesuit relations for a speech by the King of Tidore, the elected head of the anti-Portuguese confederation, to his assembled allies. The king describes "our Spanish enemies" as "the robbers of the globe, who have usurped it while covering up their avarice with magnificent and pious titles."

* Clear Brittanic model: "y hasta que convierten las provincias en soledades, no les parece que tienen introducida en ellas la paz."
* Tacitus' original: "_Auferre, trucidare, rapere, falsis nominibus imperium, atque, ubi solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant."_
* See also Ginzburg's _History, Rhetoric, and Proof_  for another reuse of Calgacus' speech...

65: Describes a naval battle between the new Portuguese captain Antonio Galván and a combined Moluccan navy reinforced by "Chinos." Galván wins through use of artillery, and is able to relieve the beleaguered fortress on Ternate.
83-84: Relates an argument which was supposedly made to Philip II in favor of abandoning the Philippines, on the grounds that they are unprofitable, as evidenced by the fact that the Chinese have chosen not to settle them even though they could sustain that enterprise much more easily than the Spanish can. The argument in favor of colonizing the Philippines, besides the most important cause of evangelization, also involves the hope of diverting the trade in spices and especially cloves away from the Malacca-Goa route, where it is said that less than a third of the product finds its way to Europe, the rest being diverted by "Persas y Turcos, Chinas y Africanos," and the King of Achem in particular, in favor of a direct path through Spanish territory.

91: Opens the third chapter by sharing that he has relied on the letters written by priests to the Governor of the Philippines as a fundamental source, and that these have provided him with a number of anecdotes not central to the immediate narrative, but which he feels licensed to utilize as adornments to the story following the model of the Greek and Latin historians. Proceeds to tell the story of the _alférez_ Duarte, who used to secretly sneak out of the Portuguese fortress to visit his lover in Ternate, instructing her in the faith & cet. In the end Duarte's "Indian wife-to-be" is secretly brought into the fortress by Captain Pereira, in imitation of a similar case written of Quintus Fabius Maximus.
97: After the Portuguese were expelled from Ternate, thousands of Christians in that kingdom were massacred, as described in gruesome detail following the annual report of the Jesuits...
100: A Portuguese galleon intended to rescue Portuguese lost in Ambon stopped in Borneo for supplies, only to find that the city had been sacked in a Castilian expedition (1579), and from this point on the Portuguese began to "esteem Manila as a barracks for the recovery of the Moluccas".

* "Por esto, de las India no venían ya sino esperanzas; en las Filipinas no tenían orden, por ser Reinos entonces de otro dueño, para intervenir en aquellas guerras, y así estaban á vista de los martirios y revoluciones..." 

106-109: Francis Drake arrives in Ternate from the Pacific and signs a treaty with the king, including the right to place an English factory on the island. Don Francisco de Toledo in Peru resolves to fortify the Strait of Magellan in order to prevent future corsairs' crossing from the Atlantic to the Pacific.
109-128: Long relation of the voyage of Pedro Sarmiento, dispatched by Francisco de Toledo, to discover the passage from Peru to the Atlantic via the Strait of Magellan. Sarmiento investigates traces of the English passage through the same region, and ends up capturing two English ships at the Azores, allowing him to interrogate them about Drake's voyages. A triumphant eastward Spanish counterforce to Drake's western voyages; the two great oceans circled from both sides, with the Strait of Magellan as the mystic portal between them.

* Argensola justifies this detour in the following terms: "La perfecta historia es testigo de los tiempos, luz de la verda, vida de la memoria, y finalmente maestro de la vida; y para satisfecer a las obligaciones en que la ponen tan altos oficios, no debe perdonar a digresiones importantes, y más cuando se divierten poco del primer sujeto y tuvieron alguna dependencia de él." In this case the digressions are necessary to better understand the prudence of Philip's decision to involve himself (and Spain) in the formerly Portuguese Moluccas in defense of the Catholic faith. 127

137: When Philip II takes his oath as King of Portugal, he promulgates a law "the same as those laws established by his Predecessors, King Don Manuel \[etc\]", according to which it was deemed permissible for the new King of Portugal to defend his territories "con el asiento que pareciese más conveniente, aunque fuese con ayuda de los otros Estadoes de Su Majestad." This decree "opened the door for \[Philip\] to, without prejudice to the old laws, be able to apply the forces of the Crown of Castille to the recuperation or conservation of the Kingdoms of the Oriental Archipelago, where martyrdoms were being exercised without resistance; _y particularmente para recuperear á Ternate puso el Rey los ojos en la comodidad que ofrecen todas las Filipinas, mayor que la India..."_
140-142: When the news reaches the Indies of Castille's union with Portugal, the King of Ternate sends an ambassador, Cachil Naique, to Lisbon, carrying "varias riquezas de la tierra," including porcelains, Chinese silks, pieces of cotton, spices, fruits, aromatic woods, and "conserves" of the same. In Banca and Bintan he encounters the fleet of 20 ships which "Samatra" (Sumatra) was said to annually send to "Catayo" filled with pepper. Argensola believes Sumatra was the source for Solomon's treasures.

* "Entró Maique en la Corte de Achein, llevando tras sí la turba de negociantes Chinas, Japones, Malayos, Persas y Turcos..." Maique successfully convinces the Sultan of Achein to block trade through the Strait to Malacca, and to promise support for an eventual attack on the city.
* Maique then goes to Java, to meet with Thomas Cavendish, who has just arrived via New Spain.

144: As Maique is on his way around the Cape of Good Hope, and just as "the King of Achein enacted his promise," a Spanish fleet captures and executes a group of French privateers led by the condottiero Felipe Strozzi at the Azores; again, the use of \[fascination with\] criss-crossing voyages and the interconnection of events at opposite removes of the globe.
146: At the same time as Maique is visiting the Spanish court, a Castillian ambassador, dressed "en hábito de China," (or: "como Sangley" !), spies on Ternate and Tidore, and brings information back which encourages the Spaniards in the Philippines of their chances of conquering the Moluccas...
149: After defeating and capturing the king of Tidore in a naval battle, the Sultan of Ternate returns home in a canoe decorated with the heads and "members" of the enemy dead. Just like the Scythians in Herodotus or the Germans in Tacitus, the Ternatans make drinking-cups from the skulls of their enemies.
150-53: The princess of Tidore, Quisaira, offers to marry whoever can free her brother the king; supposedly this offer was really intended for her "amante de acuerdo," the Portuguese caballero Ruy Díaz de Acuña. However, a Tidorean named Cachil Salama boldly rescues the king, and convinces the princess to marry him instead, after killing Diaz's nephew Pineiro in a duel.
154: When Cachil Naique finally returns to Ternate, he finds the new king is no longer prepared to negotiate with the Portuguese, but rather seeks to conclude an alliance with the English, who found a factory in the city of Ternate. In their first shipment of spices they earmark packages as gifts to Queen Elizabeth and the Prince of Orange; despite the Ternatans' friendliness to the Protestants, they continue persecuting Christians and invite Muslim preachers instead. All of this is intended to build alliances against the Spanish after Cachil Naique brings word of the Iberian Union.
156: Justifies Philip II's decision to succor the Moluccas from Manila rather than from Goa by reference to "la varia disposición, condiciones y grandes respetos de las naciones naturales de aquellos mares y tierras," leading A. to a discursion on China. Claims that China used to be much larger, as is proven by the frequency by which people (in Southeast Asia?) claim descent from China, but purposefully restricted itself to within the reasonable bounds within which "la luz de su Príncipe pudiese alcanzar y comunicárseles." Since abandoning its outermost provinces, China has banned all travel outside its borders without explicit permission of the "Magistrates." The population is infinite due to the good soil and climate and the bans on emigration and laziness, such that not an inch of land goes uncultivated. They do not make wine from grapes, preferring to eat them, but do make a fragrant drink from the herb called "chía." Further lists the many wondrous products to be found in China, which has such abundance that, besides a few fragrances and dyes, the Chinese wish to buy nothing from abroad except gold and silver. The Chinese believe that everything good in life is attributable to progress of human reason, and that religion is a yoke on liberty. The obstinance with which the Chinese reject all religion is a chief reason for the importance of the Philippines as a base for missionary activities there. Argensola claims that all the profits earned in the Philippines are spent in evangelizing the surrounding regions (161). Says that the Chinese have become desirous of "retaking" the Philippines and hints at the forthcoming story of the Sangley rebellion.
169: The first Spanish expedition to Ternate ends with a retreat home after sickness breaks out in camp. Immediately followed by a description of the Spanish Armada, the defeat of which A. claims inspired the Hollanders and Zeelanders to follow the English example and trespass on the Spanish interests in the ultramar.
173: The next fleet sets off from Manila to Ternate under Captain Juan Morones. Although this armada is well-provisioned, their departure is accompanied by discouraging predictions due to disunity in the ranks. A storm scatters the fleet and sinks the flagship, the galleon Santa Elena, but the remainder of the Spaniards manage to land on Ternate and besiege a fortress on the coast of "Limathao." They defeat a Javan skirmishing party, and later a sally from the defenders of the fort and a neighboring one, but are too weakened to actually take the fort. The Portuguese galleon from which the Spanish expect relief turns out to be "más bien prevenida para la continuación del trato, que de la guerra," and so Morones calls off the siege.
179: A translation of a letter by one Cachil Tulo to Governor Santiago de Vera in which he denounces the tyrannical murder by the Ternatan king of his brother (and the king's uncle) Cachil Mandraja. Cachil Tulo decides to flee Ternate and pledge fealty to the King of Spain, promising to assist with the conquest of Ternate in exchange for the Spanish agreeing to crown him king of the island.

* Duarte Pereira sends a letter supporting Cachil Tulo, and Governor de Vera is inclined to assist, but Cachil Tulo's death shortly afterwards causes de Vera to give up on Ternate and to focus instead on preserving the Philippines from Chinese and Japanese adventurers, "de cuyos robos é incendios jamás están seguras." 181

* Long digression on the career of Andrés Hurtado de Mendoza and his conquests in "Zeilán" in order to introduce this soldier into the Philipinne-Moluccan front. Hurtado is delayed from assisting in Ternate by an imprisonment in Goa at the hands of the Viceroy Albuquerque, who is jealous of his successes (186).

187: Santiago de Vera is replaced by Gómez Pérez de las Mariñas in 1590. The new governor immediately builds city walls and a powerful fortress, as well as an artillery factory. Among his advisors were the Jesuits Antonio Marta and Gaspar Gómez; Argensola quotes from a letter of Marta's (to Dasmariñas) which advocates the advantages of conquering Ternate and the surrounding archipelago "de Amboino hasta Banda." Marta acknowledges that it will be necessary to fight, not only the Ternatans, but all the "Moros" of the region, including those of Banda and Seiraón; the Bandans have lately been harassing Portuguese ships and native fisherman near Amboino, and Marta fears the fortress on that island will soon fall without succor from Manila.
192: Switches scenes to the westward journey of Don Pedro de Acuña, who, after service in the naval defense of Cadiz, is sent as governor to Cartagena, from whence he will eventually reach the Philippines. After heroic victories against English navies, Don Pedro proves to be a wise and noble governor of the city of Cartagena.
198: Tells a strange story of a group of Spaniards who were robbed and murdered by the crew of a Chinese ship they chartered to sail from Manila to Cagayan, the sole survivor being a Spanish woman who was treated "with violence and insolence" before being let off at a port city in China, from which she somehow found her way to a series of judges who eventually brought her in Macao.
199-200: Not having learned from the above story, Dasmarinas attempts to hire 250 Chinese rowers for his new galleys; when threats of forced service are made the Chinese lock up their houses and shops, and refuse to sell the daily necessities which sustain Manila. Dasmarinas declares the Chinese rebels and seizes 50 as galley slaves, at which point the rest manage to pool funds to pay 80 pesos each (far more than the 2 pesos/month the Governor had offered) to 200 "volunteers."
202: King Landara of Cambodia sends an embassy led by a Portuguese and a Castilian to Manila, carrying marbles, porcelains, silks, and a large elephant, asking for assistance against a Siamese invasion. Dasmariñas refuses on the grounds that his armada is occupied with the campaign against Ternate, and in so doing publicly reveals his previously secret invasion plans.
203-204: The Ternatan campaign starts off poorly, as adverse winds and currents force the Spaniards to demand more of the Chinese rowers than expected; these begin to resent their treatment, which is much harsher than promised, and in particular are angered by Dasmarinas’ threat of cutting off the hair of any stragglers: “es este para los Chinos injuria digna de muerte, porque tienen la honra pendiente de sus cabellos.” The mutiny occurs on the night of the 25th, described by Argensola in lurid detail. Notes that the Chinese call the Spaniards “Castillas.” The only surviving Castillas are a Franciscan friar and the governor’s secretary, who hide until the mutiny is over and are unceremoniously dumped on the coast of Ilocos after a period of captivity.
207-8: Description of a ceremony witnessed by the captives of the sailors propitiating a “demon” for good weather at sea, with two or three “endemoniados” serving as vessels for the god’s message.
210: Eventually (following a grisly human sacrifice of a Philippine Christian) the mutinous gallery reaches Cochinchina, where it is confiscated by the King of Tunquin, with the mutineers either scattering across China or being captured and punished by the same king, according to different accounts.
212: A number of armed Chinese vessels arrive shortly after the succession of Luis Perez Dasmariñas; the Spaniards assume they are probing Manila for an invasion, and do not at all believe their professed mission of gathering up Chinese who are abroad without permission. Luis Perez sends a letter to the King of China denouncing the mutineers, who are identified as Chincheos, but his messenger’s journey goes poorly.

\-----

Argensola relies on three fundamental sources for his history: the written reports of Iberian missionaries active in Southeast Asia, the formal examples of the classical historians, and his own fertile poetic imagination. If he was privy to any direct testimony from Spanish returnees, either via interviews of his own or his knowledge of audiences with his patron  Philip III, he does not signal any debt to such informers in his writing.
