Intro

* Around the 16th century, at the approach of the 1000th Hijri year, rulers across the Muslim world began to use a new set of titles with millenarian connotations, such as Sahib Qiran. “In most cases the new titles grounded their claims to authority in cosmological doctrines that anticipated the ordination of one individual to usher in a new era of universal justice, order, and peace. The claims were potent because they effectively articulated the aspirations of the three universal empires and they were plausible because, in the approach of the Hijri year 1000 (1591–2 CE), Muslim societies were primed and charged for great changes in the near future.”
	* The millenarian terminology of kingship, while taken up by the Safavids and Mughals, emerged earlier, in the context of competition between the smaller empires of the Ottomans, Mamluks, Aqqoyunlu, and Timurids after Timur’s death.
* The Ottoman Empire participated in wider currents of millenarianism and rethinking of sovereignty across the Persianate-Islamic world at the same time as the Ottoman sultans retained a basis in the legitimating discourses of holy war, descent from the great Turkic leaders of antiquity, and a claim to succession from the Seljuks; these discourses, however, only justified Ottoman expansion in Anatolia and the Balkans, and new ideas were needed to explain Ottoman claims to conquests of new Muslim territories. Especially after the conquests of Egypt, Syria, and the Hijaz, Ottomans began to ground their rule on new universalist bases in addition to genealogical or juridical ones.
* Cites Moin on the claim that saintliness and sovereignty became increasingly intertwined, and that this common phenomenon demonstrates the interconnection of the major Muslim empires (and perhaps others) in the 16th century.
	* “In this regard, the role of the Ottoman sultan Selim I (r. 1512–1520) was axial, not only in setting the “political, religious, and cultural agenda of the Ottoman enterprise in the sixteenth century,” but in propelling “the complete integration of the Ottoman Empire into the early modern Eur- asian political-cultural zone.”28 Çıpa interprets the common “articulation of ideas on universal sovereignty and expressions of millenarian and messianic expectations” among the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals as a clear manifestation of the “connected histories” outlined and explored by Sanjay Subrahmanyam.”
	* Questions the precise cultural context that led kings to reshape their performances of sovereignty in this connected fashion: “Can a connected history lead to something more precise than an amorphous and pervasive climate of ideas observable through broad phenomena and shared across a wide space? What processes sustained such a shared culture? What were its limits and internal tensions?” Approves of Sebouh’s approach to histories of “entangled individuals,” but emphasizes need for exploration of context to avoid “caricaturing” or “reducing” individual experiences or over-applying idiosyncratic examples; we need a combination of micro-history and biography \[not quite sure what this entails…\]
* Markiewicz’s book focuses on the career of Idris Bidlisi, a scholar-secretary who began his career under the Aqquyunlu, but migrated to the Ottoman court, where he became a historian and ambassador who M claims was a key figure in articulating new Ottoman claims to sovereignty at the time of Selim’s conquests in 1514-17.

Part One

* **Bidlisi’s father, and “primary mentor and teacher,” was a reclusive scholar and followed of Muhammad Nurbakhsh.** Bidlisi rejected his father’s emphasis on scholarly seclusion and entered the court of the Aqquyunlus in the 1480s.
* During the 1460s, the Aqquyunlu completely conquered the Qaraquyunlu confederation, becoming “masters of western and central Iran.” After the age of 10, Bidlisi and his father lived around the court of the Aqquyunlus in Tabriz, and participated in the Persian-speaking urban milieu of a flourishing capital city.
* Bidlisi entered court service in 1480, receiving the patronage of the young Sultan Ya’qub.
	* Another secretary of the Bayandur Dynasty was one Abu Bakr Tihrani, who had previously served the Timurids, and introduced the language of Sahib Qiran in his history of the Aqquyunlus; this term was later used by Bidlisi in his history of the Ottomans, and Tihrani’s work may have been the proximate source of this term/concept for Bidlisi. “Tihrani’s history of the Bayandur dynasty pre- sents one significant legitimating term that Bidlisi adopted thirty years later when writing his history of the Ottoman dynasty. In the lifetime and career of Timur, the term sahib-qiran (Master of the Auspicious Conjunction) connoted the celestial ordination of a world conqueror.”
* Besides the rhetoric of kingship, the four major sultanates of the time all shared similar administrative practices, particularly the extension of privileges to crucial groups to shore up support. “To be sure, carefully cultivated reputations for justice expressed through ideological claims of divine favor certainly helped construe or constitute a king’s authority, yet patronage and the extension of privileges to leading political and social groups helped maintain royal authority in equally fundamental ways… **Such privileges most frequently entailed the privatization of state lands or the granting of extensive tax exemptions on landed property.**” 54
	* The most common form of land grants was the _suyurghul_, a tax-exempt appanage granted to military leaders or scholars, which theoretically was not heritable as it needed to be confirmed by the ruler each generation, but in practice confirmation was normally granted. These could be included in charitable donations and thus permanently alienated from state tax registers. Similar usufruct grants were called _timar_ in the Ottoman and _iqta’_ in the Mamluk Sultanates. Substantial fiscal pressure over time —> need to either expand revenues via conquest or via seizure of appanages, even those granted as pious donations, risking severe pushback. Some of the major Aqquyunlu secretaries who attempted to reform the suyurghul were murdered or executed by people who stood to be affected.
* The civil wars following Sultain Ya’qub’s death seemed to have ended with the entry in 1497 of the Ottoman-educated Sultan Ahmad, who Bidlisi vigorously supported, but this figure was overthrown and killed within 6 months by confederate tribal leaders opposed to his plans to confiscate or tax appanages. Thereafter the stage was set for the rise to power of Shah Ismail, who Bidlisi found himself unwilling to serve, leading to his departure for Istanbul.

Chapter 2

* Upon his arrival in the Ottoman empire in 1502, Bidlisi identified key potential patrons and both wrote early panegyrics to them, and offered to include them favorably in his projected work of history, which became _The Eight Paradises_. "In this sense, Bidlisi used The Eight Paradises not only as an opportunity to laud the deeds of the Ottoman sultans, but also as a vehicle to memorialize in perpetuity key members of the ruling elite who were in a position to aid him," such as Müʾeyyedzade ʿAbdurrahman Efendi, a judge and patron of Persian learning, and İskender Pasha, a military leader and governor of Bosnia.
* Bidlisi's work was presented to the court in 1505-1506, and was both heavily rewarded and much-imitated; however, it did not win him a high administrative post as he had wished.
* Bidlisi's fortunes began to shift as his chosen royal patron, Prince Ahmed, saw support for his succession erode due to military successes by Prince Selim on the frontiers. **Bidlisi thus sought to "hedge his bets" by reaching out to Selim, who he praised as a fitting future king and even Sahib Qiran**.
* Bidlisi was able to leave Istanbul for a pilgrimage to Mecca in 1511, right at the delicate moment of Selim's violent succession to Bayezid, which ended in the killing of all of Selim's brothers.
