Attempts to explain the “urban reversal” of the Middle Ages (shrinking of towns before ~1000, expansion thereafter) by reference to the whole “economic structure” rather than just looking at demography or exchange itself. Sees _distance_ as a prime factor in town-county relations due to the laboriousness of transporting bulky goods on foot or by cart. Extreme importance of the development of a network of small market towns in the 11th century: “No peasant would then be further than seven or eight kilometers from the nearest market. He could travel there and back on the same day;” the exact same phenomenon Kuroda describes as the global basis of peasant economic life.

* This market network system did not exist before 1000; following the Roman system, there was a split between the economic system of the towns, which drew revenue from their hinterlands and produced luxury goods for circulation chiefly among other towns (thus a separate urban circuit dependent on the “fiscal capacity” of the state which the towns served as administrative centers), and that of the countryside, where almost everything was produced on a local basis and where monetary exchange was limited to a handful of crucial goods not available internally (eg salt). Common use of wine as convenient tool of barter. Credit was common and based on the mortgaging of land revenues - Bois sees this as in practice a form of barter since “money” was not involved. No rural labor, grain, or land markets. But there were seemingly weekly market fairs held at churches - seeds of a later commercial breakthrough? Example of the monastery at Cluny, which attracted a _bourg_ and thus became a market center for the nearby villages, which previously had had no nearby town; the connection of the monastic market to the nearby town also created a connection between rural purchasers and urban manufactures, which began to increasingly orient themselves to the new source of demand.
