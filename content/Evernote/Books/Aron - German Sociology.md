1. Systematic Sociology

Tönnies — Gemeinschaft vs Gesellschaft breaks down to **Wesenwille** vs **Kürwille** \- the impulses resulting from habit, memory, and instinct, versus those formed by rational calculation “which starts from an abstractly conceived end In order to determine the technically most appropriate means.” The thinking of Kurwille dissolves temporally conditioned bonds between people in exchange for offering a greater surplus of value; an excess of this “intellect” which causes many to suffer/die from the “hypertrophy” of old bonds  and misconceptions regarding environmentally appropriate (evolved?) ways. 

* Schmalenbach additionally contrasts Gemeinschaft with “Bund.” The former is organic, involuntary, and _unconscious_; “its members become aware of it only when threatened.” By contrast, the Bund is a spontaneously renewed order of fellow-feeling among people who would otherwise be strangers. “The Church is a community, the sect is a federation.”
*  “Perhaps the political significance of these terms will be less obscure if stated thus: in the heat of revolution, full of the spirit of federation, young people desire a communion which they call community. But the passion is appeased and a legal order is again essential. Thus the problem is plainly posed: is a spiritual revolt, which is by nature exceptional and temporary, enough to transform society, or rather, in what circumstances is such a revolt capable of producing a true community or a new organization?” 19. The scientific value of these categories lies in their ability to help describe state changes between them. 

Vierkandt - Attempt to study the mutual influence of sociology and psychology without prioritizing either by appeal to fundamental “social dispositions” which are essential to human beings, e.g. the instinct of submission, the drive for recognition. 
Spann resolves the methodological/metaphysical conflict between individual and group in favor of the group, arguing that the social causality issuing from an analysis that starts with individuals is flawed, and leads to the theoretical expectation of anarchy or Machiavellianism. Given that we actually see “organic” social structures, these must form the most fundamental unit of analysis, and the work of sociology is the investigation of _parts of a whole._ Individuals are incomplete and find it necessary to seek fulfillment through relation (Gezweiung) with other parts of a pre-existent whole. The group as structure precedes these relations; there is no “father” without “family.” Two major questions for this science are the socialization of sentiments and the socialization of action — i.e. how the barrier between individual and group is penetrated to alter the behavior of any subject inside the whole. 

Aron seems quite skeptical of all attempts to productively summarize this branch of sociology, perhaps because he doesn’t believe that it struck on any fundamental insights. “If we were asked: "What is the use of systematic sociology?" we should perhaps have to answer, "None". But if we were asked "What have you learned from it?" we should be able to reply that we had learned to give up such simple concepts as "constraint" or "imitation", and to understand more fully the infinitely diverse relations between individuals, the conflicting types of social relation, the continual exchanges between individuals, and between the individual and social formations, the existence of individual influences even in the most elaborate social constructions, and conversely the existence of collective influences in the most fugitive individual contacts. Systematic sociology adds to our conceptual resources and refines our sense of reality in the attempt to grasp this life which is at once so intimate and so mysterious.”

2. Historical Sociology

Oppenheimer - Marx by way of Proudhon. Rejects the notion that modern inequalities are result of “primitive accumulation;” instead, they predominately result from conquest, most fundamentally enslavement of one tribe by another. Existence of large estates, won through violence, and resultant existence of “free labor” in form of dispossessed rural laborers, is basis for capitalist divide between owners and workers. With equal ownership of land/resources, profit system would be impossible, and so the state would largely wither away. An attempt to use historical analysis to solve the “social problem” of the late nineteenth century. 

A. Weber - Civilizations each undergo natural evolution akin to aging; however, due to the influence of outside events/other civilizations, this development is not determinative so much as it explains the effects of any given event on a given stage of evolution. “In other words, it is only by the integration of events in the general development that their effects are intelligible” (44). This “civilization” is identified with science; “the sociologist's task is to show the action of external factors on the development of science and technology.” By contrast, culture is unique to each group, representing that group’s collective spirit, and its form of change in time is that of punctuated modulation driven by the felt tension between external existence and local consciousness. Civilization:Culture :: Matter:Spirit. Hegel/Marx for civilizations, Spengler for cultures. 

* Aron’s critique of the related but lesser Scheler: the unification of the antinomies body-mind and individual-society fails, because “the structure of the historical world is at a different level from that of individual psychology.” You cannot say certain basic impulses drive behavior in one sphere of society, and others in another, separated sphere; “all the human impulses influence the behavior of individuals in all spheres of society... in every one of their activities the whole personality is involved.” Strikes me as completely on point! 
	* When did people first perceive the idea of “levels” to reality, with the attendant expectation that each level would obey a different physics? This seems like quite an early case of someone explicitly saying that historical law functions differently from psychological law despite the causal interpenetration of one and other.

Sociology of Knowledge
Begins from the Marxist position that ideas (superstructure) are determined by economic order. Mannheim departs from Marxists in developing a fully relativist account: both the ideologies of the bourgeoisie and the proletariat are “perspectives” which are neither fully false nor universally true, as are the beliefs of any individual or group. 

* “Every age and every group re-writes history, but on the other hand all these systems which express historical situations and ways of life com- plete each other in a total process of development, which we are unable to apprehend but of which we can assert that it has a meaning.”

Aron’s critique of both Marxist and relativist sociology of “ideology:” neither framework is able to convincingly demonstrate that they escape the trap of a limited, interested view of reality which they claim all other belief systems fall into. Ultimately, for Aron, ideology must be seen as the transference of an individual self-consciousness onto the plane of history and politics; and this transference is presumably inevitable if we take any attitude whatsoever towards society. “Thus, from the point of view of logic, ideology can only have a psychological significance so long as a metaphysic or a philosophical anthropology has not been able to establish a transcendent truth in the place of the anarchy of desires and preferences.” Ideology will last as long as history. 
While Aron is not satisfied with “historical sociology’s” answers (plurality of cultures, aging of societies, opposition of culture and civilization), he appreciates that the above thinkers problematize the major questions of philosophy of history relevant to sociology: “the rhythm of each partial development, the interrelation of these separate processes, the hypothesis of a total process of development, the varieties and uncertainties of the forms of social conditioning of ideas, methods of historical synthesis, the relations of theory and history (or of statics and dynamics), the need to take account of one's own situation in order to under- stand the exact significance of the discoveries of historical research.” 66

3. Max Weber

Weber departs from Rickert: physical world is infinitely detailed, and scientific knowledge is therefore limited to the very general and repeatable. Thus, science cannot provide us with values, merely with means. Cultural sciences (history, sociology), because the objects of their study are determined by arbitrary human interest rather than by seizing the simplest pieces of empirical reality, can only achieve a limited description of the factors conditioning individual processes. “Reality is not reducible to a system of laws. The plenitude of the sensible world makes impossible any complete explanation. These observations are equally valid for the reality studied by the natural sciences. Prediction is only successful within closed or simplified systems. We could not calculate in advance the ways in which a shattered stone or an exploding grenade would splinter. This inability does not greatly disturb us, since in this case we are only concerned with the general laws of such events. But in cultural matters, we are interested also in the specific characteristics of qualitative phenomena. No body of laws (economic laws, evolutionary laws) exhausts the task or consti- tutes the aim of the science of culture.” With that in mind, historical study can still serve the politician-actor by elucidating proper means to an end on which it however must be silent.
