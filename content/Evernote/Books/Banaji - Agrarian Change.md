Chapter One
Standard narrative of decline (shrinking population, reduced commercialization) in post-200 Roman countryside is contradicted by evidence that large villas increased in number and scale across most of the empire through the 4th-8th centuries. “In short, from one end of the Mediterranean to the other, the late empire signified a ‘late’ expan- sionary cycle which persisted (if one sees it as a ‘long’ cycle) till the middle decades of the seventh century.” 21

* Population probably reached its height in the Byzantine period, and this demographic upswing corresponded w greater market sensitivity on part of producers and more complex trade networks centered on cities.
	

Chapter Two
Weber vs Mickwitz: Weber argued that the late Roman Empire experienced a return to the “natural economy” as opposed to the money economy, in part because he sees the “plantation economy” based on slave labor as the characteristic form of high antiquity, and thus sees that form’s decline as a reversion to “naturwirtschaft,” in the absence of plausible alternatives. The estates “turned away” from the market to protect themselves in times of economic crisis, and this destroyed their ability to maintain their slave populations. Neurath argues, rather, that it was precisely in response to changing market conditions that estates shifted from slave to hired labor, high afforded them greater flexibility in their interactions with the commercial economy.

* By contrast, Mickwitz explains changes in the landed economy by reference to the steady (but not catastrophic) inflation of the third/fourth centuries, which incentivized government officials to seek payment in kind rather than in money.  Banaji disagrees, arguing that the officials and officers who made up the dominant classes actually pushed for payment in gold, since unlike debased currencies the solids did not undergo inflation; but he agrees with M’s characterization of this group as a self-conscious “class”: “Their organized, professional existence gave them a massive superiority over the other strata, who, despite their private accumulations of wealth, remained serialized and incapable of resistance.”
* To go beyond W and M, we need to look at the moment the empire’s economy was converted to gold, fundamentally beginning with Constantine. “The upshot of all this may be summarized as follows: conversion to gold was the decisive economic movement of the late empire, as I argue in the following chapter. Far from being a massive reservoir of expanding ‘natural economy’, it was the state and its impact on the rest of late Roman society which led to the powerful diffusion of an economy based on gold. The solidus became the essential preserver of value, means of payment, medium of circulation, and eventually, indeed, by the end of the fifth century, money of account. Constantine’s social order reveals its peculiar stability in the fact that for the first time in 150 years the Mediterranean had a mass currency which was infinitely stable.”37
* At the same time, the switch to a “bimetallic” currency system created a “stratified inflation” (37: The inflation of the late empire was a ‘stratified inflation’. The differential destinies of the gold and base-metal coinages and the permanent stability of the solidus meant that holders of gold assets were not in fact exposed to an inflationary process at all.) in which those wealthy enough or close enough to the direct source of gold coin in the capital did not suffer from the inflation which characterized the petty economy of bronze. This difference in turn has to do with the nature of gold itself, as “both commodity and money.”

Chapter Three

* Constantine switched to the gold solidus after Diocletian’s silver, and removed the face value of gold coins, allowing their value to openly fluctuate with market conditions. This was fundamental in the monetary system of the late 3rd century on, where values (including of other currencies) were universally expressed in terms of gold (gold becomes the measure of value).
* the bimetallic ratio shifted from 1:12 around 300 to 1:18 in the Byzantine period, largely due to the increased value of gold rather than increased availability of silver. 42 However, this shift seems to have happened rapidly towards the end of this period, rather than slowly over the whole 4th c.
* By contrast, the value of bronze (“debased currency” in general) did steadily decrease vis-a-vis gold for the whole period; Banaji sees this as a structural problem largely unrelated to government policy, but which perhaps was influenced by the boon to gold’s value due to its status as main currency.
* Banaji repeatedly cites an Anonymous account from c. 350, which claims that the economy was disrupted by Constantine flooding the market with gold and assigning it the role of primary means of exchange:
	* “It was in the age of Constantine that extravagant grants assigned gold instead of bronze (which earlier was considered of great value) to petty commercial trans- actions (Constantini temporibus profusa largitio aurum pro aere, quod antea magni pretii habebatur, vilibus commerciis assignavit); but the greed I speak of is thought to have arisen from the following causes. When the gold and silver and the huge quantity of precious stones which had been stored away in the temples long ago reached the public (aurum argentumque et lapidum pretiosorum magna vis in templis reposita ad publicum pervenisset), they enkindled all men’s possessive and spend- thrift instincts. And while the expenditure of bronze itself . . . seemed already vast and burdensome enough, yet from some kind of blind folly there ensued an even more extravagant passion for spending gold, which is considered more precious (quod pretiosius habetur). This store of gold meant that the houses of the powerful were crammed full and their splendour enhanced to the destruction of the poor (Ex hac auri copia privatae potentium repletae domus, in perniciem pauperum clariores effectae, tenuioribus videlicet violentia oppressis).”
* although Constantine was important, gold coinage began circulating in larger quantities already in Diocletian’s time (why?).
* A major driver of the shift to gold was the expansion of the system of “grades” for officers and bureaucrats, which entailed a major expansion in direct payment of officials (especially military ones). This began with Valentinian I, “who seems both to have overhauled the existing structure of grades and based the new policy on a systematic promotion of the imperial bureaucracy over the tradi- tional ‘senatorial’ offices.”  51 This strengthened bureaucracy had a better bargaining position vis-a-vis local magnates and peasants, and thus were able to extract more favorable tributes by strategic timing of “commutations,” which their subjects were powerless to resist. Officials would reject their official salary in kind at harvest time, and instead demand payment in currency later in the year, when the same stock of produce was much more valuable in price. This practice was the core of conflict between the military bureaucracy and local landowners, which was a persistent nexus of struggle throughout the period. The flip side of this process was the fact that the military was then forced to procure supplies on the market, and taxpayers were forced to acquire large sums of currency, both of which processes increased the role of commerce (transacted in gold) in the agricultural economy. 58-59
	* “In an economic sense, such transactions represented a huge extension of the market sector (whose existence they pre- supposed) though within the peculiarly late Roman structure of ‘fiscalized commercialism’, with fiscal and market forces interacting repeatedly, from the essential pressure that money taxes imposed on landowners to produce for the market to the different ways in which salaries, tax commutations, and state purchases tied in with market fluctuations.” Increasing monetarization of taxes also propelled monetarization of rural economy as a whole, down to payment of wages, land purchases, etc. (Are we back to the decline of slavery?)
* suggestion that the monetary system was so disfavorable to the masses that they must have been “held down in mute subjection through the sheer power of the late Roman state.” Political implications of the economic system. Litany of evidence showing that people who transacted in bronze had pitifully tiny savings.
* evidence from wearage of coins as well as the location of uncovered hoards suggests that gold had a very substantial circulation, and was not just confined to military/bureaucratic circuits.
* ”Wingate effect:” The dependence of agricultural producers on market prices means that “differences in the ability to space out foodgrains transactions over the seasons lead to a division of rural households into distinct types”. Poor farmers, who must sell immediately after harvest to make ends meet, will get the worst prices, since they are selling at the time of maximum supply, while wealthier households which can afford to wait until prices rise later in the year are at an advantage. In particular, abundant harvests were paradoxically the times of worst prices, and so many would refuse to sell - and since taxes increased in good years, those who were forced to sell at pitiful prices were those who needed money for immediate obligations, ie the most cash poor peasants.
* Conclusion: After Constantine, the state drove an increasing commercialization of the economy, in which the disappearance of silver made gold the dominant means of exchange. Production became more and more oriented to the market, with producer success determined to a large extent on access to gold.
* (My analysis) As a result state officials largely benefitted at the expense of poorer farmers, while even the wealthiest landowners seem to have been on the defensive, largely cut out of profits on speculations on the harvest cycle, and reduced to gaming it for the “second best” position of reasonable income late in the year. Economic success depended to a large extent on one’s placement in the annually predictable circulation of funds following the harvest cycle, tax schedule and cash disbursements; flexibility in timing and method of major transactions (especially buying/selling crops) and access to gold were the key factors.

Chapter 4

* Summarizes multiple accounts of the development of major estates in Byzantine Egypt. Hardy: The estates operate as “autopragic” units in “pagarchy,” ie they interacted with the market only for necessities that their own lands could not cover, and that they paid taxes directly to the imperial state and largely independent of mediating regional bureaucracies. Rouillard: The lowest level of smallholders largely formed economic communes and operated as a bloc; the largest states relied on hired labor, which was less organized than tenant farmers were; and there was a substantial “middle class” of farmers who acted as both lessees and lessors. Gascou: “Assimilation” of farmers into taxpayers, rent into taxes, and landowners into tax-collector/ administrators. However, Banaji is skeptical that this process shows the state’s dominance over the aristocracy; he suggests instead that taxation was “_abandoned”_ to local power holders, and that emperors like Justinian attempted to counteract this trend, but were unable to do so in the long term. In other words: the development of this framework was not merely a matter of imperial prerogative, and it should be analyzed from a local, social perspective as well as an institutional one.

chapter 5

* Describes 9 major groups which composed the Egyptian landholding elite, tracking their shifts over time. Offers in depth analysis of the 2 most important groups, the “old” civilian, Alexandria-based aristocracy, and the later “Byzantine aristocracy” of high officials combining administrative and agricultural power bases. Also describes the transitional form of the “middle bureaucracy,” appearing in the 5th century, then crossing a “watershed” into absolute dominance over the next few centuries.
	1. The Alexandrian Aristocracy - As of 200, the largest portion of land, and all of the very largest estates, were owned by a set of wealthy families based in Alexandria.  These families were prone to decline after only a few generations due to the division of estates among male heirs; they were thus not able to withstand powerful challengers in the long term. Apparently Carthage and Antioch exercised similar dominance over their hinterlands.
	2. Middle Bureaucracy - As of c. 350, a conspicuous class of military and civil officials was emerging, but did not yet hold much land. However, this group’s first steps into landed property at this time crystallized the conflict between the “Aurelii” (civilian elite, engaged in trade, crafts, farming) and “Flavii” (civil and military officials, granted the nomen Flavius by Constantine). “The nomen Flavius, starting as the designation of a professional status (and, at a deeper level, of a mental attitude, of inflexible devotion to the late imperial state in the personified form of the emperor), became, through the actions and fortunes of the Flavii, the expression of a social one.” A social division is created, classes polarize around it, and then those on one side of the division begin to dominate the other _as a group_. After this the medium sized landholders were often “middle class” administrators based in the nearest town or city, while the very biggest landholders were the new “aristocracy” of top-tier imperial officials.
	3. The Byzantine Aristocracy - “a class which fused the power of high-ranking officials with the affluence of big landowners in a combination characteristic of the late empire.” By 700, 56% of land was in the hands of a new “aristocracy” composed of top level administrative elites. This class seems to have steadily expanded its landholdings from 5th C on, in step with the intensification of its social privileges. Banaji thinks that increased political/economic  power came first, and land was acquired later to shore it up, rather than land being the basis for that increase in power. This group was mutually antagonistic with “middle-class” landowners rooted in local elite circles, and generally expanded at the expense of this set. “Thus it would be entirely appropriate to refer to the great estate owners of the eastern Mediterranean in late antiquity as a new aristocracy—a group distinguished from the local ‘aristocracy’ by the scale of its resources and by its roots in the political formation of the late empire, and from the Alexandrian aristocracy by its specific styles of management, a historical existence defined by deeper sta- bility, and its more durable, almost “corporate” character.” 129 Starts to become prominent from 450 or so, with the polarization of official ranks: _clarissima_ and other “common” honorary titles lose importance, while _illustris_ and a handful of others become both restricted to a few specific offices and indicative of greater social distinction.
		Concluding Summary: “The fifth century also saw the consolidation, throughout the east- ern provinces, of a Byzantine ‘new aristocracy’, drawn from the aristocracy of office and actively involved in the management of their estates, which were extensively irrigated, directly managed, and founded on the control of private settlements. These landown- ers were a driving force in the incipient expansion of Byzantine viticulture (impossible without irrigation). They were also, by the main part of the fifth century, a largely hereditary group.”

Chapter 8 - Wage Labor and Peasants

* _Georgoi_ a term which could refer to wage laborers, peasant tenants, or small landholders, although the last group increasingly tended to define themselves collectively as “village leaders” and taxpayers
	* the wealthiest georgoi of Byzantine Egypt were among the largest landholders of their villages and often resisted pressure by the aristocracy to incorporate those villages fully into private domains
	* “The village ‘community’ was essentially an expression of their social and fiscal solidarity, on the one hand against pagarchs who sought to break their dominance and incor- porate the village (kômê) into their own semi-private control, on the other, against the mass of the poorer village population which they controlled or even had conflicts with. By now, between the rich peasant (the Byzantine successor of the geôrgos Sakaon who had represented the koinon of his village) and the kt&twr or middling landowner there were largely imperceptible gradations, since the village oligarchy came to describe themselves no longer mainly as geôrgoi but in terms of the corporate identity they acquired, as prôtokômêtai (village leaders) and syntelestai, the people on whose collaboration the efficient functioning of the fiscal machinery chiefly depended. The prôtokômêtai were certainly in existence by the early fifth century...” 194
* Common varieties of seasonal wage labor: grape plucking; brickmaking; irrigation (working sāqiya?)
* the very existence of wage labor presupposes and would have extended the relationship between landholders and the market...
* Different forms of agriculture tended to involve different forms of labor; for example, only viticulture was usually sharecropper, whereas grain farming involved fixed advance payments.
