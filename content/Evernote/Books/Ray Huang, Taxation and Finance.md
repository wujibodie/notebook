Chapter 1:

* “One of the functions of the surveillance commissioner was to check and rectify abuses in tax administration. As an extension of this, some surveillance commissioners set up ground rules for collecting silver payments as service levy. In so doing they were virtually acting as tax legislators. Two outstanding surveillance commisioners who did this in the sixteenth century were Pan Jixun (1521-95) in Guangdong and Pang Shangpeng (_jinshi_ 1553) in Zhejiang, both of whom were instrumental in devising the Single Whip Reform." E27

Chapter 2

* Cites 梁方仲，彭信威 that silver in circulation increased from 30,000,000 taels to 250,000,000 taels over the course of the Ming, and says even the latter was not enough to cover demand. Attributes price fluctuations in agricultural goods to scarcity of silver. C93
* Believes the shift to silver was largely unplanned and beyond government control, and that administrators could not have predicted the consequences.
		
* General movement of tax silver was from south to north, but it largely returned south in payment for manufactures; Southeast as both Peru and Netherlands? 94
	* Consequence followed that the regions outside the silver circuit completely lacked silver and had a hard time making tax payments; see Gu Yanwu on 鄧州 and 菜州.
* Switch to silver as main currency reduced overall investment by encouraging hoarding in rich households

Chapter 3

* In 1559, the Shunde 御使+巡按 潘季馴 called for a simplified silver payment called the 均平銀, which combined a number of labor levies. To Huang this was a "concept not fundamentally different from that of the Single Whip reform."
	* This payment was collected from 3 sources; 1. A head levy on each _ding_; 2. A levy on "abstract _ding_," i.e. a tax on each 50 mou of land; 3. a "piggy-back" surcharge on each _shi_ of regular grain tax. This system was based on the 里甲 exactions and was kept separate from the 均搖.
* Huang believes that the tax system of the late Ming could have been "somewhat rationalized if silver had been formally declared the national fiscal standard and the remaining taxes in kind been altogether commuted." However, this was never done because it would have gone against vested interest at court, and because it would have required a separation of imperial and local income with a requisite increase in provincial administrative expenditures.
* In 1574, the magistrate 白棟 of Dongao, Shandong, collected a very simple one-whip tax with a single rate per mou and per ding. He was impeached due to "local dissatisfaction" but saved by the intervention of Zhang Juzheng.
* Claims that tax reforms along the southeast coast tended to coincide with the state of emergency caused by the _wokou_, since it gave local officials greater autonomy. 116
* First appearance of the term 一條鞭法 was in a 1531 memorial to the Jiajing emperor. In the 1570s Yu Shengxing argued that the Single Whip was a "general and not a specific term," which could refer to any of 1. shifting basis of taxation from household to property; 2. paying taxes in silver; 3. reapportioning surcharges uniformly among all taxpayers; 4. combining the service levy with land taxes. 118
* Mentions that Hai Rui was one of the "most vigorous supporters of the Single Whip Reform" 119.
* In many (populous and prosperous) portions of the southeast coast provinces, the service levy as commuted into silver was greater than the land tax, in many cases effectively doubling the taxes paid from commutation of grain alone. This often caused the perception of an excessive and sudden tax increase, even though it was only meant as a combination of existing taxes. 127-128
* In 1566 the governor of Huguang (who?) ordered all local units to implement the "Single Whip," with emphasis on the junyao account.

Chapter 4:

* Because tax reapportionments gave opportunities for local clerks and collectors to manipulate the numbers for their own gain, the Single Whip reform aimed to "freeze the accounts permanently." 144
* The huohao expense shifted from an extra expenditure to a source of income for collecting officials during the last quarter of the sixteenth century.
* The Single Whip reform also involved shifting tax delivery from the people to the government; in 1568 Shaoxing made an official responsible for major deliveries of tax silver. In the Yangtze delta tax cotton was also temporarily delivered by the government.
* Zhang Juzheng worked to remove the receiving personnel (such as Li Wei) who demanded bribes in exchange for accepting tax payments
* The picul of grain remained the unit of account for tax purposes, despite the fact that its value as commuted into silver varied widely; this made it impossible to get a view of overall tax figures from across the country
* In 1570, Hai Rui ordered the largest landowner in Nan Zhili, Xu Jie (The same Xu Jie??) to sell half of his land.
* Tax reform which reduced the burden on land caused price increases and lawsuits as landowners who had recently sold their land attempted to win it back (see Zhang Taisu on right to reclaim sold-off property?)
* On the other hand, when crop prices fell (as they did in the 1570s/80s after the _wokou_ were suppressed) the effective tax rate rose (due to the rate of commutation being fixed at an earlier value) and land prices declined as many people attempted to get rid of now less profitable land
* In the 16th century parts of Fujian and Jiangxi saw the rise of the 一田三主 tenureship system, where an original owner "sold" his land to a second party in exchange for a permanent payment from the land's annual proceeds. The second owner was responsible for both tax payments and payments to the first owner, but leased the land to a third tenant-cultivator. All three held their claims in perpetuity and passed them to descendants, who could not transfer any portion of the land to others. 161
	* Mentions that in Zhangzhou even the second, tax-paying owner often hired tax agents to actually pay taxes; this agent only received just enough grain to cover taxes plus a small fee, and often caused trouble for the government by being unable to pay up and having no resources which could be seized if he went into arrears.
	* Attempts were made in 1572 and 1581 (in Zhangzhou) to reduce this system to a single owner, or at least register all three owners as each being responsible for parts of the land tax, but none ever succeeded
	* Huang uses this system as an example of how taxpayers tended to win the complicated "hide-and-seek" with collectors; it was extremely difficult for officials to fully grasp and overcome these sorts of local complexities in their short terms

Chapter 7

* 5 phases of Ming financial management.
	* The fifth and final phase, 1587(!)-1600, is one of "financial irresponsibility," including the Wanli Emperor's sending of eunuchs to the provinces as tax-collectors. Huang also sees this as a clear abuse - is there anyone who's considered whether this policy had the potential to be constructive?
* "Fiscal Retrenchment under Zhang Juzheng"
	* "Chang Chü-cheng's insistence on rigorous auditing of fiscal accounts was unprecedented in the sixteenth century." Zhang's attempt to simplify and centralize accounts became the 萬曆會計錄，which was the basis for large portions of the revised 大明會典.
	* Because "governmental finance in the Ming was far more responsive to the power structure in Peking than to economic conditions in the empire," "Chang was forced to maintain his position for over ten years chiefly through the manipulation of personal relationships." Hence Zhang's relationships and correspondence are critical to understanding his administration.
* A list of people Zhang placed in important positions, 298: 凌雲翼，張學顏，王崇古，梁夢龍，潘季馴，張佳允，殷正茂，戚繼光，李成樑.
