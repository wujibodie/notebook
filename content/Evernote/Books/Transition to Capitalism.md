## Heller, Birth of Capitalism

Starting point: Dobb-Sweezy debate on whether long-range commerce was an “external mover” which pushed feudalism towards capitalism, and Brenner Debate about whether the class struggle which all sides agree contributed to breakdown of feudalism also explains rise of capitalism. Heller sides w critics of Brenner: small landholders formed part of nucleus of proto-capitalist class, along w some landlords, but against majority of aristocrats.

* “In accord with a forces of production approach, furthermore, we insist that the economic and political capacity of this class of proto-capitalists has to be understood in terms of the previous development of the forces of production during the Middle Ages.”

Dobbs: Breakdown of feudalism occurred by 1500 due to over exploitation of the peasantry in view of expanded noble consumption; at the same time growing distinction between rich, middling and poor peasants led to weakening of landlord power and growing vagrancy/migration to free cities, all of which cleared the way for a regime of wage labor.

Sweezy: Autarchic manor feudalism would have stagnated indefinitely had it not received an external push from the growth of commerce. Heller takes Dobbs’ side; trading towns and merchant capital were internal to feudalism and stimulated class contradictions within it. Petty production for trade expands even while nobility remain the ruling class.

Takahashi/Hilton: Analysis of feudalism in terms of basis social units, of which manor is dominant. Decline of feudalism is determined by breakdown of these categories, a process which is internal and not caused by shifts in commercial patterns. Class struggle over rent is key issue in this development; it stimulates greater exploitation and greater production up until a point where richer peasant class emerges along with landless wage laborers. Peasants as a whole resist landlords and succeed in reducing rent in 13th/14th centuries (where is Black Death here?). This only increases advantage of richer peasants, who merge with lesser nobility into a stratum of “most efficient producers” for commodity markets as well as key employers of wage labor. In this model towns served mostly to facilitate exchange w/in feudal order and did not have a key role in rupture towards capitalism; Heller points out indispensability of merchant capital in eventual entry of capital into production, even if that role postdated devs. mentioned above.

Hobsbawm picks up question of uneven development and role of exploitation in transition to capitalism, which Anderson and Wallerstein develop into theories about Japanese feudalism’s capitalist potential and the relations bw different world systems. On Japan, Takahashi argued that Japanese capitalism fundamentally emerged out of feudalism, and that the ground was fully prepared by the last century of the Tokugawa shogunate. Anderson demurs, arguing that integration into the world market is a prerequisite for capitalism and that this was impossible in Japan until the arrival of Commodore Perry.

* Anderson: Towns and commerce were critical in the shift towards a money economy and the breakdown of old relations bw nobility and peasants. The end of serfdom leads to strengthening of territorial monarchies as instruments of social control in defense of the threatened nobility. However, these states maintain political control at the expense of direct control over the economy, which becomes (in some cases) increasingly dominated by capitalist relations. “The political order remained feudal while the society under its aegis became more bourgeois.”

Brenner: Breakdown of feudalism is fully explained by class struggle over rent, which varies by region; in France it largely results in a free peasantry and a noble class which is supported by “centralized rent” in the form of taxes levied by the territorial state. In England the peasants won personal freedom but the nobility kept control of the land and began to rent it out for profit. “According to Brenner, ‘with the peasants’ failure to establish essentially freehold control over the land, the landlords were able to engross, consolidate and enclose, to create large farms and to lease them to capitalist tenants who could afford to make capital investments’.” Resulting division bw landlords, capitalist tenants, and hired laborers leads to development of agrarian capitalism. All actors become dependent on the market and old logic of social reproduction is replaced by one of profit-seeking and constant economic growth.
Bois: Brenner’s view is overly voluntaristic and does not consider shifts in the factors of production themselves.

## The Brenner Debate

Brenner 1974:
Begins by staking out the position that class conflict, rather than demographic trends or commercial growth, is the key factor in explaining the economic development of Europe from the Middle Ages to 1800. The processes by which class structures rise and fall are relatively autonomous, and other factors are the explananda to class conflict’s explanans.

Demographics: Key opponent is Ladurie, esp. Les Paysans de Languedoc. While notion of a bisecular “respiration” of demographics across Europe is of relevance to economic growth or decline, it cannot explain change in structures themselves. Ultimately, the relations between landlord and tenant only change through conflict between the two parties, and demographic pressures etc are merely part of the terrain where that conflict plays out. Neo-Malthusianism cannot explain why some economies broke out of a seemingly stagnant cycle while others did not. 

* Contra Postan, points out that a universal trend of population growth in the 13th century was associated with higher rents in some areas (England, parts of north and east France) but not others; Brenner concludes that the ultimate effect of population pressure depended on prior class arrangements. The decline after 1300 led to opposite outcomes in different areas: a free peasantry in the West and intensified serfdom in the East. And w rising pop after 1500, some regions stagnate while others develop self-sustaining growth.

Commercialization: Disagrees with the premise that serfdom could be dissolved by movement from labor to money rent; “serfdom denoted not merely, nor even primarily, labour dues as opposed to money dues, but, fundamentally, powerful landlord rights to arbitrary exactions and a greater or lesser degree of peasant unfreedom. Thus serfdom involved the landlord's ability to control his tenant's person, in particular his movements, so as to be able to determine the level of the rent in excess of custom or what might be dictated by the simple play of forces of supply and demand.”  (26)

Postan and Ladurie’s methods prevent them from posing the two basic questions about transition debate: 1. The end vs persistence of serfdom; 2. The development of free smallholders vs large landlords/tenant farmers. This in turn requires comparative analysis of 1. West and East Europe, 2. England and France.
A: Decline of serfdom. Demographic crises were inherent to serfdom, but only as a result of class dynamics which made it unlikely for landlords and impossible for peasant to invest in animals and techniques->long term decline in productivity. Fate of serfdom after 1300 can only be explained by varying results of peasant wars; Western peasants won land rights while transelbian ones were beaten lower than before. Key differential factor is strength of peasants vis-a-vis nobles, which has to do with numbers, solidarity, organization, political alliances (w towns and monarchy), etc.

* “The relative absence of village solidarity in the east, despite the formally similar character of village settlement (the so-called "Germanic" type), appears to have been connected with the entire evolution of the region as a colonial society - its relatively late for- mation, the "rational" and "artificial" character of its settlement, and especially the leadership of the landlords in the colonizing pro- cess.”
* Only England saw the development of landlord/capitalist tenant/wage laborer relations out of medieval class conflict, and thus only England saw agrarian capitalism w consistent capital investment and growth in efficiency per unit labor. This also left England immune to the general crisis of the 17th C, which saw Malthusian collapse elsewhere.

Postan and Hatcher
Demographic historians are not simplistic “malthusians” nor do they think demographics are exclusive determinant of historical change. East European serfdom postdated population collapse, contra Brenner. In West, unevenness of feudal form means that many places had free peasants from early on, and these were not immune to demographic pressure or to general economic trends (Lincolnshire, Normandy, Paris). Further, Brenner cannot show that lords became stronger vis-a-vis serfs at times when rents increased; nor were villeins as helpless as Brenner claims. Finally, landed nobles did not invest in productivity bc only marginal technological improvements existed, and bc extension of estate lands was in almost all cases the preferable use of money.

Croot and Parker
Appreciate Brenner’s emphases, but dispute his characterization of peasant position in both England and France. English peasants consistently improved productivity in response to rising prices. Real distinction bw England and France in 16th-18th Cs is differentiation among peasants into yeomen and landless laborers in E, compared to universal “free” peasant immiseration + growth of large noble and ecclesiastical estates in F. Thus England’s economic success relies on middle tier of farmer-landholders while France is held back by state and landlord exactions on so-called free peasants.

* Ultimately, Brenner’s “basic contention that the impact of particular phenomena such as demographic change can produce radically different results depending on the character of the class structure is acceptable. But unless he wishes to take the unlikely step of claim- ing that the conjuncture of circumstances and relatively short-term phenomena cannot affect in any significant way the pre-existing structure, his recognition of their interaction, far from demonstrat- ing the autonomous nature of the evolution of class structures, actually calls it into question.” 90 Brenner, too, raises one transcendent factor above all others and assimilates seemingly opposed causal relationships into the “internal” dynamic of class conflict.

Wunder
Brenner’s comparison of West and East German peasants falls prey to the “Hohenzollernlegende,” and thus projects early modern class relations onto the Middle Ages. East German villages had unique hereditary position of _Schulz,_ a larger landholder, judge, and spokesman who mediated bw peasants and lord. Thus the rise of forced labor rents in east Germany was not simply a product of weaker communal organization. _Political_, not only class, power is a key variable; the Western princedoms saw state support of peasantry against nobility, but eventually Prussia also took on policies of _bauernschutz_ vs the Junkers. The rise of the Junkers itself thus cannot be explained without reference to patterns of political power, involving the state along with noble, peasant, and urban organizations.

* What is role of competition between states?

Ladurie
“Neo-Malthusianism” is not simply demographic determinism; “On the contrary, it incorporates it, quite simply, by taking care to stress the role of concrete social groups (landowners, farmers, agricultural workers and the like) over and above abstract economic categories (ground rent, business profits, wages).” Mentions the work of Wilhelm Abel, also cited by Wunder. Thinks Brenner conflates political and propertied power; understates complexity of factors in Neo-Malthusian model and its openness to modifications in its variables (ie soil fertility may or may not matter); and fails to assign appropriate importance to epidemics, especially w claim that 14th century demo. decline was result of seigneurial abuses and not the Black Death. Sees population rise and fall itself as having clear effect on peasant QOL (esp. land rents) irrespective of status of serfdom. Questions the idea that the peasants had to be crushed for capitalism to emerge; while this is what happened in England, powerful peasant families did well in transitions in Holland, northern France, northern Italy, and Catalonia.

Bois
Likes Brenner’s attack on “Neo-Malthusian orthodoxy” (despite Ladurie claiming that Bois’ work validates the paradigm!); demographics can in no way account for the development of capitalism, and class relations must thus come in to account for it. However, disputes Brenner’s elevation of the monarchic state’s role as the single key factor distinguishing France and England; sees Brenner as having a weakness for starting at hypertheoretical level and only then moving on to study concrete historical conditions. “The starting-point is the fundamental principle of historical materialism: the driving role of the class struggle. And he sets out to verify it through decreasing abstraction, by comparing it with the available empirical data. This is characteristic of a system of closed thought, where ideology triumphs over scientific rationalism.”

Bois’ revised summary starts with the crisis of feudalism: “In the first place, in the feudal system the rate of seigneurial levies shows a tendency to fall which originates in the structural con- tradiction of small-scale production and large-scale property. When economic expansion draws to an end (around the middle of the thir- teenth century) the fall in the rate of levy is no longer offset by the establishment of new tenures, with the result that seigneurial income in its turn tends to decrease. The crisis of the feudal system is bound up with this phenomenon: the dominant class does not succeed in maintaining the economic basis of its hegemony. This takes place against a background of social and political confron- tation the results of which are, on the one hand, the strengthening of the middling peasantry and, on the other, the hypertrophy of the machinery of the state (royal absolutism). The consequence is a radical rearrangement of the production relationship, characterized by the addition to direct seigneurial levies of a centralized levy organized by the royal administration to the almost exclusive advan- tage of the seigneurial class.” Further, long-term economic trends w/in this decline favor the expansion of production along with increased pauperization of day laborers; and when rents had fallen low enough the lords tended to expand their demesnes by swallowing smallholdings and to hire wage labor.  **Thus it is differences in feudal production, not social or political differences, which determines different fates of European regions.** (111-112)

* Northern French feudalism developed earlier than elsewhere, and thus hit its ceiling more forcefully in the 13th-15th centuries. Therefore the period of recovery also saw less total restructuring of feudal relations than in England, where economic malaise poised between serfdom and a free peasantry found a solution in commerce, especially with the richer feudal society of France.
* “Variations in both the age and the degree of maturity of the feudal system in one place as com- pared with another probably play a leading and certainly a very complex role in the rhythms which then affect the emergence and development of the capitalist structures.15 This is moreover a phenomenon which is found again mutatis mutandis in the evolution of contemporary capitalist societies.” 115

Conclusion: Brenner founders on an ideological and voluntaristic notion of class conflict which leaves no room for the factors of production or productivity of labor, which to Bois should be the basis of historical materialist method. Movement from detailed source research to abstraction and generalization, rather than abstraction downwards from theory to evidence. Work of Ladurie et al should be criticized for inattention to social factors and to structure of feudalism, but their findings are valid and should be integrated into an eventual “general model” of history, though stripped of ideological content.

## Heller (again)

Continuation of Brenner debate via Harman, Hilton, and Byres. Harman criticizes Brenner for lack of focus on changes in the control over forces of production, which leads him to overemphasis on landlord power as opposed to group of enterprising protocapitalists who emerge out of middling farmers and artisans. Byres stresses the role of social differentiation among the peasantry. A stratum of rich peasants increasingly separated itself from the mass of farm laborers and by the 16th century was prepared to take advantage of the dispossession of the lowest tier of peasants by buying land and hiring labor, and to turn their surplus earnings into investments=class of proto-capitalists in control of large portion of rural production.
