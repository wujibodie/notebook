Class 2: Emplotment in India. Natyasatra. 

* "The \[assemblage of\] acts which are fabricated with a view to (lit, by reason of) the attainment of \[some particular\] result, is to be known as the Principal Plot. \[Acts\] other than those constitute the Subsidiary Plot.” In particular, the Primary Plot will show the Heroes’ process of accomplishing some particular end (the result). 
* This process has five parts (“Junctures”):  "They are : Beginning Prarambha), Effort (prayatna), Possibility of Attainment (prapti-sambhara), Certainty of Attainment (niyata phala-prapti) and Attainment of the Result (phala-prapti).” “Their Fruition relates to _dharma, k__ama__,_ and _artha__."_
	* _Beginning: "_That part of the play (lit composition) which merely records eagerness about the final attainment of the result with reference to the Germ (bija)."
	* _Effort:_ Heroes’ striving towards the Result when it is not in view, and further eagerness. 
	* _Possibility of Attainment:_ The attainment of the object begins to be slightly suggested by an idea. 
	* _Certainty of Attainment:_ One visualizes ideally a sure attainment of the Result. 
	* Attainment of the Result: The intended result appears in full at the end of events and corresponds to them. 
* "If one Juncture is to be omitted then the fourth one goes; in case of an omission of two Junctures, the third and the fourth are to be left out, and in case three are to be omitted, the second, the third and the fourth should be given up.” But a Subsidiary Plot can appear at any time, and showing any number of Junctures.
* Each juncture makes use of “limbs,” which seem something like tropes, and which "
	* "Expressing the desired object, non-omission of any essential item in the Plot, accession to feeling in production, concealment of objects to be concealed, telling tales of surprise and disclosing things to be disclosed are the sixfold needs of the Limbs as described in the Sastra."
* There are 64 Limbs, each specific to their proper Junctures.
* An ideal Nataka: “The playwright should write a Nataka having \[different\] Styles and minor Limbs, Episode Indication, Explanatory Devices arising from the five stages, having five Junctures, twenty-one Alternative Junctures, sixty-four Limbs, thirty-six _laksanas,_ Gunas (excellence) and figures of speech, many Sentiments, topics of many enjoyment, exalted speeches, characters of great people, description of good conduct, and it should be popular, well knit in its Junctures, easy for production \[on stage\], composed with soft words and capable fo giving pleasure.” 
	* “The Nataka is to be composed as to include all States, Sentiments, inclination to all deeds, and the various conditions \[of men and nature\]."
	* One is to construct a Nataka only after observing the human character, strength and weakness of me their mode of enjoyment and reasoning.
* Finishes by enjoining the playwright to use simple and pleasant words, since as the world deteriorates future audiences will have less and less learning and intellect.
* In class, coupled with Ricoeur’s treatment of emplotment in Aristotle’s _Poetics._ 
* Aristotle divided tragedy into six parts: Plot, Character, Spectacle, Thought, Language, and Melody. 
	* The “what,” or object of representation: Plot, Character, Thought
	* The “by which” or means: Language, Melody
	* The “how” or mode: Spectacle
	* Within this hierarchy Plot comes first,  since Plot imitates real actions (mimesis praxeos), whereas agents are represented only as a means of representing action. Thus plot is the “end” or even “soul” of tragedy, and thus is warranted the line “The imitation of action is the Plot.” The mimesis of a play is that play’s muthos; it is by means of emplotment, arranging of events, that action is represented.
