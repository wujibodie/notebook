Chapter One: The Formation and Breakup of the Camphor Procurement System 
- The camphor procurement office grew out of the naval procurement of lumber. The "foreman" managing camphor lumber and crystals was a deputy of the Daotai (titled 軍工匠首). Danshui foremen had rights to purchase camphor from the northern stands and thus to buy low sell high, while the Daotai profited by selling foreman status (陋規). 54
	- There were 6 such foremen on Taiwan, in Tainan, Danshui, Fengshan, Jiayi, Zhanghua, and Yilan. 57 
- "Plagued by rampant smuggling that cut into his profits and hampered his ability to fulfill his services to the Qing government, the Danshui foreman established new depots for the collection of camphor in key coastal ports in the 1850s and 1860s. In addition to a main depot in Mengjia (Taipei), subsidiary depots were located in Qiannan, Zhonggang, Houlong, and Wuqi. Up to 20 camphormen (laoding) were attached to some of the depots to patrol the surrounding area and apprehend smugglers." 64
	- Mengjia was the main depot for camphor collection in northern Taiwan, and in 1863 it was changed to specifically a collection center for camphor oil/crystals rather than general "materials" (from 料管 to 腦管). This is how Milisch would have encountered it. (Crucial to track shift from camphor lumber to essential camphor as main product.)
- In the 1860s camphor forests were regulated as military necessities, but there was no conception of a state "monopoly" in camphor commerce: "Camphor production and trade outside the foreman’s supervision was not “illicit” because it violated a “monopoly right.” Instead, officials saw it as an activity that destroyed trees valuable for warship lumber and that jeopardized the foreman’s income and the state’s military readiness." 66
- Tait & Co and Jardine & Matheson both began purchasing camphor on Taiwan via Chinese agents in 1856. 71
- Between 1861-5 British firms collected camphor along the coast in schooners, before it was pointed out that this violated the Nanjing Treaty's restriction of free foreign shipping to treaty ports; thereafter they were restricted to Danshui, causing rapid price rises in 1866/7 (just when Milisch arrived...). 75
- In October 1867, the J&M agent John Dodd, who wrote that he considered the camphor monopoly "illegal," requested vice-consul Holt's intervention when his shipment of camphor was seized by the Daotai. 
- Argues that after the camphor procurement system was abolished in 1868, Chinese merchants were best positioned to take advantage of the new, freer trade in camphor, and Europeans disappeared until the development of industrial uses after 1886 brought them back to the camphor trade. 86
- "As a result, foreign merchant capital in the 1870s, while maintaining its control of general shipping from the treaty ports, either lost or abandoned the camphor trade to Chinese merchants shipping by junk out of coastal ports (Suao [in Yilan], Mengjia [Taipei], Huwei [Danshui], Zhuqian, Xiangshan, Yanshui, Zhonggang, Houlong, Tunxiao, Wuqi, and Lugang) or by steamship out of Danshui and Dagou. The amount of camphor taken to Taiwan’s treaty ports by transit pass, an index of direct foreign involvement in the trade, sank in the late 1870s from 2,027 piculs in 1877, to 741 in 1878, and 395 in 1879." 91
- Almost immediately after Liu Mingchuan became governor, Lin Chaodong and Lin Weiyan proposed direct government control of camphor production to fund pacification-and-reclamation work.
