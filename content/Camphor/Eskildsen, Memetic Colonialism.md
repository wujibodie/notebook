- "Second, it will explain the specific colonial logic of bringing civilization to the "savages" of Taiwan, a legalistic definition of political jurisdiction that the Japanese government used as a way of justifying the colonization of Taiwan." 389
- "A legalistic explanation of civilization and savagery informed the colonial logic that the Japanese government employed during the Taiwan Expedition, and it derived from a theory of sovereignty and jurisdiction devised by Charles LeGendre during the years he spent as American consul in Amoy." 394
- "During his negotiations with the Chinese, LeGendre argued that, under the terms of the Treaty of Tianjin (1858), an "unequal treaty" that gave Western powers unreciprocated legal and economic rights in China, the Chinese government had an obligation to punish the acts of the aborigines. Moreover, he argued from international law that, unless China sustained the exercise of its jurisdiction over the aboriginal territory... it could not claim sovereignty over the territory. In an attempt to motivate the Chinese to action, LeGendre repeatedly raised the threat that unless China exercised effective jurisdiction over the territory, a foreign power would colonize it." 395
- Cites Yen for the Qing side of the response, so would be better for me to cite her directly. But his point is that the Qing claimed "sovereignty" over the whole island of Taiwan but disavowed legal jurisdiction over the aborigines of Taiwan.
- In 1872, LeGendre stopped at Yokohama after leaving his consular post in Amoy, and was hired as an advisor by the Japanese foreign minister Soejima Taneomi. LeGendre prepared reports proposing a Japanese annexation of eastern Taiwan on the basis that the Qing had no sovereignty there as long as they failed to discipline the natives. 
- In the end, Japan shelved the colonization plan, but only after doing extensive research and planning, and only because they feared war with China, not because they had doubts that "civilizing" the savages of Eastern Taiwan would bring claim to legal jurisdiction.
