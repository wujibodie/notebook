
- Between 1868 and 1895, sugar, tea, and camphor made up 94% of Taiwan's exports. On average tea represented 53.5%, sugar 36%, and camphor under 4%, but camphor was a larger portion of exports before 1870 and after 1890 (with almost no exports in the 1880s). 
	- The year with the highest total camphor exports was 1894, when 833,243 taels worth of camphor exports were registered by customs, or 11.5% of Taiwan's total exports. Table of export statistics on page 4. 
	- Points out that Taiwan was probably the region of China with the highest per capita export income (3.33 taels/person on average); Fujian as a whole was second only to Guangdong among full provinces. 
		- Thinks this export reliance is partially explained by the fact that Qing Taiwan was already in a relationship of highly specialized production for the mainland market, importing almost all manufactures while exporting rice and sugar. 
		- Cites a passage in the 1696 Taiwan Fuzhi which claims that Taiwan's farmers were responsive to market conditions, quickly shifting from rice to sugar or back when relative prices changed. Lin sees this as distinguishing Taiwanese from Fujianese farmers, who (citing Rawski) often avoided cash crops in favor of subsistence crops. 
		- After 1860, the most important market for Taiwan was European, not mainland Chinese, and the shift in exports to tea, sugar, and camphor reflected that demand. As a result, Lin proposes that studying the Western influence on the techniques, capitalization, and transport of these export sectors is a way to analyze the impact of Western trade on Taiwan's economic structure as a whole. 14
	- Primary sources: Maritime Customs Report and the reports of the English consuls. 
- Chapter One: Analysis of Export Markets
	- Between the years of 1878 and 1892 Japan surpassed Taiwan as an exporter of camphor, in part due to difficulties in production in Taiwan's interior, and in part because Japanese camphor was easier to crystallize and so more suited to the medical market; Taiwan recovered when celluloid manufacturing, which does not require crystalline camphor, became the main source of demand. 34
	- In the 1890s, 93.4% of Taiwan's camphor exports headed to Hong Kong for transshipment to the West, with only 6.6% going to mainland China via Xiamen. 
	- Note that the price of camphor also went up dramatically after 1890, from averaging 5-10 taels/dan to 15 in 1890 and 24 in 1895. 
		- (Although also note that the value of the maritime standard tael decreased relative to most western currencies, but by a smaller proportion than camphor's price rise - from 1.6 gold dollars to .8 between 1869 and 1895.)
	- Note that price differences mean that the jump in camphor exports by weight is much less dramatic. Per chart 2.3 on p. 48, it was only in 93 and 94 that camphor exports were really much larger (>2x) than in the years 67-72, and it was only in the 1880s that exports dropped to a truly low level. 
	- Chart of factors affecting the export of camphor on 55-56. Notable moments: 
		- Camphor prices in Hong Kong especially low in 1864; the "camphor treaty" signed in 1868 led to an immediate tripling of exports, but demand cooled in 1869; conflict between the indigenes and Hakka laborers intensified in 1875 leading to a 40% drop (Danshui customs report 1875); in 1879 the English consul blamed the fact that *local merchants bought Western goods with camphor rather than silver for a loss in business of the major exporting merchants* (suggestion that small-scale, unregistered camphor exports were cutting into exports as registered by customs?); the crash of the early 1880s was explained by the exhaustion of available camphor forests while war with the natives made further expansion difficult; prices began to rise again in 1889, while Liu Mingchuan's camphor monopoly was abolished in 1890. 
- Chapter Two: Analysis of Production
	- Nice map of tea, sugar, and camphor regions on 58. Camphor only grows in economically significant quantities in the mountains in the northern half of the island. By 1860 the low-lying regions had been entirely deforested and camphor stands were only exploited on the boundaries between Han and "Fan." 64
		- Because conflict with the natives was more serious in the northern camphor regions, camphor production in the center increased in prominence after 1875. Liu Mingchuan created two camphor offices, one in Dakekan (Daxi in Taoyuan) in the north and one in Zhanghua in the south; in 1890 the northern route produced 3,300 dan while the southern office collected 5,700 dan. 66
		- In the 1890s camphor production was growing most quickly in the south, around Ji-ji, Douliu, and Jiayi. 
	- In 1823 conflict over camphor trees broke out in Yilan between military artisans and private artisan households, and there was some camphor production there for the entire 19th century. 
	- In her brief discussion of production techniques, Lin says that before the Japanese occupation no one bothered to invest even in replanting camphor trees that had been cut, and so there were "no production techniques to speak of" besides simply chopping trees. 74 
		- Worth noting though that as Lin says elsewhere camphor clearcutting was normally done as a first stage in settling of a frontier, and places where camphor stands had been fully cleared would then be cultivated with more economically valuable goods, first rice and then especially tea (suitable for similar environments).
