Huang Fusan, in 中國海洋發展史論文集1
- Describes Milisch & Co. as a “Prussian” company which began acting as an agent of Jardine Matheson & Co. from October, 1865. They closed and left Taiwan due to debts in 1870. 
- Huang’s analysis is based on the Jardine Matheson archives and emphasizes the practical economic reasons for Milisch’s failure, without reference to the political context. The four main problems he notes are losses to their merchandise, failed payments in Chinese merchant drafts, bad loans to Chinese merchants, and difficulties with shroffs. 
1. Milisch & Co. primarily imported opium and textiles in exchange for camphor, and they took losses on each of these over the relevant period. For example, when Jardine Matheson steamers delivered opium chests to Milisch at Danshui, the latter found that some of the merchandise he had paid for was missing. The same happened with deliveries of textiles. On the other end, camphor often lost weight in delivery due to poor manufacturing or improper packaging/handling, and Chinese camphor merchants sometimes mixed in fake product. 
2. Towards the end of his time on Taiwan Milisch attempted to pay JM with “native drafts” from Taiwanese merchants which were not accepted by merchants in Fuzhou or Ningbo; despite his apologies this led JM to investigate Milisch’s finances, which led to his admission of bankruptcy. 
3. Vague treatment of the notion that Western firms’ loan terms were unusually good in the Chinese context, and so people were eager to lend from them, but often failed to repay; Milisch mentioned such a situation when explaining his troubles to JM. 
4. Milisch was repeatedly tricked by dishonest shroffs and thus lost good deals of silver. Huang says he covers this topic in his other articles. 
- The major competitor to Milisch was Dent & Co., which was based in Hong Kong but had agents (John Dodd) on Taiwan who traded camphor for opium, sometimes bought from Elles & Co. in Xiamen. Other firms also entered the market occasionally, such as Tait & Co., and there is a reference to a “Lassen [?] & Hagen” who approached JM in March 1866 claiming to have just started a business in Danshui and Jilong — certainly Karl Christian Hagen. 
	- Could the consulate situation, which placed Hagen in the south, have partially come out of a negotiation between the two Hamburgers to separate their spheres of interest?
- After the camphor monopoly was lifted more foreign firms became interested in the trade, with Tait and Co. in particular sending steamers directly from Xiamen to Taiwan, and then to Hong Kong and New York. 
- Competition from Chinese merchants was also fierce; in particular Milisch repeatedly complained that one of the “camphor monopolists” empowered by the Daotai through 1868 was also a major importer of opium, which he arranged to buy directly from Hong Kong steamships.
	- Notes the name of two steamships selling directly to the “camphor monopolist”: Glendover and Finke. Would be interesting to learn who these ships belonged to. 
	- Milisch complained that his warehouse at Mengjia (Wanhua) was never able to hold a majority of the opium in the market; Chinese merchants always had larger stores.
- Conclusion takes Milisch’s failure as an example which illustrates that foreign firms did not always hold a dominant position in the Chinese market and could indeed be outcompeted by Chinese merchants, which in turn is evidence that the forcible opening of China to the world market ultimately benefitted Taiwan’s economy and its native bourgeoise. I wonder how Huang’s conclusions would change knowing the political side of Milisch’s failure… 



Milisch‘s consular report from 1867:
Vor kurzem wurde von dem hiesigen Zollcommissär eine Uebersicht des Handels der Häfen Tamsui und Kilung, auf der Insel Formosa, veröffentlicht und erlaube ich mir nunmehr, Eu. Hochwohlgeborenen ein Exemplar derselben unter Separat-Couvert zur Büchergast zugängig zu machen. Von Lübeckischen Schiffen haben keine hiesige Gewässer besucht. 
Ein Freunschaft-, Schiffahrt- & Handelsvertrag mit China wird nur in sofern betrachtet, wo es gelte Zoll und andere Gebühren einzucassieren: sonstige Verpflichtungen, welche die Kaiserliche Regierung übernommen, werden so viel wie möglich von den Beamten umgangen. 
Solches läßt eine Representation des Interesse der Hansestädte zu Pekin, sehr wünschenswert erscheinen, und erlaube ich mir hierdurch die Anfrage, ob ich den Beistand des Gesandten der M. Des Königs von Preussen zu Pekin, ansprechen kann. 
Der Import-Handel, von Opium und den Erzeugnissen Anderer Länder, wird hauptsächlich in fremder Schiffen betrieben. Produkte des Festlandes Chinas werden größtenteils in Junken zugeführt. Der Export-Handel, ist fast ausschließlich in Chinesischen Händen und bedienen sich dieselben großtentheils ihrer eigenen Fahrzeuge. Die Haupterzeignisse Nord Formosas sind Reis, flüssiger Indigo, kaufthee, Kampher, und andere Hart-Hölzer. Camphor (?) letztgenannten Artikel, wird den Stipulationen des Handelsvertrages zuwieder, von den hiesigen Representatnen der Chinesischen Regierung Monopolisirt und have ich dem gemäß meinen Protest eingerichtet, welcher bis jetzt ohne Erfolg geblieben. Von Kelung werden fast ausschließlich Kohlen exportiert, welche in der nächsten Umgebung des Hafens in großer Menge vorhanden. 
Der größte Theil der Insel ist gebirgig und dicht bewaldet u fast ausschließlich in den Händen der Ureinwohner Formosas - selbe werden indessen mit jedem Jahre mehr und mehr von der Chinesen ins Innre der Berge verdrängten die Wälden werden zerstört um Indigo-Plantagen Platz zu machen. 
Die Flüsse sind sehr sucht, indessen bis weit zwischen den Bergen, für kleine Fahrzeuge schiffbar. Die Ureinwohner stehen in Kürzerform und Gesischtsbildung über die Chinesen, haben indessen, soweit man bis jetzt hat erfahren können, keine Schriftzeichen in denen sie sich ausdrücken können; von ihren Sitten und Gebrauchen ist wenig bekannt. Der Charakter die hiesigen Chinesen, ist derselbe wie auf dem Festlande Chinas.
Die Einfahrt zum Hafen vom Tamsui ist von einer Barre behindert und sind somit nur flach gebaute Schiffe praktisch. Der Hafen von Kilung hat sehr tiefes Wasser und können die größten Schiffe unbehindert einlaufen. 
Indem ich mir noch erlaubte, Eu. Hochwohlgeboren Aufmerksamkeit auf beigebende Statistiks hinzulenken, aus welchen weiter Information über hiesigen Handel, in fremden Schiffen zu ersehen, benutze ich mit Vergnügen diesen Anlaß, Sie meiner Hochachtungsvollen Ergebenheit zu versichern. 
Hanseatischer Consulat. 
James Milisch, Consul, Tamsui, 31ten August 1867.

Consular Reports:
[1870 British report](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=wu.89097360416&seq=94) notes that 16 North German ships sailed from Tamsui, as opposed to 32 British ones, although the total tonnage of cargo was only about 1/3rd of the British total. Opium made up about 3/4s of imports into Tamsui by weight, while camphor (29,000 sterling) accounted for 1/5th of exports by value, about the same as rice and half of tea. 
- Note that a lijin on camphor was instituted at the end of 1870, which caused foreign merchants to send agents into the interior to buy camphor rather than camphor merchants trekking to Tamsui; "the course of trade was considerably disturbed." The lijin was dropped due to protests in 1871. 84
[69-70 Consular report](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=uc1.a0000745307&seq=83) from Hewlett in Taiwan-fu notes that the coastal carrying trade is dominated by North German vessels due to their light and quick design, but the actual cargoes are owned by British merchants. 75
- This point is repeated, though with more concern, in the '70 report above. 
- "In spite of the promises held out at the end of 1868, when free trade in Camphor was proclaimed, the export of that article in 1869 has only reached the amount of 3,393L against 2,195L in the previous year. Local seizures, intimidation and persecution of Chinese agents, with many other obstacles placed in the way of British merchants by the native officials, have been of so frequent occurrence as to have almost amounted to a revival of the monopoly, the abolition of which represents an annual loss to the local Government of $60,000. Until these obstacles are removed British merchants will not feel inclined to embark capital in a trade subject to such arbitrary and oppressive interruptions." 76
- William Gregory's letter from Tamsui, December 29, 1870:
	- "Of the events of the year I may mention the failure of the Ta-lamo project. This was a scheme for the establishment of a little colony or settlement for agricultural or other purposes, at a place called Ta-lam-o in the wild Aborigines' territory, on the East Coast of Formosa, a few miles below Saw-o-Bay. The subordinate settlers were Kabaran people, or "Pepoes," Aborigines, but subject to Chinese rule. The immediate leader was a British subject, and he was supported with funds, &c., by a non-British mercantile resident. The settlement was commenced in 1868. The project was a hazardous one, and was not well conducted. It would probably have failed if it had been let alone, but it was brought to a somewhat speedier end by the interposition of authority. In October 1869, the British subject above-mentioned (who had already become bankrupt), took his final departure from the place, with his people, to the number of thirty-four.
