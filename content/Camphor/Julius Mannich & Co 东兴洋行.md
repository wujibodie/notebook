

Bits and Pieces
- Early on Mannich owned a wooden schooner, the *Auguste Reimers*, for which he successfully applied for the right to fly a German flag in 1876. https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=uiug.30112100054755&seq=594&q1=mannich; the captain was listed in 1879 as one A. Temsen of Flensburg: https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=uiug.30112068903720&seq=152&q1=mannich&view=2up. 
- 	In the 1876 Reichsministerialblatt, Mannich is said to be "im Königreich Preußen heimatsberechtigten" and living in Takao; he purchased the ship, which was built in Germany but had been sailing under a Chinese flag, in Takao, and applied for a German flag from the consulate in Amoy.
- A death certificate of a Julius Mannich, who passed away in Charlottenburg, Berlin at age 67 in 1915, might fit Mannich. It gives his religion as Catholic and his birthplace as Rößel, an East Prussian town near Königsberg. He would have been born in 1848, and thus have been 28 when he first appears in the records. It is noted that nothing is known of his parents.
	- Rößel was part of the Polish region of Warmia (Ermland) until the 1773 partition, and as a result was a largely Catholic region with religious autonomy, but administered under Königsberg. It is quite likely he was ethnically Polish. 
- There is a death certificate for Mannich's wife Anna Mannich in 1899 in Charlottenburg; Mannich's occupation is "Kaufmann." Anna was the 40 year old daughter of a Hamburg merchant. 
- Mannich then remarried in 1906, to Marie Emilie Elise Fiedler, a 35 year old from Neumark. Here his title was given as "fabrikdirektor," and his home was said to be in Fürth. His father was "Geschäftskommissär Joseph Johannes Mannich," and his mother was born Fornaçon, a name for French refugees who settled in East Prussia; they were listed as residing in Königsberg. Appended to the certificate is a note that a divorce was approved in 1912, with Mannich signing an order to forbid his ex-wife from using his surname.

Herr Mannich, der selbst Touren ins Innere gemacht, nahm den lebhaftesten Antheil an meinem Unternehmen und war mir mit seinen praktischen Rathschlägen von unschätzbarem Werth, besonders in der wichtigsten Frage, der Wahl der Geschenke, welche mir bei den Eingeborenen Eingang verschaffen sollten. Da ich mein Gepäck möglichst beschränkte, so brauchte ich nur zwei Kuli als Packträger (Pferde oder Esel giebt es hier nicht); beide waren mir von einem Missionär als zuverlässige Burschen empfohlen und bewährten sich in der That als solche. - Mannich gave a [brief response to a questionnaire](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=nyp.33433008900338&seq=339&q1=julius+mannich) on opium-smoking in Formosa sent to him by the British Consul Hunt. His full response: "Mr. Mannich, Taiwanfoo, February 23, 1894 .
1.  Opium is commonly consumed by the Chinese in Formosa .
2. I consider that one - half of the male populace of Formosa smoke opium. I do not know of opium being smoked by children or women .
3. I do not know of bad effects of opium being smoked . To a certain class of Chinese ( such as car- riers , chair - bearers , and rice farmers ' hands ) I consider opium smoking of beneficial effect physically , as the moderate use of the drug assists them to overcome the hard work which their occupation on the road and ricefield entails upon them .
4. As far as I know , consumers in Formosa smoke only .
5. I cannot say , since I met no injurious cases .
6. It is not correct to say that there cannot be such a thing as moderation in the consumption of opium . During my 25 years' residence in China I met but Chinese who smoked moderately .
7. Almost all outdoor labourers and most of the merchants smoke opium without the slightest effect on their efficiency and calling.
8. If the opium-smokers of Formosa were but to consume the fourth part in value of alcohol, I think they would all become incurable drunkards .
9. Opium - smoking is not condemned by Chinese as degrading .
10. I have heard of Chinese breaking themselves of the opium habit .
11. If the supply of Indian opium were to be cut off , Formosa would at first consume less , owing to the rise in price of Persian opium , but the greater im- port of native opium would soon lower the price of Persian opium , which has long ago driven the Indian opium from the Formosa market .
12. I do not know of Europeans ever having contracted the habit of opium-smoking.
13. Well-to-do  Chinese of sedentary habits and occupations smoke opium as freely as Europeans smoke tobacco . Outdoor labourers will smoke opium to refresh themselves , and I have seen frequently chairbearers stagger quite exhausted under their load in a broiling sun, and after a pipe or two of opium resume their load quite refreshed. In all serious and painful sicknesses Chinese doctors prescribe the use of an opium pipe , so there is a general belief among Chinese that opium - smoking is the best prophylactic against any illness.
14. Not that I know of .
15. The Chinese in Formosa ridicule the idea of England not allowing the Indian or other opium to come here , and if a patriotic ( ? ) Chinaman was to wish such a thing , it would be prompted by the simple desire of seeing the native opium come to the front .
16. I can talk of Formosa only , and here no native opium is being grown , and very little Indian opium is consumed .
17. a) Consumption would not materially decrease , since Persia would treble its cultivation ; and
	b) Native opium would be grown much more on the mainland .
	 c)The consumption of native - grown opium will enrich the provincial authorities , but lessen the revenue collected by the foreign customs for the central Government 
18. No.
19. I cannot answer this question .
20. As a general remark , I wish to add my solemn conviction that opium - smoking as it is generally indulged by Chinese does not do harm to the people."

Clemente 1897: "**Various Europeans have been robbed just like ourselves in the district of Táu-lák. The revolutionaries robbed some 3,000 pesos from Mr. Bain, and from the German Catholic Mr. Mannich, all the camphor he had in Chip-chip, and a Persian was also killed and robbed. Both we and the victimized Europeans have made the English Consul aware of these sad occurrences. We will see if he does something for us.**" So he was a Catholic! - David Oakley 2018 book on Robert Hastings, Ch 3:
- On 6 August 1879 Francis Chomley and Daniel Addison Darling, the two resident partners of Brown & Co at Amoy transferred the land at Anping to Julius Mannich, their Agent in South Formosa for one thousand (silver) dollars. On the same day in August 1879 Brown & Co sold their remaining land at Takow, which had been originally held by Dent & Co, to Mannich for $4,500. At the end of 1881 the business of Julius Mannich & Co was taken over by Dircks & Co., a Swatow-based German shipping company and commission agent, in 1881. Dircks & Co operated the Anping trade under Friedrich Wilhelm Hulse, but after his death and burial at Takow on 19 April 1882, Dircks & Co closed their Formosa business in July 1883. In 1886 Mannich & Co resumed his business at Anping until 1898.
- Julius Mannich, a German businessman probably from one of the Hanseatic Towns, joined Brown & Co of Amoy at their Takow branch in 1872 under Daniel Addison Darling, who had established the branch in 1869 with E C dos Santos. In July 1872, Brown & Co moved their business up to Anping, retaining just a small section of land at Takow. By 1874 Julius Mannich had become the Agent for Brown & Co for South Formosa with E C dos Santos as his assistant. In the same year of 1874 Brown & Co moved the major part of their business, tea, up to Tamsui where Daniel Addison Darling established a new branch. Mannich continued on as the Agent in south Formosa, but now without the help of E C dos Santos...
- "After the departure of Robert Dross in 1880, Julius Mannich carried on alone until **September 1881, when he left for Yokohama** and Friedrich Wilhelm Hulse, a partner in the German firm of Dircks & Co, Swatow, took over his business."
- By **1886** Julius Mannich had returned to Anping, lured no doubt by the burgeoning trade in camphor. Initially he took on his old associate E C dos Santos as his assistant, but later employed Alexander Turrian, then Ferdinand (Fritz) August Meyer, before taking on Johannes Petersen as his manager and Agent in the camphor districts in 1895. Julius Mannich & Co was also importing considerable amounts of opium to help finance his camphor outlays, **as the British Foreign Office records shows that he had about 400 lbs of Opium, worth $2,905 stolen from the Tainan warehouse in July 1888.** Things were also becoming tougher under the Japanese authorities who were soon to declare a monopoly in the trade in camphor. In 1896 Petersen, acting as the Agent of Julius Mannich & Co in the camphor districts, was denied the right to work by the Puli Prefect despite his having the required documentation. The only remaining son of his parents in Germany, Johannes Petersen died at Hongkong on 12 February 1897 mourned ‘by his many friends in South Formosa’. During this period of lucrative camphor trading, Mannich also retained, as did Robert Hastings, an office in Hongkong to be close to the fluctuating exchange. Despite the camphor trade from Anping dwindling after 1896, Julius Mannich & Co persevered under the management of Robert Hasting’s son, Harry Hastings, assisted by his old classmate Stuart Elphinstone until 1898. On 26 May 1898 Julius Mannich transferred all his Takow and Anping properties to Bain & Co for 30,000 Japanese Yen.
