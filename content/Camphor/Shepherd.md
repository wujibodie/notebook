### 1. The "Quarantine Policy" vs. "Masterless Land" (Chapters 7 & 12)



- **The Logic of Quarantine (pp. 180-182):** Shepherd argues that expanding civil and military administration into the mountains would have imposed massive "control costs" that the meager tax revenues of the frontier could not offset. Thus, the state chose a policy of quarantine—drawing boundaries to keep Han settlers in and raw aborigines out.
    
- **The 1722 Boundary (pp. 186-187):** Shepherd discusses the piecemeal establishment of the boundary (the mounds and trenches). Crucially, he quotes officials who viewed the boundary as a way to prevent Han from provoking the aborigines into rebellion.
    
- **The Payoff for Your Argument:** You can use Shepherd to explicitly refute Milisch. The Qing drew internal ethnic boundaries not as the edge of their empire, but as the outer limit of their fiscal and administrative investment. The land beyond was still Qing territory; it was just deliberately managed as an unadministered buffer zone (see Shepherd's conclusion, pp. 408-410).
    

### 2. Hijacking the Frontier Land-Tenure System (Chapter 9)

Perhaps the most brilliant connection waiting to be made in your paper is regarding the "Perpetual Lease" Milisch and Horn signed with the Kavalan/Pepo chiefs (your Appendix 2). Milisch thought he was doing something European—signing a Lockean treaty with indigenous sovereigns. But Shepherd's Chapter 9 shows that **Milisch was actually participating in a well-established Qing frontier land-tenure practice, just stripping it of its Qing legal framework.**

- **Qing Recognition of Tribal Claims (pp. 240, 244-245):** Shepherd emphasizes that the Qing did recognize tribal ownership of vast tracts of uncultivated hunting grounds.
    
- **The Aborigine Large-Rent (fan ta tsu) System (pp. 252-255, 268):** By the 18th century, a system evolved where Han settlers could legally lease tribal land by paying a large-rent (ta tsu) to the aborigines.
    
- **The Missing Magistrate's Stamp (p. 249):** Shepherd translates an early 1709 land patent. The crucial difference between the Han-aborigine leases and Milisch's lease is that Han leases required the local Qing magistrate's investigation and seal. The state sanctioned the lease and then registered the land for taxation.
    
- **The Payoff for Your Argument:** You can argue that Milisch wasn't inventing a new legal reality; he was engaging in a standard fan ta tsu lease-purchase (p'u mai), but misreading the indigenous chiefs as independent sovereigns rather than Qing subjects. As you correctly note in your draft, the sine qua non of these contracts was the magistrate's stamp. Shepherd gives you the exact historical precedent for these contracts.
    

### 3. The I-lan / Kavalan Precedent (Chapter 10)

Your episode takes place in southern Yilan (Gamalan/Kavalan). Shepherd briefly addresses the incorporation of the I-lan plain, and it perfectly mirrors the Qing reaction to Milisch's Talamo colony.

- **Strategic Incorporation (pp. 358-359):** Shepherd notes that the Qing did not extend administration to the I-lan plain until 1810. Why did they do it then? Because of "the threat of pirate raids on Taiwan's coasts and the fear that rebels and bandits would find a haven in I-lan."
    
- **The Payoff for Your Argument:** The Qing calculus in 1810 was identical to 1869. The Qing left the area alone to save money until a foreign or rebel threat forced their hand. Horn and Milisch became the exact strategic threat that tipped the Qing cost-benefit analysis, forcing them to officially open the Danan'ao quarantine and recruit Han settlers (as Tang Zangun noted in your draft).
