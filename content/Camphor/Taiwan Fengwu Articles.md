王威 - 清嘉道年間葛瑪蘭土地賦稅制度之政治過程及其影響
- Gemalan 廳 was first established in 1810 following a series of pirate raids, which led the Fu-Zhe zongdu, Fang Weimian, to request that it be formally brought under imperial control. The official Yang Tingli was assigned to survey and establish the district, which primarily meant taking a census, recording land ownership, and establishing a tax base that could sustain a small civil and military administration. The surveys taken at this time earmarked the majority of tax revenues for local discretionary spending, and Kamalan, despite its small population, came to provide 12% of Taiwan’s total tax receipts. 
	- Wang follows the positive reappraisal of the Jia-Dao era (citing Rowe, Wang Wensheng, and Daniel McMahon) by taking the example of the Kamalan survey to see how local officials squared central requisitions with local fiscal demands as well as the expectations of common people. 
- Yang Tingli (as Taiwan zhifu) had been protesting for the establishment of a Kamalan *ting* since the 1790s, in order to pursue rebels and pirates who sheltered there, but had previously been rejected on the grounds of the expense involved and the risk of triggering conflict with the natives. Wang suggests that the Fujian officials were really concerned that Kamalan would be a burden to them, since any tax revenues would be siphoned to the central state while the local government would be responsible for major expenses. As a counter-proposal, the general Saichonga 賽沖阿 suggested establishing garrisons of native troops overseen by 屯弁 (minor garrison officers); this would avoid extra expenses on salaries etc. Saichonga seemingly wished to keep Kamalan as a purely “Fan” region under military administration and avoid registering it on the tax rolls. 
	- The hubu fiercely opposed Saichonga’s proposal, precisely because they saw it as a way of avoiding the entry of Kamalan into the central taxation system. 
- In 1810, after Fang Weimian requested the extension of Qing administration to Kamalan, an initial petition was made by a group of local notables to establish a full yamen; however, Yang rejected this idea, which he saw as an attempt by the strongest settlers to profit from the expenses of the administration and ultimately to pressure the government to allow them to encroach more land from the natives. 29
- Yang’s initial survey set the land tax of Kamalan at 12,664 *shi*, about 1/3rd of Zhanghua’s. For as long as this sum was levied outside the ordinary tax structure, it could be used within Kamalan itself, and the goal of Yang (as well as his superiors in Fujian) was to keep these funds local for as long as possible. This was done in part by classifying most of the levy as “surplus rent” 餘租, which was reported to the hubu but not forwarded to the central treasury. 32-34
- Conclusion: since the incorporation of Kamalan into the Qing administration did not much resemble the original court proposals, we have to assign a large role to local officials in deciding how Kamalan should be administered. The structure developed there reflected local officials’ attempts to manage their own needs alongside the demands of the central government and the local population. Notes that Kamalan was unusual in the large proportion of revenues available to its administration, which allowed them to cover their ordinary expenses as well as occasional crises without external help.
