- **Boundaries as Security Belts, Not Sovereign Limits:** Lin writes that the Qing boundary was "not perceived as a line demarcating a bounded unit, rather that it was seen as a series of small bands forming a security belt that surrounded the center of civilization from a distance." (p. 194).
    
- **The Rejection of Westphalian Borders:** Lin explicitly contrasts the Qing system with modern nation-states: "The border might have served as a defensive and regulative mechanism, but it was not an exclusive boundary serving to establish an independent sovereign state" (p. 194).
    
- **Managing People, Not Claiming Soil:** The Qing empire didn't recognize geographical limits in theory. The purpose of the borders was "not to define and defend the state sovereignty but to manage the flow of people and things across social classifications" (p. 169).
    
- **The Takeaway for your paper:** You can cite Lin to prove that when the Qing expelled Milisch, they were not arbitrarily changing the rules of international law; they were defending an established, functional spatial system of ethnic quarantine (the "Earth Ox" line) against a rogue actor who fundamentally misread how Qing territory worked.
    

### 2. The "Pulse-Vein" (Dimai) Argument Decoded

In the German archives (Chunk 1), the Qing officials complained to Minister Rehfues that Milisch could not chop down the trees in Talamo because the mountain slopes formed the "Puls-Ader" (Pulse-Vein) of Formosa, and disturbing them would ruin the natural order. Lin's Chapter 6 provides the entire intellectual context for this.

- **Earth-Vein Theory (Dimo / Dimai):** Lin explains the Chinese cosmogeographical belief that the landscape is a living organism made up of "dragon veins" carrying vital qi (energy-matter). The topography of Taiwan was seen as a "dragon vein" that originated in Fujian, traveled under the ocean, and emerged in northern Taiwan (p. 316-318).
    
- **The Ecological/Cosmic Threat:** Because the landscape was connected to the vitality of the empire, "the dragon vein was vulnerable; it could be cut or weakened by natural calamity or human interruption" (p. 319).
    
- **The Takeaway for your paper:** When Milisch's 180-man logging camp began mass-clearing the old-growth forests of Danan'ao, he wasn't just extracting a commodity (camphor); in the eyes of the Qing literati and local officials, he was physically severing the cosmic arteries of the island. You can use Lin to show that the Qing objection was a sophisticated blend of state security and imperial fengshui, not just primitive superstition.
    

### 3. The Institutional Context of the Camphor Monopoly

You noted Milisch's intense frustration with the Qing camphor monopoly. Lin (pp. 211-212) explains exactly how this monopoly functioned in the north/Kavalan region.

- **The Depository of Military Supply:** The Qing state needed timber for naval shipyards. To get it without paying high administrative costs, they appointed a "large head lumberer" (dajiangshou) and gave him a monopoly over camphor extraction.
    
- **The Squeeze:** The lumberers were allowed to cross the aboriginal border to cut trees. To fund this, they forced smaller camphor makers to sell to them at half the market price. The "large head lumberer" kept the massive surplus.
    

### 4. The Uselessness of Milisch’s "Perpetual Lease"

Milisch signed a highly formalized "Perpetual Lease" with the indigenous chiefs, believing this gave him airtight property rights. Lin’s Chapter 5 on Contracts destroys this assumption.

- **Contracts Required State Sanction:** Lin shows that the Kavalan region was awash in written contracts. However, the validity of a contract was absolutely "contingent upon a moral order believed to be sanctioned by government" (p. 121).
    
- **The Power of the Yamen Seal:** For a land or resource contract to be truly secure, it required an official seal (chuoji or guanfang) from the local magistrate. The seal represented the "objectification or projection of personal socio-political authority" and connected the local document directly to the Emperor (p. 264-266).
