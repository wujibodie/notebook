
Horn's Deposition (f. 27-31)
Tamsui, 20th May 1869

Sir,
I have to acknowledge the receipt of a despatch from you informing me that you had received a letter of instruction from His Excellency Sir Rutherford Alcock (K.L.B?) Her Majesty's Minister at Peking, stating that a complaint had been made against me of violation of the Chinese law, and of the Treaty, in so far that I have established my self within the territory of the aborigines at aplace named Ta-Lam-O and that I am now cutting down timber trees. 
In compliance with your orders I have suspended all operations at the place in question, and present myself to you for advice and assistance. 
I now beg to lay before you a statement of the affair, which I shall feel obliged if you will communicate to Her Majesty's Minister, who after this explanation I trust will take a different view, and grant his support and assistance in this difficulty.
On or about the 1st July last -- being at Saou baay for the purpose of purchasing timber necessary for the new buildings I am now erecting for the Imperial Maritime Customs at Kilung, I renewed an acquaintance with several aborigine chiefs, who invited me to visit them in their own territory, in doing so they offered me a lease of the valleys of Talamo and Lam-o on certain conditions. My principal object being to obtain an honest living and improve our relations with the Aborigines, I accepted their offer under an agreement with them, the revision of which has been handed to you. The Chiefs themselves, under guarantee that they will not be molested by the Chinese, are willing to come to this Port and ratify the same. On concluding this agreement, I forthwith consulted Mr. James Milisch of this Port, who volunteered to carry out the venture, being convinced that it was not Chinese territory and solely the property of the Aborigines, we had the usual right to clear the same, more so as we had the good will and sanction of the masters of the soil, who do not acknowledge Chinese authority, and that therefore no offense against the Treaty has been committed. 
We commenced operations by erecting the usual border stockades, making extensive roads, clearing and putting under cultivation a large tract of land, and other improvements necessary for the object in view; aso made arrangements with the Cabarrans who have engaged to settle on the land, cultivate the same, on our finding implements and food for them and their families as the agreement with them put into your hands may show.
This same people the Cabarrans are the descendants of the original inhabitants of the Capsulan Valley, who have been gradually driven back and deprived of their homes and lands by the Chinese, in most instances by foul means, and have now gladly taken advantage of this opportunity that offers them fair prospects of bettering their condition and obtaining a permanent home for their families. A large number of these people with their families are now settled there following the peaceful avocation of tillers of the soil. If these poor aborigines are abandonded to their fate without compensation being given to them, it will not only be the cause of great distess among them, but also result in disturbances with the Chinese. The latter will then attempt again to overrun and settle at these places, as done on several previous occasions, from which they have always been expelled again with considerable loss on their side of killed and wounded. In proof of this statement, on taking possession of the valleys, I buried the bones of upwards of forty headless Chinese, which I found scattered about near the remnants of a Chinese stockade deserted some seven years ago, which has been the last attempt the Chinese have made in colonising this place. 
The cause of this wholesale slaughter on the part of the aborigines is said to have been in retaliation of some outrageous breaching on the part of the Chinese. Under these circumstances I claim the credit of having peacefully settled with the aborigines a question of possession, which the Chinese have hitherto not been able to maintain, aside therefore of other considerations I am entitled to a considerable reward, which is due by any government that desires to claim such a valuable possession of virgin soil, which without my aid, might for a considerable time to come have remained in the hands of Aborigines, who if treated honestly have proved to be friendly disposed to our race, as well as to the Chinese and others under my superintendence. 
From these facts you will observe that a good deal of capital and labour has been invested by all concerned. It is therefore a matter of absolute necessity to obtain from the Chinese government such compensation as will enable me to meet all liabilities entered into, without which I shall be utterly ruined for life.
Under the foregoing conditions all concerned are prepared to abandon the place.
I therefore now pray that a favorable opinion may be taken of this affair; that you will give me your serious support in obtaining the redress called for; that a reasonable time may be allowed for removal of the property of all concerned to Treaty Ports and the disposal of other property that cannot be removed to the native government; that the local government may not be allowed to interfere with me until the above affair has been settled to the satisfaction of those interested; when I shall be in a position to hand the valleys over to your order. 
I have &c. 
James Horn

- The Perpetual Lease is f. 31-33; the Agreement with the chiefs is 34-36. Note that the Agreement's witnesses are E.C. Taintor and D.K. Wood. Taintor was the Maritime Customs Commissioner for Tamsui/Jilong; Horn also mentions in his deposition that he first came to Su'ao on business for the Customs Commission. The Talamo venture had the clear blessing of the Qing Maritime Customs agency itself, or at least of its local (European) representative.
