- In the 1930s and 50s Fu Yiling published a series of preserved mid-Qing tenancy contracts from Fujian, which have served as the basis for speculations about Ming-era tenancy in Fujian as well. Rawski takes it that the basic structure of tenancy did not change much between the Ming and Qing. 
- Basic format of Fujianese tenancy contracts was fixed rent in grain payable after the fall or winter harvest. “From the landlord's perspective, land thus represented an investment with assured annual income. The Fukienese landlord, unlike his north Chinese counterpart, did not generally provide either tools or seed to the cultivator. He did not participate in the farming process, and his only link with the land was the rent received from the tenant.” 18
- The yitian liang/sanzhu system was a form of “permanent tenant rights,” in which a tenant could gain heritable rights to rent land under his original lease, either in recognition of his clearing efforts or following payment of a lump sum. “Whatever its origins, by the sixteenth century, security of tenure could be obtained in Fukien by paying a fee, the "manured field silver" (fen-t'u yin).” This topsoil right could be further sublet or sold without consent of the landlord. 20 
- Absentee landlordship: “Ming contracts usually stipulated that tenants should deliver rent rice to the landlord's warehouses, which were often located in the walled city.” 29
- “A combination of new seed strains, improved tillage, and more fertilizers brought productivity gains to Ming Fukien. Whereas the top yield of good land in Sung times seems to have been three piculs of unhusked rice per mou, some areas in sixteenth century Fukien enjoyed yields nearly twice this figure.”

- 75: “Yet another major factor in changing the Chang-chou economy was the silver that flowed into the prefecture as a result of its expanded trade. In addition to providing fortunes for a lucky few, this new wealth affected agriculture through land prices, land rents, and productivity.”
- By the 1570s Zhangzhou land prices were inflated to 2/3 times those elsewhere: “ Land prices in Chang-chou reflected the competition for land. According to a 1572 report, land was selling for 8 liang of silver per mou, and the rich temple lands in Hai-ch'eng brought from 7 to 10 liang per mou.61 Land prices in Chienning during the same period ranged from 2 to 4 liang per mou,62 about a third of the Chang-chou prices. ”
- “Increases in land prices in Chang-chou probably made it difficult for peasants to purchase land, especially good paddy land.83 The increase in absentee landowning which resulted was by no means to the disadvantage of the cultivator-tenant, however.” 87 Suggests that relatively high rents were made up for by unusually high productivity, meaning that Zhangzhou tenants tended to keep a larger share of a bigger total crop than inland tenants. Because most rents were fixed for long tenures, tenants tended to absorb most productivity increase.

- Sixteenth century guidebooks are another potentially useful source. Often written by merchants and intended for their use, the guidebooks provide detailed information on routes throughout China, including notations of distance and travel conditions, with occasional citations of local products. Unfortunately, they provide no means for distinguishing and comparing trade volumes on the routes described. These guidebooks, found in Japanese libraries, have been discussed by Niida Noboru, "Gen Min jidai no mura kiyaku to kosaku shösho nado — Nichiyö hyakka-zensho no rui nijü shu no naka kara," Toyo bunka kenkyü-jo kiyö, 8:123-166 (1956), and by Sakai Tadao, "Mindai no nichiyö ruisho to shomin kyöiku," in Hayashi Tomoharu, ed., Kinsei Chügoku kyöiku-shi kenkyū (Tokyo, 1958), pp. 25-15
