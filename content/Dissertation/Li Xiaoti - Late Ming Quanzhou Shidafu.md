	- Particularly quotes complaints that low-status people are parading in fine clothes.
	- 同安縣志：晉同二邑為尤多也。

	- 17: “惟濱海為島夷之販，安平鎮其最者矣。至於紳士之家，為書而已。” No contradiction between flourishing foreign trade and literate culture.

- 35: Proposes to focus on the harm caused to Quanzhou society by the wokou issue. 
	- Emphasizes the differences between the politically engaged Confucianism of Jiangnan and the more conservative Quanzhou school. 
		- Lin Xiyuan, most famous Quanzhou 程朱學著 of the late Ming, known enemy of Yangming thought. Multiple contemporaries referred to Lin Xiyuan as a representative thinker of 閩學 Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy. 
			- With others, his most famous contributions were orthodox primers for keju takers.
		- After Lin was removed from office, he despaired that he lacked the means to build an ancestral hall befitting a 士大夫, a local official donated the grounds of 天興寺. This temple was founded in the Song and had been left intact through the Hongwu reign; Li does not know why it was abandoned. The larger sangha it was attached to was forcibly dismantled in **Zhengde 15**. His writings here give a good view of land transfers and the economic realities of 士 life in Jiajing Quanzhou. 

Chapter One - 倭亂與開海
- Lin criticized the policy of “rehabilitating” 招撫 pirates as encouraging their successors; what was needed was a military capable of wiping out their bases 巢滅. The garrison troops were incapable of pursuing bandits deep into the seas: therefore Lin advocated mobilizing the “salt and fishing households,” who could match the Wokou on water. He also advocated uncovering and punishing the wokou’s home villages, assuming that the bandits had support from home — this called for cooperation between Fujian and Guangdong officials. 
	- Also called for merchant and fishing boats to be repurposed as war vessels, with retrofits paid for with silver. 
- A major wokou incursion in Quanzhou was ended by a naval battle at 東石, led by the Tongan notable 葉元忠. 
- He Qiaoyuan describes a general (沈有容) who defended the people of 嘉禾 (Xiamen) from a eunuch’s (Gao Cai?) attempt to tax grain merchants’ boats, arguing that depriving the people of food was what pushed them to join the wokou. 
- Shen Yourong complained (in 1601) that escaping wokou often visited Taiwan, and that the people there (presented as forest-dwelling primitives) suffered as a result. He Qiaoyuan praised Shen’s anti-pirate victories for pleasing not only Minnan’s fishers and merchants, but the natives of Taiwan as well (“who were pleased after their barbarian manner” 東番之人快於夷).
- Li Zhi returned to Quanzhou in 1560 after his father’s death, and describes in 焚書 how he was pressed to lead his brothers and servants to defend the city walls from wokou. 
- He Qiaoyuan’s 海上小議 argued to rehabilitate and reward Zheng Zhilong — He was among Zheng’s ardent supporters in the Quanzhou gentry. 
- He’s argument for trade with the West and East circuits on 126. 
	- Also mentions that anyone who knows a craft can make a good living in Luzon, because 夷雖名慧巧，顧百工技藝皆不如中國人。
	- “今天下人民日眾，圖生日多，若洋禁一開，不但閩人的所衣食，即浙，直之絲客，江西之陶人，與諸他方各從其土物往者，當莫可算。” 129
- In his later 開洋海議, He describes the relations b/w the Dutch and Spanish.
- He Qiaoyuan had a long running correspondence with Zheng Zhilong - 142

Chapter 3 - 商業
Separates this chapter into the viewpoints of his 4 “protagonists:”
- Li Zhi emphasized the moral dignity of merchants as opposed to hypocrites who spoke of “eremitude” or “sageliness” while seeking office and riches. Arguments that every vocation and preference has its place even extends to landlords (多買田宅為子孫謀) - more of a liberal than a real 左派王學 advocate. 
- Lin Xiyuan narrates the stories of some merchants who became rich through selling their own farmed goods, not going abroad. Classic oekonomist: emphasizes hard work, frugality, management of servants. 
- He Qiaoyuan singled out Anping merchants among seafarers. Both He Bingdi and Fu Yiling recognized the importance of the 蕃薯頌. 
	- In 壽顏母序, He directly compares Anping and Huizhou. 176
- 李光縉，景璧集
	- Gives the biography of a sojourning merchant named Li Yuxi 李寓西 who spent time in Macao and Manila. Favorably compares his exploits to those of the moneymakers from Sima Qian. Especially praises his abilities as translator. Describes how Yuxi predicted and escaped the 1603 massacre, **because he knew eunuchs taking over the maritime customs would lead to disaster.** 
		- Why was this massacre so universally blamed on Gao Cai?
	- The porcelain merchant 狀天夔 intervened with officials to alter the tax status of his native 清源, in one case by increasing the number of tea trees covered by one accounting 畝, in another by having some local lands taken away from 開元寺 (critique here of “farmers laboring to fatten the monks”).

Chapter Four - Religion 
- Li Zhi section mostly follows Li’s respect for Buddhism and spirits, including a nice inscription for a Tudi Gong temple. 
- Lin Xiyuan critiqued Buddhist monks as largely profit-seeking, and suggested a regional difference between Northern and Southern monks on this count: whereas northern monks were largely fleeing poverty and could expect little gain, southern monks lived luxuriously and were thus more purely driven by greed and harder to eradicate. A comment on the relative wealth of temples in the two regions? 
	- Jiajing (being an ardent Daoist) had publicly criticized Buddhism and called for stricter surveillance by the state, to which Lin Xiyuan responded with a lengthy essay attacking the Buddhists of the south as simply coveting easy access to land (“頭髮衣落，田園連阡“), and describing the history of monastic tenure. 224
	- Lin Xiyuan cites an order from **Zhengde 11** to limit monasteries in Fujian to 60 mou of land each, with the rest to be sold or seized by the state 入官. Lin Xiyuan advocated another round of dispossessions in Jiangnan, with all lands going to the state rather than being distributed to private farmers, as the latter would benefit wealthy purchasers rather than the tenants themselves. 226
	- In his career as official, Lin took it on himself to tear down superstitious temples and statues; writing about his home prefecture, he was glad to report that in his time Buddhist temples were having their lands claimed and their buildings reused as schools and lineage halls. 227-8.
- Section drawing on He Qiaoyuan, the Kaiyuan Gazetteer, and the Quanzhou gazetteer on the occupation of Kaiyuan by military artisans; they were driven out in 1594, supposedly because numerous nearby residents dreamt that their presence there was a bad omen, and shortly thereafter an explosion in their gunpowder stores caused a huge fire. Use of temple grounds for (professional, civilian) military artisans and subsequent removal as index of urgency of military situation? Substantial demand for gunpowder and weapons from 1560s on, driven by silverized professional armies which bought their supplies on the market, then slackening of demand once relative peace had returned?
