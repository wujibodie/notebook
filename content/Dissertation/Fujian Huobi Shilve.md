**七、年号钱“万历通宝”**

福建是否铸过万历钱，近年已有出土证明，漳州曾出土白铜母钱，厚3厘米，可证明有铸。《明神宗实录》卷66载：（万历五年闰八月辛酉）“福建抚按庞尚鹏、商为正条议钱法十四事：一、岁铸额数。各府州应扣存留银两，解司买铜，其有真正黄铜、废铜，折银解纳。一、创建局所。将废弃兵营改造钱局。一、工料。每一千文费银九钱八分零。一、督责磨钱。一、钱质定式。一、出纳定例。除起运钱粮及秋冬赎罪仍征银折谷外，其存留钱粮及春夏纸赎，各令银钱平半上纳。至驿递、兵粮、各衙门工食，亦许兼用。若一时多寡不同，听从民便。一、兼用旧钱，照原定文数折易，与新钱并用，但必须古钱无杂低假，亦止行之民门，若钱粮赎罪俱用制钱，于兼用之中默定一尊之法。一、严禁私铸，凡民间废铜赴官中卖，不许私自收藏，希图凯利。一、责成所司。一、劝民愚民。闽省钱法久废，卒然行之，愚民不无疑惧。议将铺户诚实有身家者，听其自愿领铸钱，存留官银以为资本，即照原定折易之数以钱还官，盖在官散银与铺户以易钱，在铺户纳钱于官以抵银，则人知钱与银并贵，而铺户与民兼利矣。一、设立铺户，举市镇殷实之家充之。随其资本多寡，赴官买钱，以通交易。其民间有愿赴官领钱者听。一、禁煎低银。一、暂借库积。一、禁革骚扰。部复允许。”从字里行间可以看出福建应铸有万历钱，再说万历年间福建还有产铜，如万历《建宁府志》卷4《物产》云：“铜铅，八县俱产。”还有民国《建瓯县志》卷25《实业》载：“铜，宋时置丰国监，取自铜坑，在城东二十里，遗迹犹存。明末（指万历时）奉旨开采，产铜颇盛。”① 这些说明了此时已具备了铸钱的条件，万历年间历时48年，是明朝最长的一代，也可断定福建有铸“万历通宝”。
道光《重纂福建通志》卷 53“钱法”引顾炎武《郡国利病书》曰：“明钱法，遇改元即随年号各铸造通用，但民间使用则随其俗。如闽中福、兴、汀、邵、福宁皆不用钱；漳、泉、延、建间用之，泉、漳所用之钱，与延、建异，泉又与漳异，或以七八文，或以五六文，而准银一分；漳郡如龙岩、漳平亦不用钱，其同俗者龙溪诸县，而诸县所用又有美恶不齐，诏安极精，漳浦次之，龙溪则极恶亦用之；又非时制钱及宋诸年号民间盗铸钱，用者传流，而数年一变。以漳浦一邑言之，嘉靖三年、四年用元丰钱；七年、八年废元丰钱，而用元祐钱；九年、十年废元祐钱，而用圣元钱；十三、十四年废圣元钱，而用元崇宁之当三、熙丰之折二钱。万历三年，废崇宁钱，专用照宁钱；五年废照宁钱，而用万历钱，方一年而万历钱又置不用，用者以抵铜而已，方其用之也”。值得注意的是，此处“圣元”、“熙丰”年号有误，当改为“圣宋”、“熙宁”。顾炎武在福建乡间曾多年调查，其所述明中叶之前各府用钱之差异，引人深思。可以看出当时铸钱萧条，民间多行使历史货币，商品流通有所停滞。自万历之后，民间交易转变为使用白银为主，表明福建在明末流通领域里的白银增多。另一方面，从《明神宗实录》的记载可以看出：官府已允许私人开办银与钱的兑换，表明福建的商品经济有进一步发展，出现了金融业的萌芽。

**VII. Era Name Coin: "Wanli Tongbao"**

Whether Fujian minted Wanli coins has been proven by recent excavations. A cupronickel "Mother Coin" (master mold coin) measuring 3mm thick was unearthed in Zhangzhou, proving that minting occurred. Volume 66 of the _Veritable Records of Emperor Shinzong of Ming_ records: (August of the 5th year of Wanli) "Pang Shangpeng and Shang Weizheng, the inspectors of Fujian, proposed fourteen regulations for currency law: 1. Annual minting quotas... 2. Establishment of minting bureaus (converting abandoned military camps into mints)... 3. Labor and materials (costing 0.98 taels of silver per 1,000 coins)... 4. Supervision of coin polishing... 5. Standardization of coin quality... 6. Regulations for payments and receipts... allowing for a mix of silver and copper coins... 7. Use of old coins alongside new ones... 8. Strict prohibition of private minting... 9. Accountability of departments... 10. Encouraging the populace (as currency laws had long been defunct in Fujian, people were suspicious; the proposal suggested allowing reputable shopkeepers to lead the distribution)... 11. Establishing designated shops... 12. Prohibiting the smelting of debased silver... 13. Temporary borrowing from treasury reserves... 14. Prohibiting harassment [by officials]. The Ministry approved these."

Between the lines, it is clear Fujian was meant to mint Wanli coins. Furthermore, Fujian produced copper during the Wanli period. The _Gazetteer of Jianning Prefecture_ notes that copper and lead were produced in all eight counties. The _Republican Era Gazetteer of Jian'ou County_ also mentions that copper mining flourished during the late Ming (Wanli era) by imperial decree. These facts indicate that the conditions for minting were present. Given that the Wanli era lasted 48 years—the longest in the Ming Dynasty—it can be concluded that Fujian did indeed mint "Wanli Tongbao."
"The "Currency Laws" section in Volume 53 of the Daoguang-era _Recompiled General Gazetteer of Fujian_ quotes Gu Yanwu’s _The Strategic Advantages and Disadvantages of the Prefectures of the Empire_:

> "Under the Ming currency laws, whenever a new era began, coins were minted with the corresponding era name for general use. However, the populace used them according to local custom. For instance, in central Fujian—Fuzhou, Xinghua, Tingzhou, Shaowu, and Funing—coins were not used at all. In Zhangzhou, Quanzhou, Yanping, and Jianning, they were used, but the coins used in Quanzhou and Zhangzhou differed from those in Yanping and Jianning. Even Quanzhou and Zhangzhou differed from each other: sometimes 7 or 8 coins, or even 5 or 6, would equal one _fen_ of silver. In Zhangzhou Prefecture, places like Longyan and Zhangping did not use coins; in Longxi and nearby counties where coins were used, the quality varied wildly—Zhao'an's coins were of the highest quality, Zhangpu's were second, while Longxi used even the poorest quality coins."

The text continues:

> "Furthermore, unofficial 'private mints' of current coins and various Song Dynasty era coins circulated among the people, with the preferred type changing every few years. In Zhangpu County alone: in the 3rd and 4th years of Jiajing, _Yuanfeng_ coins were used; by the 7th and 8th years, they were abandoned for _Yuanyou_ coins; by the 9th and 10th years, they moved to _Shengyuan_ coins; and by the 13th and 14th years, they used the 'Value of 3' _Chongning_ coins and 'Value of 2' _Xifeng_ coins. In the 3rd year of Wanli, they abandoned _Chongning_ coins for _Zhaoning_ coins; in the 5th year, they abandoned _Zhaoning_ for _Wanli_ coins, but after only one year, the _Wanli_ coins were set aside and used only for their raw copper value."

It is worth noting that the era names "Shengyuan" and "Xifeng" mentioned here are likely errors and should be "**Shengsong**" and "**Xining**." Gu Yanwu conducted years of field research in the Fujian countryside; his descriptions of the vast differences in currency usage across prefectures during the mid-Ming are thought-provoking. It shows that official minting was in a slump, and the people mostly used historical [Song Dynasty] coins, which caused some stagnation in commodity circulation.

**After the Wanli era, private transactions shifted to being primarily silver-based**, indicating an increase in the supply of silver in Fujian during the late Ming. On the other hand, records from the _Veritable Records of Emperor Shinzong_ show that the government began allowing private individuals to open exchange businesses for silver and copper coins. This indicates further development of Fujian’s commodity economy and the **early sprouts of a formal financial industry**."
