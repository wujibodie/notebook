Intro
- In general, 財政 can be used to analyze any social group’s division of resources, but the English concept “public finance” (“fiscal policy?”) is generally restricted to “modern” style states; as a result, debates about caizheng in Chinese history often focus on establishing chronologies for progress towards this modern style (he doesn’t use “fiscal state” here, but that may be the end goal he’s referring to). In any case, the Single Whip reforms are usually taken as a crucial moment in the overall development of Chinese caizheng.
	- Cites Liang Fangzhong and 趙鐵鋒, the latter claiming that the Single Whip led to the overcoming of extra-economic exploitation and transition to fully “property-based” system. 
- 3 ways of periodizing Ming fiscal policy: 
1. “Ming-Qing Exceptionalism.” Sees the whole Ming-Qing as forming a distinct period in Chinese economic history; Iwai Shigeki emphasized this as a period which kept to the policy of low and restrained taxation implemented by Zhu Yuanzhang. 
2. “Mid-Ming return.” Liu Guanglin sees the Hongwu system as an exception to the mainstream of Chinese fiscal policy, and the mid-Ming reforms as a return to the practices developing in the Tang and Song.
3. Mid-Ming reform.” The mid-Ming (especially Single Whip) as the moment of a fundamental break with the past. Liang Fangzhong, Zhao Tiefeng, Wan Ming. Service silverization as a shift affecting all layers of society. 
- Ding’s evaluation: Liu was right to point out that the Hongwu system and mid-Ming reforms were completely at odds, and saw this as resulting from the exceptional military needs of the Ming founding; however, “Liu’s theoretical analysis is more fruitful than his empirical research,” and more needs to be done to specify what exactly changed in the mid-Ming and why. 
	- Ding takes issue with Liu’s idea of the Ming reforms “endogeneity” (內生說); specifically, while the fiscal system might have turned towards the market to meet its needs, this needn’t have forced land taxes and corvée towards monetization; Ding proposes to focus on how and why this process occurred.
- Emphasizes that the complexity of the mid-Ming reforms means we need in-depth analysis of specific regions’ experiences. Proposes Zhejiang because of its southern location and abundance of written sources, and proposes to focus on the 布政司 as the provincial official responsible for fiscal matters.
