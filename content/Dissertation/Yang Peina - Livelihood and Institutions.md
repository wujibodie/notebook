Liu Zhiwei Preface 
- Emphasizes that the traditional view of a landbound China (citing Hegel) has been comprehensively overthrown, and maritime history is now a lively topic, but without enough attention to the genuine tensions existing between the coastal and inland ways of life, or to the coinciding interests of the imperial state in both monopolizing coastal resources (mainly salt) and preventing the free mobility of potentially dangerous coastal populations. 

Introduction
- Goal of the book is to serve as a resource on the lifestyles and strategies of coastal people in Ming-Qing Guangdong and Fujian (really the border region, Chaozhou-Zhangzhou). Identifies the historical background as 1) the economic history tradition of Fu Yiling and Liang Fangzhong, carried to maritime context by Yang Guozhen; and 2) the social history tradition of Zheng Zhenman, Liu Zhiwei, Chen Chunsheng, Faure, etc. 
- Cites Chen Chunsheng's argument that maritime populations, lacking the security of property in land, have a need for mobility and flexibility of investment, and less need of accurate household registration or descent networks -- they are "natural commercial groups." 
- Sees the Tang and Song as the period when central state control began to be exerted over coastal people in Yuedong/Minnan, but the populations in question were fragmented and "alienated" from the sources of this control; it was in the Ming that they were really thoroughly incorporated into state systems. 
- Chapter one focuses on the Ming founding, with the basic point that the military focus of Zhu Yuanzhang's initial regime meant that the weisuo become the basic nodes of control over taxation, salt levies, etc for the coasts, and thus the nuclei of urban agglomerations, coming to take on very different roles than the Ming founder envisioned. Proposes to look at the adjacent weisuo of Chaozhou's 大城所 and Zhangzhou's 铜山 for two case studies on the development of these garrison-towns in the Ming.
- Need for more study of fisher households; whereas salt production was geographically stable and salt was highly portable, fishermen ranged around the seas and their produce could hardly be shipped inland; thus the latter proved more of a challenge to government control (and were a natural case for early monetized levies).
	- The initial Ming system of regulated fishing wharfs was largely abandoned by the Zhengtong reign, with local officials choosing to levy monetary 渔课 instead; soon the right to extract profit from coastal fishing was passed over to local elite clans as intermediaries, with fishing households themselves forced to either be adopted into, hired by, or flee from these elites. Deep-sea fishing itself required large ships, large crews, and long-term investments which forced fishermen further into relationships with wealthy patrons. 

Chapter One
- At the end of the Yuan Chaozhou and Zhangzhou were both held by Luo Liang, a subordinate of Chen Youding's, and surrendered to 廖永忠 in 1367/8 (Zhizheng 27/Hongwu 1). Local Chaozhou records note that the fall of the Yuan involved a decade of fragmented authority between pirates, mountain tribes, and "豪强" around the county seats. 19-20
- The earliest weisuo in the region (Xinghua and Chaozhou) were probably garrisoned by troops of Liao Yongzhong's army, rather than the surrendered Yuan forces, which were sent elsewhere. 23
	- Notes that in addition to basic security tasks, weisuo troops had numerous responsibilities relating to public order, including participating in the construction of city walls and moats (83% of early Ming constructions in Guangdong and Fujian note weisuo participation) 25
	- Sees the establishment of the Huangce in the southern coasts as accompanying a second wave of weisuo foundings: “笔者关注的是，黄册制度在沿海地区的落实过程，其实伴随着另一波地方州县卫所的设立.” For example, in Guangdong, 17 suo were founded in Hongwu 14-24, which Yang defines as "州县卫所" since they mostly filled functions of local public order, especially functioning to enroll and manage "bandit" and Tanka populations. 29-30
- Example of garrisons used to control problematic coastal populations: in Hongwu 24, the whole population of Nanaodao was forcibly moved inland, and at the same time Chaoyang's 千户所 was founded; Yang infers that the Nanaodao islanders were enrolled as Chaoyang's garrison troops. The practice, adopted in the late Hongwu years, of forcibly enrolling coastal locals as garrison troops "killed two birds with one stone" by both suppressing troublemakers and topping up garrison numbers, but the new native troops proved difficult to manage and prone to fleeing or keeping illegal contact with outside elements etc. 38 
- Ends first section by affirming the need to see the full set of Hongwu military and administrative policies as a single whole, designed and implemented in unity to fulfill the founder's driving vision, rather than analyzing each policy piecemeal. 
- The standard practice for garrison recruitment along the coasts was for households to provide 1 soldier per 4 ding; in Fujian this was increased to 1 soldier per 3 ding, presumably because of the relatively greater need for garrisons, and certain households were requisitioned twiceover. This would provide very strong impetus to lie about household size. 
	- Cites Hong Kan 洪堪 's report of Yongle 2 for proof that the double-requisitioning of soldiers in Fujian was a known problem. 
	- Note though that the "3 ding" could be calculated in various ways, either abstracted directly from grain tax quotas, and/or combining multiple smaller hu (such that 10 households with 18 ding between them might collectively provide 6 soldiers.)
- The practice of levying garrison soldiers directly from household registers meant that the (fiscal) enrollment of coastal populations proceeded hand-in-hand with the staffing of the garrisons: 垛民入军与编民入籍同时进行，相辅相成。46
- In Hongwu 27, under the inspection of Zhou Dexing, major population swaps between garrisons were enacted in order to lessen the discipline issues resulting from soldiers being stationed near their homes; cites the WL Zhangzhou Fuzhi that the Zhenhai wei 镇海卫 was garrisoned by troops from Putian, who thus spoke with Putian accents "镇海卫皆莆人，故尚莆音." 52. 
	- Apparently the troops at Zhenhai still recalled that their ancestors were from Xinghua/Putian and preserved certain customs from that region 有莆遗风. 
- In Guangdong, Hongwu 27 was the year many of the main coastal garrisons were founded, after being relatively neglected previously, including Dongguan and Dapeng. 
- In general, weisuo troops were divided between farming 屯 and garrison 守 duties at a 7:3 ratio, but in coastal Guangdong and Fujian this was reversed; some especially barren suo did not have tuntian at all. The garrisons were expected to be supplied by outside resources.
	- In Guangdong the standard ration was set at 2:8 after 1443. 
- At the start of the Ming, coastal fortresses 水赛 were subordinate to the weisuo, which supplied their soldiers and vessels; coastal patrols via the 水赛 were a routine part of weisuo duties. In the later Ming, however, the 水赛 were separated from the weisuo and put under an independent command structure. 
Part 3：Development of Weisuo Population Management
- In addition to being symbols of the power of the Ming state, the coastal garrisons also were home to the daily life of their soldiers, and as a result they accrued hanger-on populations not part of the official household  or weisuo registers. 
- Historians like Gu Cheng 顾诚 and Zheng Zhenman have contributed the understanding of the "localization" of weisuo and their development into nodes for the management of civilian populations; Yang proposes to study the historical process by which they merged with their local societies. One important point is that certain people were registered with weisuo but not with 州县, and they went through the weisuo for exam participation, taxation, etc., making the weisuo administrative units independent of the zhouxian. Sees the period when the full populations living around garrisons came to be identified as "卫所军户" tied to their particular suo, as opposed to members county-registered 军户 who happened to be serving in a given place, as the mid-Ming, following both the growth of independent economic life at the weisuo and a set of military reforms culminating in the Zhengtong/Jiajing years. 
- In the early Ming, the weisuo began to experience a decline in soldier populations, but an increase in hangers-on, as civilians sought to register under weisuo to avoid normal lijia corvee. This led to reforms in the Jingtai-Tianshun years, which Yang sees as reflecting competition between the Zhouxian and Weisuo for this population: "州縣與衛所在關於衛所多餘人戶管理權上而爭奪。" 75
- After Zhengtong, the responsibility of managing "surplus" weisuo populations largely passed from the 軍戶 lijia to the weisuo themselves, partially so they could mobilize these pops to make up shortfalls in the ranks, and in part b/c soldier reassignment often made tracing a a missing soldier's 原籍 family difficult -- it was easier for weisuo to deal w/ this on their own. 77
	- Became more pressing after losses in the Tumu incident
- Starting in Hongzhi 5, household registers were prepared for the weisuo, which began formally recording the civilian populations. Once so registered, they could be forced into military service when needed. **In the jiajing reign, it was also proposed to make weisuo registered civilians liable for normal 均徭 levies, which policy was mandated across the empire in Longqing 6**. Pang Shangpeng, while tasked with surveying the tuntian in 榆林, proposed enrolling them in the Single Whip. 80-82
	- NB: Surely "junyao" and "single whip" here mean taxing the civilians in silver to fund the suo themselves. 
	- In 1535 戴璟 had levied a silver tax on Guangdong, charging from the silver earned on "fields, pawnshops, rent." Garrisons with little land instead had silver levied by ding. 
	- Weihaiwei was enrolled  in the Single Whip first in Wanli 47 -- what about Minnan garrisons?
- 衛學: Crucial part of "civilianization" of the weisuo was the establishment of wei schools. Proposes to study weixue as an aspect of the garrisons' evolution into units of household registration and management. 
	- Permission to construct schools was extended to all wei in the Zhengde period, though individual garrisons still had to actively construct them. In 1466 歲貢 was extended to weixue, at a rate of one 生員 per two years. 
- Disagrees with Gu Cheng's extension of his finding that frontier garrisons acted like/took over 州縣 admins to the coasts; garrisons functioned quite differently on coasts than in the mountains or far north. 
- In coastal Fujian/Guangdong, weisuo were established in and enrolled from preexisting dense populations. They relied on rather than replacing existing county admins. 96-7
- The coastal garrisons never had much 屯田 to start with and lost much of what they were initially granted within decades. They were always reliant on 民運糧，with granaries administered by the 州縣. 
- Very nice summary of this whole section on 99: basically, the Weisuo which were initially just seen as camps or stations inside the territories of the 州縣 gradually developed local identities and settled populations distinct from their surrounding counties, even as they became more administratively and fiscally intertwined with those counties. 

Part 4: Salt and Fish
- Lin Xiyuan, serving in Guangdong, noted that local saltern registers hadn't been updated in 60 years. 107
- Because salterns had no corvee duties, they tended to accumulate land through purchase and 詭寄. 
- Great quote frorm Hongzhi Xinghua Fuzhi on how salt monopoly and fishing customs were established in response to growth of profit that threatened state authority: "上以益軍國之需，下以彌逆節之萌，其計深矣。" 108
- In the early ming, though fishers and Tanka were registered with the county admin, their records were kept separate from those of the 民戶. Boat households would not be made to perform normal lijia corvee.  117
- 28 河泊所 were originally established in Fujian, 46 in Guangdong, mostly in Hongwu 14-16. It's possible that the "rebellious Tanka" gathered into weisuo in Hongwu 27 were fleeing these new tax stations. 
- Around the start of the Ming saltern households began switching from kiln-evaporated salt to sun-evaporated salt 曬鹽. This had numerous consequences, one of which was of course to reduce costs for the producers even as their tax quota remained the same. Another was to break the dependence of saltern households on upland wood supplies -- previously salterns had been split into the coastal saltwater gatherers and hillside wood gatherers, but with sun evaporation the latter disappeared while the former prospered. 
	- Chaozhou and Putian among the leading regions in adoption, circa 1450s.
	- The wealthier and more flexible saltern households now had funds to hire wage workers; meanwhile the ease of production also made smuggling salt far easier. 
	- The earliest case of silver remittance for salt taxes was the release of mountain salterns from in-kind duties after their role in production became obsolete （120-21）
- Zhangzhou originally had no salt offices 鹽場, but in Jiajing 27 (1548) silver collections were begun on salt fields which had sprung up in the region. In Wanli 4 Liu Yaohui put Zhangzhou under the jurisdiction of the 南路 salt office operating out of Quanzhou, which meant that only officially licensed salt merchants could trade salt in the prefecture, but complaints from residents about price gouging led to this policy being rescinded in Wanli 7 (Geng Dingxiang?); thereafter salterns were taxed based on the number/size of their salt mounds and salt trading ships, in Quanzhou as well. 125
- Guangdong was the first province in the empire to remit the salt tax to silver and allow private trade. Hui'an followed suit in 1503, while 94% of Fujian's salt tax was collected in silver by 1508; the last parts were remitted to silver in Wanli 3 (Pang Shangpeng?). 127
	- “改折過程中，福建地區的官員與廣東官員態度相差甚多.”
- In the Zhengtong-Hongzhi years, Fujianese officials attempted to control the salt market by restricting private shipping and destroying "excess" salt fields, even as their remittance policies encouraged some legal trade; this created the conditions for a massive private salt trade, which most observers noted flourished in Quanzhou from that time. 128
- One consequence of silverization of the salt tax is that many "saltern" households no longer produced salt, preferring to earn their silver in agriculture or trade, while hiring laborers to actually do the work of making salt. 

Fish tax reforms
- In the course of the Ming, most fishing tax stations closed down, above all in the Jiajing reign. In Fujian, only 2 of the original 28 were left by the end of the Wanli reign. Largely a consequence of tendency for Dan households to be shifted into lijia or weisuo registration.

海界圈佔
- In the Ming, deep-sea fishing made advancements, and because of the large capital outlays required tended to be monopolized by the wealthy (hiring wage laborers on the actual ships) while shore fishing/combing was the preserve of the poor; but the reform of the 魚課 tended to permit wealthy to claim coastal fields as their own property. 140
- Cites a 1574 text by 朱天球 claiming that Yunxiao's coastal nets were taken over by the powerful between the Chenghua and Wanli years. 
- "明代中期從後濱海地方宦族豪民可以以承納科米爲由，依勢恃力圈佔海塗，劃定海界，佔有私業，小民或避往別處或繳納私稅方可作衛." 143
	- Fundamental reason was state reliance on written ownership records for tax collection purposes - bias to accept claims of families who were literate and could deliver the relevant taxes, esp. in the context of urgent need for 軍餉. 
	- General tendency to cut through the knot of household status by levying taxes on property (land, boats) tends to strengthen government reliance on the wealthy, who are thus trsusted with determining tax boundaries. 

Part 3: 海寇與文教並興
- Starts from the 東山志 written in late Jiajing by one 陳天資, the gazetteer for both Dongshan Peninsula and Dachengsuo. 
- Summarizes recent research by Zhang Kan, Liu Zhenggang, Chen Shangsheng; private maritime trade was stimulated by the decline of Ming coastal forces after the Zhengtong years, with the major patterns of trade (connecting domestic trade + tribute trade along the coasts) set in the Chenghua/Hongzhi years. 
- Rise of maritime trade: the rise of maritime commerce in the mid-Ming also saw the comprehensive takeover by teh wealthy and powerful of maritime resources. 
- Maritime "confiscations" became the channel through which the Weisuo provided a legal cover for coastal private trade. In Guangdong, the co-anchoring of private and tributary ships provided another opening. 154
	- Cites a 1493 account of Guangdong traders 私通番船 -- earliest such reference?
- Both Chaozhou (4 ->11) and Zhangzhou (5->10) saw large scale establishment of new counties to deal with banditry (and pop. growth?) 165
- Guangdong Tongzhi on the first 知縣 of Raoping: “饒始分治，民多頑暴，楊公從嚴治之建城池,置公署，興文學，劈田野，驅畲瑤，勸農桑，均賦役，教民禮樂，駸駸向化.” 166
	- Along with this a number of new postal stations were established, especially along the coast. 
- The Jiajing years were tumultuous in the Chao-Zhang boundary, esp. ~1560-66 （JJ 39-45). In JJ 39 one 張璉 rose up in Raoping and linked with other bandit groups to harass Pinghe and Zhaoan, even seizing 鎮海衛. 171
- Notes that officials in Guangdong blamed Zhangzhou for producing bandits and vice-versa (mutual recrimination between Qi Jiguang in FJ and Yu Dayou in GD). 173
- Dongshan literati pointed to their locale's exam success as proof of good customs and blamed piracy/bandity on neighboring Zhangzhou. 

Chapter 3 - Militarization
- First part focuses on conflict over jurisdiction b/w Guangdong and Fujian military authorities, especially over question of 撫 and 巢, e.g. Zeng Yiben case, where Yu Dayou pardoned Zeng only for him to attack Fujian, incurring QiJiguang's anger. (Yu was censored and demoted for this.) Later, Tan Lun petitioned against placement of a military official reporting to Fujian in Dachengsuo. 186-7.
