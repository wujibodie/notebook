Fu Yiling - Ming-Qing Merchants and Commercial Capital

Summary: The Ming-Qing was a period of transition within feudalism.
- The Song-Yuan era saw the quantitative accumulation of traditional commercial capital reach a new stage, with the institutions of trade perfected to match new requirements. 2 At this time the techniques and infrastructure of commerce were more advanced than in Europe.
- sees rebellions of" salt bandits' ' and' ' tea bandits" in the Song as signs of small merchants struggling against "feudal" elites suppressing their scope of action. 3
- Sees the commercial rise of the Ming as originating in the great increase in production unleashed by rural class struggle, namely Zhu Yuanzhang’s pro-farmer policies.
- Lists some of the agricultural tech of the Ming, noting that main advancements occurred in Jiangzhe, Jiangxi, and Nanhai — where was Fujian? Again left out of neighborhood intellectual circuits?
	- Does note that Fujian had water pumps for papermaking.
- Notes that cotton-growing Jiangnan villages suffered when Fujianese merchants were cut off in the late Ming; they had come to rely on them in the Longqing/Wanli years. 
	- 吳梅村，木棉吟：“隆萬中閩高麕至鎮洋，采購木棉，州賴以饒。今累歲弗登，價賤如土，不足以供常賦，余作詩紀之：賬貝當初萬曆間，陳花窗戶積如山；福州青韈島言賈，腹下千金過百灘。“ 8
- The general trend of commercialized agriculture “stimulated the appetites” of landlords and led to renewed concentration of land. Further, these landlords required money, not goods, of their tenants in order to fund luxury consumption. 9 
- On merchant use of female wage/piece labor for textiles in Jiangnan: “despite the pressure of feudal exploitation, “但這新的資本主義的萌芽因素即逐漸孕育滋長于封建社會的母體中。“ Making precisely the metaphor of capitalism “in embryo” in feudalism which Wood criticizes the commercialization thesis for. 
- On rural specialization, cites 閩書 that the town of 長市 produced wigs for export to Jiangnan, especially to Huizhou merchants. 
- Reproduces a long summary by Zhang Han of the commercial map of the realm on 19-23. 
- Common origin of diasporic merchants (Huizhou, Fujian, Shanxi) in overpopulated, land-scarce regions where cooperation in bringing in resources from outside is vital to survival. Notes habits of those with capital to spread it among numerous 伙計 sent “abroad.” Fu sees these agents as being “extra-economically exploited” during their apprenticeships, during which all profit redounds to the principals. 27
- Mentions a 20 year violent feud which broke out between Nanxiang fu and Nan’an fu after the former deceitfully lobbied to have an age-old customs-sharing arrangement rewritten in its favor — sign of merchant classes competing for monopoly rights. 
- Cites the genealogy of the 許 clan of Quanzhou 石崖, who began as silk traders and came to open their own spinning workshops; suggests that they developed (learned) techniques from family members’ frequent stops in Jianning, then “China’s center of silk production.” The Xus also opened factories in local products at places where they exported silks, e.g. iron and paper factories in Shaanxi. 
- Despite the existence of a wage-earning proletariat, the growth of this class was restricted by its embeddedness in “feudal” and “familial” structures (e.g. working in the boss’s home as family servant) — blockage of a truly smooth labor market. 32-33
- Believes it was precisely the integration of agricultural and artisan production, and of merchants into the feudal elite, which stopped the free development of a capitalist class which would have transitioned from commercial to industrial capitalism. 
- Cites Marx’s peasant-hating on the destructiveness of merchants returning to their home villages, where they “waste” their wealth on “鄉族關係.” 39 


**Fujian Maritime Merchants** 
- Takes the 300 years around the Zheng as the peak of Fujianese maritime activity, even by comparison to the Song-Yuan. Foundation was the prosperity of the agricultural-artisan economy and landlord luxury demand, stimulated by Zhu Yuanzhang’s agriculturalist policies in spite of his bans on commerce. Perfectly Brennerite?
- The period when Fujianese merchants expanded from domestic to overseas trade preceded European stimulus circa 成弘之間 (1465-1505).
	- A report of Jiajing 3 reported that Fujian vessels were intercepting and “enticing” 誘惑 tribute trade ships. 108
- By the mid-Jiajing years Portuguese ships were a common sight at Yuegang, Haicheng, and 泊浯洲.
- Repeatedly cites 小叶田淳, e.g. on claim that most Chinese ships at Malacca came from Yuegang. 
- 柯喬 was an early describer of Yuegang as a 閩南一大都會 with 10000 households. 
- Another major smuggling port was 詔安 梅嶺. Cites Yu Dayou that this town had 1000 households dominated by the Lins, Tians, and Fus. Cites Zhangzhou Fuzhi on a story of 中丞 阮鶚 chasing “wo” who fled to Meiling. His men want to massacre the town, but Ruan replies “其在浙直為寇，還梅嶺則民也.” 
- In addition to Fujian merchants in Zhejiang and Guangdong, there were also large contingents of Huizhou merchants selling silk + porcelain in Minnan; Huizhou merchants as direct suppliers of Minnan traders’ goods. 
- Cites Xu Fuyuan’s Wanli 21 census of overseas ships: 44 to Dongyang, of which 16 to Luzon; 44 to Xiyang, of which 4 each to 下港，暹羅，舊港，交趾. 2 to Malacca. Xu requested to add 12 more licenses; by 1597 the total was increased to 190. 
- Emphasizes Manila’s role as silk entrepôt for the Japanese market. 
	- Notes that by at least the late Wanli years traders to the Philippines often brought back rice - could some of this have been farmed by Chinese settlers? 
	- Rice was exempt from Ming custom duties, making it an especially attractive import. 
- The merchant who enticed the Dutch to visit Fujian from Pattani was one 李錦 of Haicheng. Cites the story from Dongxi Yangkao. 
- In Jiajing and Wanli sources the term 那達 etc often comes up in Nanhai trade; this is a transliteration of the Persian Nakhuda, the master of a ship, and usually refers to the title granted to CHinese merchant-captains active in the Malay ports. 
- Dates the start of Ming-era trade from Fujian to Japan to 1534, when a mission to Ryukyu decided to continue further and made a large profit. Numerous Zhang-Quan voyages to Japan attested in the 1540s. 125
	- Cites the 日本一鑒
- A 1610 report by Fujian Xunfu 陳子貞 stated that Fujianese traders claiming to trade with Luzon would actually trade with Japan — not clear if he’s complaining about entrepôt trade or direct voyages by licensed merchants. 
- Cites a report by 沈鈇 in the 詔安縣志 that Li Dan was trafficking Japanese and Dutch goods via Xiamen. 
- Sees the opening of Fujian as product of struggle by merchant class. 开国论者，如林希元，何乔远，傅元初等的出现，皆证明自由商人的力量已逐渐开始抬头。130
	-  for Fu Yuanchu cites a report preserved in the 天下利病书
- After Haicheng was opened to sea trade, it became home to merchant guilds responsible for tax collection called 铺商. 133
- Sees the Fujian merchants as ultimately still “feudal” in nature because their organizations did not have clear and permanent structures beyond “rich investors and poor agents,” and because the leading owners used “extraeconomic exploitation” to extract profit. 135-36
	- these merchants were carrying on a rural landlord logic of earning without work to the simple circulation of commodities, and did not involve themselves in production, unlike Florentine merchants. Further, the victories of European colonialism in Southeast Asia reduced the scope of opportunity for Chinese capitalists there and left them as mere intermediaries. 137
- Views the excessively close relationship between merchants and “aristocracy” as one reason for the persistence of Chinese feudalism. 145

Conclusions: The various export industries stimulated by Fujianese traders were all mere extensions of the traditional subsistence economy which could entirely satisfy their limited markets without making any fundamental changes in the direction of industrialization. Further, to the extent that an independent capitalist class was developing towards the end of the Ming, it was beaten down and ruined by the “backwards” Manchu who were more concerned with stamping out sources of resistance than with the enlightened cultivation of commerce (cites Marx on the Qing suppression of foreign trade).


明清時代福建佃農風潮考證
- Begins his study of Fujian’s agricultural economy after noticing frequent “disturbances” 風潮 in his readings of Ming-Qing gazetteers. Believes that Fujian belonged to a “feudal” economy which carried from the Tang to the end of the Qing, defined as an economy where the state and large landlords dominated most production, while the little people were squeezed in corners of the land with barely enough to survive. Cites Yuan and Ming 列傳 for arguments to this effect.
	- Cites Cai Qing from the 福建通志 on monastic land: “天下僧田之多，福建為最。舉福建，又為泉州為最。多者數千畝，少者不下數百。“ He seems to take this on face value and not consider that it may be a polemical exaggeration… 
	- Cites [謝肇淛， 五雜俎](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/五雜俎)， for a claim that (in 閩中) wealthy clans amass land through force, especially uncultivated land or “寺觀香火之奉,” such that 富者日富，而貧者日貧矣。“
		- The full quote, which Fu Yiling compresses to cut out the reference to low land taxes (!): 江南大賈，強半無田，蓋利息薄而賦役重也。江右荊、楚五嶺之間，米賤田多，無人可耕，人亦不以田為貴，故其人雖無甚貧，亦無甚富，百物俱賤，無可化居，轉徙故也。閩中田賦亦輕，而米價稍為適中，故仕宦富室，相競畜田，貪官勢族，有畛隰遍於鄰境者。至於連疆之產，羅而取之，無主之業，囑而丐之，寺觀香火之奉，強而寇之，黃雲遍野，玉粒盈艘，十九皆大姓之物，故富者日富，而貧者日貧矣。Note that the original specifically cites the imbalance between fairly high crop yields and low land taxes as the key reason for Fujian‘s unequal state, which is symbolized particularly by the heaps of grain filling *ships*, 9/10ths of which belong to the wealthy; he also particularly blames the rich clans for their theft of temple lands. 
- Argues that what seem like contract based tenancy relations in Ming-Qing Fujian are really “status-based” 身分的, because of the long contract terms (often lifetime) and abject dependency of tenants on their landlords. 5
	- Notes that tenants could be transferred along with land, as in an interesting case from the Quanzhou Fuzhi 學校 section when an official in Quanzhou gives some land that had been held by the Kaiyuan Temple over to the Zhu Wengong temple, along with the tenants attached to that land (this would be an interesting anecdote to work on). 
- Believes that, while tenants’ rental contracts were often denominated in silver, they would most often be paid in kind. Sharecroppers took half the produce in Haicheng (per Gu Yanwu), but as little as 3 or even 2 tenths elsewhere (Yong’an). 7
- Cites an anecdote from the Zhangzhou Fuzhi where a Tuiguan of Changtai earmarks the 僧田租穀 together with the 認佃銀 (payments made by incoming tenants to either landlords or the previous tenant for transfer of long-term cultivation rights) of certain plots of government-owned land for payment to county schools. The fact of small tenants making these silver payments, for Fu, is a case of “商品經濟侵蝕中國後期封建農村.” 9
- The 冬牲 in-kind „gifts“ (e.g. chickens and ducks) to landlords were especially prevalent in inland, northern and western Fujian. Even there, Fu cites Qing-era Yong‘an county contracts to show that these could be commuted into grain or cash (although was that true in the early Ming?). 9-10
	- The Quanzhou fuzhi (which year?) Fengsu section mentions that in parts of Huizhou and Nan‘an, it is customary for landlords to be feasted by their tenants when arriving for annual inspections, with the tenants providing a chicken and rice, while the landlord gifts cloths or fans in return. The gazetteer takes this as a kind of 禮 rather than a payment. 11
	- In various different places the specific goods customarily owed by tenants to their landlords alongside their rent varied, e.g. tea, wine, beans, pigs, fish and so on.
- In West Fujian (Yanping, Shaowu etc) tenants were often expected to offer labor services as well as rent to their landlords; Fu includes the military service to which tenants could be drafted in the wokou crises as one example. 12-13. 
	- Cites Deng Maoqi’s grievance (recorded in the Yanping Fuzhi) for this; so going back to the 15th century…
	- Also notes fees called 市租，鄉租: payments made on bringing rent grain to the landlords’ urban granaries.
- Conclusion to the intro: Clearly tenants and landlords were highly unequal in status, with tenants subject to various forms of subjection and extra-economic exploitation (額外收取); together with the general isolation and backwardness of feudal China’s rural societies, this provides the background for the various tenant rebellions and disturbances listed in the sources. 14
- First sort of rebellion: those inspired by feudal exactions （封建貢納與勞役). Example: Deng Maoqi (is Fu’s article the source for the later Japanese studies?). Believes Deng’s rebellion to have gathered support because of widespread peasant discontent at the fixing of mandatory grain-delivery and “winter gift” fees; although it failed, it has historical importance as the first clear expression of the people’s disgust with feudal tributes and labor service. 14-15
- Second sort: inspired by conflict over measurements. Because various regions each had their own 斗, without standardization even in a single xian, landlords often tried to use the measures which would increase their own takings. (Presumably a problem worsened by absentee landlordism.)
	- Cites a Quanzhou Fuzhi on a large protest in Quanzhou in Chongzhen 4, which was pacified by agreeing on a standard dou size; however, in Chongzhen 10, another uprising occurred when Nan’an tenants claimed their landlords were using larger measures. In Chongzhen 15, a peasant rebellion occurred in Nan’an, which Fu connects to this issue. According to the Fengsu section of a Quanzho Fuzhi, this rebellion was put down after the 鄉紳 raised a militia (自餉兵四百人) to cut off the rebels, and then negotiated with their leaders. Still, troubles in Nan’an continued until the end of the dynasty.
