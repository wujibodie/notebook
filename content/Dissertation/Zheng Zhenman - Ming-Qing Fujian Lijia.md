Section from Family Lineage Organization on Southeast Fujian:
- In the late Qing, the standard pattern in the coastal plains of Fujian was for villages to have a single or only a small number of surnames; very few villages had people of many surnames living together, and those that did tended to be newer and less populous. 
- "As early as the northern Song, Cai Xiang wrote; 'The people of the prefectures of Quanzhou, Zhangzhou and Putian must pay a head tax in copper every year... [the poor cannot afford it] so the powerful descent-lines and prominent lineages exert themselves to take over their land, and the dissipate or lazy peasants are forced into vagrancy.' Large and powerful lineages clearly already dominated the socio-economic structure of Fujian at that time." 191
	- However, the Song-era lineages were mostly built around temples and monasteries, and split away from these in the Yuan and early Ming. "By the Yuan and Ming periods, residentially concentrated lineages in coastal regions were building ancestral halls, establishing lineage property, and compiling genealogies, and successively forming control-subordination lineages led by the gentry stratum." These lineages disintegrated under the pressures of the wokou and dynastic transition before being reconstituted in the early Qing. 
	- The first "ancestral halls" separate from private residences were constructed in the Southern Song around Putian, but these had not become widespread by the early Ming. 
	- Cites Zhu Weigan's calculation in his *Draft History of Fujian* that between 1555 and 1563, one prefectural seat, eleven county seats, four guard garrisons, and four battalion garrisons fell to wokou, while almost all were besieged. Emphasis on the great dislocation caused to the major lineages, which were especially vulnerable to looting and ransoming of members.
	- Cites Gu Yanwu's claim (in Tianxia Junguo...) that the building of walled forts began in Zhangzhou after 1561. Local militia also began to form to defend these *sai*. These militias increasingly replaced the official garrison troops, and effectively protected their homes against pirates, "but it must also be pointed out that the development of this kind of territorial lineage fighting force frequently also led to intervillage or interlineage feuding, intensifying social contradictions within localities... I believe that the interlineage village feuding that was so common in southern coastal Fujian in the Qing can be traced back to the organization of such militia." 210-211
	- Lineages of coastal Fujian recovered from the early Qing evacuation policies in one of two ways: either a small number of powerful men rebuilt control-subordination lineages, as in the case of Shi Lang's gathering of his clansmen into the new village of Yakou; or lineage members who returned to their native places "willingly organized themselves into contractual lineages." 216
	- In 1690, the governor-general of Fu-Zhe, Xing Yongchao, implemented the lianghu guizong reform, which was meant to formally allocate tax responsibilities by lineage; this in turn led to the formation of a numer of residentially dispersed lineages for tax purposes. This policy was intended to entirely replace the old lijia divisions, in part because "the lijia system had historically generated severe problems in the coastal region," mainly involving the most powerful families seeking li head status and using it to control the subordinate jia families. 230
