Tanaka Masatoshi - Popular Uprisings, Rent Resistance, and Bondservant Rebellions in the Late Ming
- Calls the 1448-9 Deng Maoqi rebellion "the earliest clear-cut example of tenants rising to oppose the payment of rent and to consciously assert a peasant-like claim to the land they cultivated." In his conclusion he says that "Deng's anti-rent peasant struggle was the first great rebellion in Chinese history genuinely led by the tenants themselves that approaches the concept of peasant war." 201; 204. 
	- Sees the origins of this revolt as lying in the opening of silver mines in north Fujian to meet the demand for newly enacted levies of *jinhuayin*. Mine workers dismissed from some of these mines formed bands of *kuangzei,* and were joined by vagrant farmers (some of whom were impoverished by "officials' labor-service extortions) until they developed organizations which struggled in an organized way against the authorities. Notes that at this time (1449) a majority of land in Jianyang county, Jianning prefecture was reportedly owned by absentee landlords. 202
		- Cites Fu Yiling, "Ming-Qing shidai Fujian diannong fengchao kao," in "Ming-Qing nongcun shehui jingji."
	- Deng Maoqi was appointed as head of one of the peasant patrol (zongjia) units in Sha county, Yanping. In this capacity he demanded the abolition of a traditional supplementary rent called the "winter gift." When the landlords attempted to have him arrested the tenants rose up and killed the local magistrate along with the chief of police. The rebellion grew until Deng's troops had captured the Sha county set and pressed on to the walls of Yanping fu. An appeal for amnesty from labor levies (!) was intercepted by stubborn local officials, leaving the rebels with no choice but full-scale war against the troops sent to crush them. Deng was killed in early 1449, but the rebellion continued long enough to overlap with the capture of Emperor Yingzong by the Mongols. 
- "The 1612 gazetteer for Quanzhou prefecture describes how the system of cash crops tended to encourage rent resistance: 'The tenants in the morning go out to the fields to bring in the harvest, and in the evening they go off to the town to sell their crops to the merchants. The situation has reached the stage that tenants made pacts among themselves beforehand not to pay rent to their landlords." 207


Tsurumi Naohiro - Rural Control in the Ming Dynasty
- Basic research questions: 1. Which "peasant strata" were the core of society, and how did that core form the basis for the lijia; 2. what relationship existed between the lijia hierarchy and village class relations? 3. Systemic contradictions and class conflicts w/in lijia. 
- The original definition of "ding" excluded slaves and tenants (both theoretically illegal) and only included "owner-cultivators" (within a single household) "capable of performing labor service." 249
	- However, in actual practice the use of tenants as "ding" in provision of labor levies was accepted very early on. 
- "The aim of the early-Ming policy of resettlement and reclamation was therefore conceived as one that could create an economy based on the small landowner and on the labor of the blood-related family. It was designed to foster the self-sufficient peasant who combined agriculture with household industry and who could provide for the fiscal needs of the state." 250
- Sees the origins of the *lijia* as stemming from the need to organize people on the basis of actually-existing landlord-tenant relations. 254
- *Li* never crossed village or town boundaries, and when they needed to be reorganized they were usually amalgamated together rather than single units being split apart. 
- Table on 257 showing that the number of wards in Luoyuan county, Fujian, decreased from 36 in 1391 to 14 in 1462, then 16 in 1472-42.
- Sees the basic cause of the lijia's breakdown as being the insistence on uniform tax collection, which exacerbated the actually existing inequalities in capacity to pay among the households of a given *li* and *jia*. The increase in tax fraud through commendation, transferal of land registration, etc. led to reforms like the junyao and the shiduan, which fixed the previously irregular labor levies and commuted portions of them into silver. "This testifies to a change in tax-levy principles from a variable lvy per household based on that household's ranking to a uniform levy on adult male labor and land." And, as the basis of taxation shifted to land, "ordinary" landlords increasingly registered their land under the names of officials and gentry to gain tax exemption. 267
- After the Single Whip, functions still performed by li heads included compilation of yellow registers, supervision of tax collection (not delivery), and general maintenance of good order and waterworks, but even these tasks were eventually broken up and performed as regular labor service. 
- Furushima Kazuo suggested that the collapse of the *lijia* resulted from the breakdown of local landlord power as large-scale agricultural work increasingly shifted from bondservant to tenant basis (taken as a necessity for the intensification of productivity). This argument was meant to counter the explanation that monetization led to a rise in absentee landlordism. 268-9.
	- Tsurumi prefers an explanation that looks at the increasing productivity of the labor of tenants themselves as an explanation. Based on an improved legal status and considerable autonomy despite their dependent status, tenant-peasants were steadily improving their participation in commodity production and retailing; when taxes and rents were duly increased, the tenants became aware of their exploitation and struggled against it, in instances like the Deng Maoqi rebellion. 
	- The struggle between tenants and landlords was intensified precisely because of the expansion of gentry landholding, as an identical tax burden came to fall on a smaller portion of landowners. 

Yamane Yukio - Reforms in the Service Levy System in the Fifteenth and Sixteenth Centuries
- Criticizes the view of e.g. Ma Duanlin, that the Song-era *yi* was primarily a system of local participation as minor administrative functionaries. "My inclination is rather to believe that distinct from the community self-governmental service duty, the Song service levy system also embraced the running of errands and performance of miscellaneous tasks within the governmental offices." 282
- The earliest Ming labor levy, the jungongfu, supplied labor for the construction of Nanjing from the nearest prefectures and temporarily replaced the land tax. Only after the 1381 compilation of the first Yellow Registers and creation of the lijia did the "normal" service levy begin. Adjacent to this system, the irregular service levy corresponded to the provision of labor for local government seen already in the Song. 
- By the Xuande and Zhengtong reigns landholding was already becoming unequal, which resulted in the service levies becoming intolerable to some taxpayers. Zhou Chen as the first official to suggest a remedy, in this case by suggesting that certain miscellaneous and irregular services be assigned in a regular, rotating manner to the lizhang households, which were assumed to be better able to shoulder the burden. 
	- Next, Xia Shi implemented the equalized labor service in Jiangxi. Uses Shilu entries from the 1440s and 50 to determine that this meant; concludes that Xia's service gave fixed service levies to the top-grade households, arranged in decennial increments (opposite the decennial lijia service) and came under substantial criticism. 
	- The junyao fa was introduced to Fujian by administration commissioner Zhu Ying, who also spread it to Shaanxi. 289
- The 金花银 was first collected in 1436, and "represented a slackening of the Ming government's control over the circulation of silver" and "manifestation of the strong governmental craving for silver receipts," but not as a part of the equalized labor service reform. 291 
	- Exceptional cases occurred throughout the 15th century of labor levies being commuted to silver, but it was not until a bit after the junyao fa that commutation became regularized. Sees this system as originating in wealthy households paying others to serve their labor levies for them; "once service by proxy had been recognized, it was only a logical step for the rich to pay the authorities, who then hired servicemen rather than finding and paying replacements themselves." 292
- "We have shown that the commutation in the equalized labor service system took place some half a century or more after the land-tax commutation, and was an early sixteenth-century development. Whereas the land-tax commutation was imposed as a matter of fiscal policy arising from the government's demand for silver, the labor service system held within itself the seeds of commutation, and this commutation was adopted with a view to lessening the burden of the services. Naturally, empire-wide the process took place in ways adapted to the particular conditions obtaining in the different localities." 297
- The biography of Sheng Yong in the Min Shu describes his implementation in Fujian of a system similar to the 十段法, though without that name. "Previously, the labor services within the prefecture had tended to be levied by the li clerks... Yong took the acreage and adult male figures for each entire county, established ten divisions (*jia*), and had each year's services met from the adult male levies and taxes due pertaining to that year." 302
	- Based on local gazetteers of Sha county, Yanping and Quanzhou prefecture, the shiduan fa seems to have been operating throughout Fujian at least by the late Jiajing reign. 304
- Ultimately views the shiduan fa as a “countermeasure” to the widespread tax evasion which came in response to the shifting of the service levy onto land in the 15th century. 306
