
Chapter One
- Primarily establishes that Quanzhou had not been a major economic center in the Tang, with Fuzhou (called Quanzhou before the Sui) taking that role. 
- "The communication route linking Fukien with Chekiang and Kiangsi,
which ran along the Min River basin, over the inland mountain ranges, and
through the city of Chien-chou, was well developed by the mid-T' ang." 13
- Tang and early Song sources mention that Quanzhou produced coarse, fairly low-quality textiles. 
- Mentions a local legend that three of Muhammad's disciples settled in Quanzhou during the Tang, but this is first attested in He Qiaoyuan's Min Shu, and the earliest uncovered mosques are from the Song; So doubts that substantial numbers of foreigners settled during the Tang. 
- Further doubts Clark's narrative of maritime trade developing in a relatively independent Minnan under the Min kingdom; instead, it was only under the warlords Liu Zongxiao and Chen Hongjin, who ruled for 30 years at the end of the Min kingdom, that Quanzhou took off. 25-26

Chapter Two - Takeoff 
- Fujian was early to double-cropping rice, both indigenous varieties and Champa rice; the latter was introduced from Fujian to the Yangtze in 1012. Champa rice was mostly important because it required less water and so was well-suited for poor (especially hilly) frontier regions without good irrigation. 
- Southern Fujian in the early Song was exporting textiles, but for the bottom of the market. Fuzhou was the main center for sugar, with the south mostly producing for local consumption. Minnan did export some lychee and cotton. 
- The largest landholders in Northern Song Minnan were Buddhist temples, which could own hundreds of *qing* across multiple counties worked by sharecroppers. Secular landlords were seemingly less important. 
- Suggests that the maritime trade of Fuzhou collapsed after its occupation by Wu-Yue, which used Hangzhou and Ningbo as its commercial ports; Quanzhou was left under the control of warlords who actively encouraged South Seas trade and sent tributes of exotic southern goods to Kaifeng. 33-35
- Evidence that in the early Song, Quanzhou built its wealth mostly on its merchants visiting foreign countries rather than the reverse; a majority of recorded visitors to Koryo were natives of Quanzhou. 
- Xie Lv noting the familiar stereotype of maritime Quanzhou in the mid-11th C:  "The population in Ch'iian-chou is dense, yet the valleys are sterile.
	Although one may wish to plow, no virgin land can be found
	But to the south there lies an ocean vast and without bounds.
	And every year people build boats to try to reach foreign lands"
- Before 1087, Fujianese merchants were legally required to register with government offices in Guangzhou or Hangzhou before venturing abroad; in 1087 a *shibosi* office was established in Quanzhou itself. So sees this as the turning point when the already growing Quanzhou maritime sector was able to truly assert itself. 
	- "Finally, due to the unusual institutional constraints under which the South Fukien merchants operated, they were compelled to develop sojourner communities in other major port cities such as Kuang-chou and Ming-chou. Although these communities stemmed from the restrictions imposed on these South Fukienese, the extensive networks they built up along the China coast as a response in turn proved an advantage for commercial expansion once the formal institutional environment became more conducive." 49

Chapter Three - Maritime Trade and Cross-Sectoral Prosperity
- South Fujianese merchants dominated trade with Koryo, although they often departed from Mingzhou; the Japan trade had no single dominant group, but Quanzhou natives were involved, again mostly operating from Mingzhou. 
	- Significance of the sojourner community in Mingzhou for Quanzhou's establishment as an entrepot for north-south trade 
- The foreign community of Quanzhou emerged after 1087 and consisted largely of Arabs/Persians and Srivijayans/Indians. The foreign quarters of Quanzhou were a frequent subject of commentary in the 12th century. 
- To offer a safe haven from Jin raids, an office of Song imperial clan affairs was set up in Quanzhou after 1129; hundreds or thousands of imperial clansmen settled there, drawing large stipends from the government and constituting an important source of luxury demand. 
- Commodities: the most important import class was aromatics, followed perhaps by raw materials like sulfur, timber, and iron/steel. Gold and copper tended to flow out while silver flowed in to China. Decorations like ivory, pearls, amber etc. were also important; silk was certainly exported but So doubts that the *zaytuni* referenced in the Geniza was actually from Quanzhou; likelier this was a name given to Western imitations. Evidence that wine was a very important export, especially since large parts of Fujianese land shifted towards glutinous rice. Certain kinds of pottery shards only found outside China likely came from the vessels used to transport wine. 
	- Ceramics, the most important export, are covered in a separate chapter (8).

Chapter 6: South Fukien as Internally Integrated Region
- Unusual situation that, when the prefectural capital of Quanzhou was moved five km down the flood plain, its old site was preserved as the county capital of Nan'an, allowing for "an urban agglomeration of twin cities with a much higher concentration of urban resources than most other coastal maritime centers," while being administratively easier to manage than a single large metropolis. 137
- Most large-scale manufacturing of ironwares and ceramics took place in the countryside, close to raw materials, implying that the costs of transport to Quanzhou were not a barrier to the profit export of these goods. 
- 153![[land routes quanzhou.png]]
- Substantial contrast with Wickham's picture of major Mediterranean cities: no major workshops at all (foundries or kilns) located in Quanzhou proper, and whatever small craft shops existed within the walls left no trace in surviving sources and are "likely to have been small and insignificant for the local economy." 174
- Conservatively estimates the population of Quanzhou (city) in 1230 at 250,000 people (50,000) households, about half the estimated population of Jinjiang County. This would have made Quanzhou substantially denser than Tang Chang'an or Ming Beijing, and most of its residents must have relied on non-agricultural occupations. 

Chapter 8: Export Ceramics in South Fukien
- The three primary counties participating in Southern Song export ceramics production were Nan'an, Jinjiang, and Dehua, probably in that order. 
- Two kinds of celadon in Song Fujian: imitation Longquan ware, mostly produced in Jinjiang and Nan'an, and *juko seiji* ware popular in Japan and originating in Tong'an. Dehua was best known in the Song and after for distinctive "chalky" and "creamy" white porcelains. Imitations of Jingdezhen style *yingqing* ware were produced almost everywhere (with techniques transferred from Chaozhou).
- Conservatively estimates that around 65,000 people were supported by the ceramics industry in Quanzhou prefecture, about 6.5% of the population. 
- [Ho Chuimei]  believes that this was possible only because of organized migration of potters from Ch' ao-chou, probably initiated and funded by the South Fukien merchants. She also estimates that starting a large-scale production almost overnight in this highly populated region would have required enormous local power to settle a wide range of problems, such as transfers of land and changes in land use and the land tax. All these would call for the involvement of the local elite or highly influential locals." 197
- Chaozhou was a major center of ceramics production oriented towards export through Guangzhou in the early Song, but the industry declined in the late 11th century and vanished by the Southern Song. 198
	- One possible interpretation: Chaozhou ceramics had largely exported by Quanzhou merchants who stopped along the way to clear customs at Guangzhou. Once a customs office was established in Quanzhou itself after 1087, these merchants shifted towards supporting local supply; evidence of technological transfer from Chaozhou to Quanzhou supports this hypothesis. 200
- Mingzhou also did not have a large and integrated hinterland of ceramics production; So sees the huge scale of the South Fujian ceramics industry as a sign that Quanzhou enjoyed a substantially larger portion of maritime trade than its chief rivals, and probably also had a larger share of its trade in the hands of local merchants, who had an incentive to invest heavily in the local economy. 201

Chapter 9: Patterns of Trade
- "Although there is no evidence that the term "South Fukienese" (Minnanren) was in use at this stage, the term "South Fukien" (Minnan) was." 207
- In Sung sources, they were often called "overseas traders," poshang, or occasionally "foreign merchants," fan-shang. Some of them were of foreign origin or were actually sojourner foreign merchants. But there is strong evidence that the term was also used to refer to ethnic Chinese merchants trading in overseas countries." 208
- Fu Tsung-wen's study of cargo labels in a Song shipwreck: "He further shows that labels in the shipwreck strongly indicate the active participation of the imperial clansmen in the overseas trade in both official and private capacities, that the crew was organized in a hierarchy commonly adopted in maritime voyages during the Sung and Yuan times, and that although the ship was operated mainly by the imperial clansmen or  their agents, other merchants were allowed to take part as passengers." 217
- Two basic stages of Minnan overseas trade: in the first, Quanzhou traders largely depended on their contacts with Srivijaya, with Guangzhou as a crucial stop on the way, and Quanzhou served as a transshipment hub for "south seas" products to the north; in the second Quanzhou itself became a main center in larger and more diffuse network, with merchants coming and going along numerous channels radiating from Quanzhou, and with Fujian's own supply of and demand for commodities becoming substantial. 226
	- "The next stage, roughly from the late eleventh to the early thirteenth century, saw a fully developed commercial link between the port and the empire. I argue in Chapter 3 that the overseas trade in Ch'ii.an-chou evolved from a transshipment trade to a mixture of transshipment and export trade. It now was maintained not only by the locals but also by a growing community of foreign traders. Although transshipment of import items to Koryo and other parts of China continued, there emerged a substantial local consumption market for luxury goods in both Ch'uan-chou and Fu-chou after hundreds of imperial clansmen settled in these two cities at the beginning of the Southern Sung. Equally important was the rise of local industries, most notably the export ceramic kilns and cash-crop farming. Both sectors became so commercialized by the early thirteenth century that they may well have constituted some of the basic structural problems behind the financial crisis South Fukien suffered in the first half of that century." 224

10: Formal Institutional Constraints
- Recent research has nuanced the old claim that China lacked legal enforcement of contracts. Especially follows Valerie Hansen's work on contracts in the Song. Examples: the government punished breaches of "red contracts" carrying an official seal; these red contracts were taxed at a rate which grew throughout the dynasty, and the fact people used them despite the cost shows that there were advantages to official recognition; the law stipulated that parties to a contract must be a family head, his representative, or an officially-sanctioned replacement. 
	- "As mentioned in the preceding section, the basic unit of ownership was the household rather than the individual. The parties engaging in a contractual agreement involving household property were representatives of family collectives." 242
- The government took a special interest in enforcing terms of interest for maritime trade, both setting a maximum rate of interest and forcing debtors to pay off their obligations. 

11: Informal Institutional Constraints
- Main question: In Song-era Minnan, "Were there informal institutions that constrained behavior to the extent that they lowered transaction costs in the maritime trade and helped promote the region's economy?" 253
- Contemporary writers seem to have considered Quanzhou people fairly peaceful and harmonious in the 11th and 12th centuries, but observed a great increase in litigiousness in the economic downturn of the 13th century. 258
- On Zhu Xi's influence on later Minnan intellectuals, cites 高令印，陈其芳， 福建珠子学; 小島毅、福建南部の名族と朱子学の普及
- Believes (along with Clark) that in Southern Song Fujian "a transition from descent group to lineage had already taken place"! 273-274
	- The chief example chosen is the Yongchun Lius descended from Liu Zongxiao, which accounts for 13 of the 30-some eminent Yongchun natives documented in the Minshu. This lineage compiled genealogies as early as 1192, with claims that Liu Zongxiao himself had attempted to compile one in 962.  In the Southern Song the clan held annual ceremonies in memory of 4 major deceased branch heads at a Buddhist temple; operated at least 3 ancestral halls; and established an *yizhuang* in 1237. It followed the basic model of Fan Zhongyan's estate but was 5 times larger. 

Conclusion
- Focus on regional difference within imperial institutions: "And when the state power became localized to a considerable extent in some regions, the actually enforced framework became quite different from that found in other places; for example, the maritime law in action in Ch'uanchou diverged from that in Kuang-chou. This led to regional diversities in institutional change. In this sense, in regional perspective institutional change took place not so much in the structure of the formal institutions themselves but in their enforcement characteristics." 285
