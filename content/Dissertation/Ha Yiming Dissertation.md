

#### Chapter 5: A New Era
- Post Altan Khan's 1550 raid: "After the raid, the Ming court embarked on reforms that changed the nature of military service, abandoning coercive conscription in favor of voluntary recruitment. The *weisuo* that was instituted by the dyanstic founder was reduced to a logistical and management unit. Yet as was the case with military reforms after the Tumu crisis, the changes... were not instantaneous -- the Ming did not simply abandon one set of institutions for another. Rather, the foundations for marketized recruitment and payments in silver had been established long before then, and the 1550 military crisis simply accelerated these trends." 240-1
- Summary of divisional army troop types and their leaders on 243: 總兵 - 正軍； 副總兵 - 奇軍； 參將 - 援兵； 游擊 - 游兵. The *qibing* "shock troops" reinforced the main armies and laid ambushes. The "reinforcing troops" *yuanbing* could be called on if necessary. "Mobile troops" were mainly engaged in patrol, but also participated in offensive raids -- I suspect these were the main troops handling overseas missions in Liu's time. These came to supplant the old *weisuo* divisions, and were institutionalized in 1533. However, note that divisional commanders recruited and trained their own troops, which "essentially transformed the divisional army into a private retainer force that was paid for by the state," though presumably answerable to the *xunfu*? 245
	- The battalion commander 總把 led groups of 500 (half a chiliarchy). 
	- "Thus, the superiority of the divisonal army and the garrison comand system over the traditional *weisuo* was not only because they were composed of more elite troops, but also becasue they allowed for more flexible responses by more cohesive military units -- troops can be dispatched rapidly to any area and different circuits can come to each other's aid." 244 
- Acceptance of "housemen" 家丁 (military retainers tied to individual commanders) in the mid-late Ming: "The first major sign of formal acceptance occurred between the Zhengde and Jiajing periods, when the court started granting border military leaders "cultivating honesty land" (*yangliantian* 養廉田)." 253
- Southern garrisons were in an even more deteriorated state by the Jiajing years than those of the north, as recorded by Zhou Wan, Hu Zongxian, Tan Lun, the Chouhai Tubian, etc.  Thus, turn to voluntary recruitment. "Unlike in the north, where the court subsidized recruitment with annual subsidies, in the south officials were left to their own devices to raise funds." This was due to the court's priority being the northern border. Funds for recruits were often raised by silver commutation of milita service (12 liang per annum in Suzhou), or tax surcharges. 256-7
	- In the south, unlike the north, recruits "were never organized into the *weisuo* and there was no hereditary conscription for them." They were organized in battalions (總) of 500 led by a zongba, not by weisuo commanders. "they were similar to the professional (or career) soldiers that started appearing in early modern Europe -- men whose sole occupation was to fight and who commanded high pay due to their military expertise." 258 
- Notes that troop totals were lowered in Zhejiang after the wokou crisis passed, to some 13,500 and then 5,000 in 1570 and after. 258 
- On pay: "Yu Dayou's troops were given 1.5 *liang* of silver as a monthly wage and .45 *shi* of grain as traveling rations when on expeditions. They further received 20 *liang* of silver as a sign-up bonus to purchase equipment and clothing... While the wages were close to the average paid to laborers in the Ming, the sign-up bonus of 20 liang was extremely generous." 261
- Estimates of total northern border military spending: 3.47 million *liang* per year -- 1.47 million *liang* more than the Taicang's revenue. 279 
	- Ray Huang estimated that equal amounts were spent in the south, so total "conservative estimate" of seven million *liang* in the 1550s. (I find this absurdly high.) 
	- NB: In the original Huang is clearly talking about *just* expenses on the northern frontier, and includes local taxation and tribute there.

Ray Huang, "Military Expenditures"
- "The fund-raising programs in the south had the following distinctive features: Firstly, all the funds were locally raised, not to be handled by the Ministry of Revenue. The court either permitted the governors and governorgenerals to exercise discretional power, or, at their requests, authorized their impositions. Secondly, in principle all extra incomes were to be separate from existing revenues. They were to be audited independently. Thirdly, the sources of income were numerous. Managed by provincial officials and army officers, the total amount was never disclosed. Even the auditing by censorial officials failed to present a true picture. And fourthly, many of the new revenues and surcharges originated during the campaign, including a number of nuisance taxes, were never abolished afterwards." 48-9
- "In the course of the protracted campaign most of these impositions gradually transformed themselves into surcharges on landtaxes. The distribution varied from one county to another, and from one prefecture to another. The most common formula was ting-ssu-ťien-lu l68l,or 40% of the fiscal burden to be borne by the pool of registered male adults in the district and the other 60% by landowners. What the central government had failed to legislate was now, at least partially, put into effect by provincial officials, or by governors-general in the form of military ordinances. At the high point, in both Chekiang and South Chihli the surcharges on landtaxes were close to 500,000 ounces of silver41. After the campaign the surcharges were reduced but not totally eliminated42. On account of the campaign miscellaneous taxes in the southeastern provinces mushroomed. Monastic properties in Fukien province had been hitherto tax-exempted; during the campaign they were taxed to pay for the military expenditure. Hilly land in K'uai-chi county, Chekiang province, which had been only lightly taxed before the campaign, was saddled with an added assessment." 50
	- Final example of such a surcharge: "In the last quarter of the century, Yüeh-kang,Fukien province, was finally open for international trade. The income from the maritime trade was administered by military commanders. A tonnage based on the ship's carrying capacity was designated as "water force supplies" ; import duty on merchandise was designated as "ground force supplies". The rates of collection of all these impositions were low. Even the combined annual proceeds at Yüeh-kang were only in the vicinity of 20,000 ounces of silver. Nevertheless, the revenue was fragmented, its management decentralized, and there was no effective auditing."
- Suggests that silver wages for frontier troops tripled in the 16th century, then stayed flat: "Furthermore, due to the widening use of silver, pay for recruited soldiers was also rising sharply during the period. Until the mid-century, six ounces per man per year would have been adequate. In the later part of the century some of the recruited soldiers began to receive 18 ounces; the standard was gradually accepted and remained so in the seventeenth century." 53
- "In the southern provinces ever since the Wo-k'ou campaign the defense installations and their finance were very mudi in the hands of provincial officials. Tax revenues for military expenditures were collected by the governors and governor-generals and disbursed by them. Once started, this practice also extended to the regions which had never been affected by the pirates," like Yunnan. 55
- Ends on an evaluation of Zhang Juzheng. 
	- "With the young emperor under his control, Chang did, indeed, actually exercise the power of appointment. Nevertheless before he proceeded to measures of any significance at all he had to urge his entrusted ministers and governors to submit memorials to the effect; only then in drafting rescripts to these memorials could he have his own wish fulfilled. In a letter to Wang Tsung-mu (!), Imperial Commissioner for the Grand Canal, Chang disclosed: "Your servant now serves a youthful sovereign. He is obliged to be fearfully law-abiding and to go easy with the population. All policies involving grand designs must wait until His Majesty adds a few more years to his august age, and until his precious wisdom has further developed. Only then can suggestions be leisurely presented for his imperial decision.""
- "In summary, we conclude that the rising military expenditures in sixteenth century Ming China were largely caused by the decay of the Wei-so system, and to a lesser extent by the wide circulation of silver and the higher costs of modern warfare. When the effectiveness of the military colonies reached its vanishing point, army logistics should have been reorganized in toto." 59
