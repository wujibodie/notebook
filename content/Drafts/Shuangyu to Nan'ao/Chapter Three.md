In 1566, the Jiajing Emperor passed away, ending a tumultuous 45-year reign. The following year was the first of the new Longqing Emperor, a relatively weak personality who entrusted most governance to a circle of officials who had been his private tutors before his ascension and now were made the core of the Grand Secretariat. A triumverate centered on the old Chief Grand Secretary Xu Jie, brash new arrival Gao Gong, and the youngest Grand Secretary, a man who had never held anything but academic posts, Zhang Juzheng, immediately enacted a number of sweeping reforms to clear away the worst excesses of the previous decades' corrupt administration, when eunuchs, Daoists, and above all a coterie of venal officials raised by the despised Yan Song had controlled the sequestered Jiajing emperor and severely damaged the fabric of the empire. The new regime promised to end the worst abuses and punish their perpetrators while swiftly tackling the crises left by the old order. 

Among the first actions of these reformers was to tackle the lingering problems of piracy on the southeast coast by addressing the root cause: private maritime trade was permitted out of the Zhangzhou port of Haicheng, a newly established county seat built on top of the old smugglers' den of Yuegang. Traditional historiography has dated this "opening of the sea ban" to 1567, the first year of the Longqing reign, which thus becomes the turning point for Ming maritime history as a whole. While the opening of Haicheng was certainly a turning point in Fujian's history, the choice to open it was no sudden whim, but rather built on years of experience and lobbying by a core group of officials close to the ground of Minnan's pirate problem in the early 1560s. These men – led by the successive _xunfus_ Tan Lun and Wang Daokun – were the real architects of Fujian's opening to the sea, and their work to lay the ground for this radical shift bridges the supposedly stagnant and corrupt coastal policy of the late Jiajing reign with the reformist policies of the Longqing and early Wanli reigns. Yet these were not radical reformers, but rather practical military men seeking to broaden a narrow fiscal base to pay for the professional armies they imported from Zhejiang. This chapter will carefully examine the core group of military reformers responsible for reforming Fujian in the last years of the Jiajing era (1561-6), looking at their understanding of the problems facing the coast and their – by no means uniform – proposals for solving them in order to understand the institutions which were in place at the crucial moment of transition when the Fujianese bureaucracy learned to be as reliant on the sea as Fujianese people.

For Fujianese, the 1560s were the very worst period of the Ming pirate crisis. The raids of 1561-3 were unprecedented in scale and left marks deep in the culture and institutions of Fujian. Numerous cities and towns were sacked or besieged, including several of the very largest, and this traumatic experience led to a wave of wall-building and rural militarization which would last through the next centuries. The raids, particularly the pirates' habits of kidnapping women and children, also left deep marks on gender politics in the region. Institutionally, the crisis forced the establishment of a separate regular office of Fujian xunfu in order to manage campaigns along the coast, separate from (though still dependent on) the provincial military of Zhejiang. The military men who ultimately quelled the crisis were drawn from the military bureaucracy of Hu Zongxian in Hangzhou, and relied to great extent on their personal connections in Zhejiang to create fighting forces capable of defeating the pirates. The solutions they devised, while effective, were therefore personalistic and ill-suited for long-term use by a regularized bureaucracy; they were largely replaced by the next round of reformers, but created crucial precedents in a number of areas, above all the separation of silverized and non-monetized military circuits and the acceptance of private maritime trade as a basic staple of the region's economic life.

### Minnan's Exteriorization and Tactical Debates
The historical geographer Li Zhijun has proposed that Fujian went through a continuous process of "exteriorization" (_waixianghua_ 外向化) over the course of late imperial history, as the center of economic gravity shifted from the inland agrarian economy outwards, towards coastal production and maritime trade, and eventually outside the boundaries of the Chinese state.[^19] The dynamics of "exteriorization" also favored the concentration of capital in large organizations for a variety of purposes.[^20] As Yang Peina has argued, the very nature of coastal life demanded a highly mobile, monetized society, particularly as the numerous registered fishing households, with their produce being unsuited for shipment inland, were among the first groups to be taxed in silver instead (the "fish tax" 渔课); soon the right to extract profit from coastal fishing was passed over to local elite clans as intermediaries, with fishing households themselves forced to either be adopted into, hired by, or flee from these elites. Deep-sea fishing itself required large ships, large crews, and long-term investments which further entrenched fishermen further into relationships with wealthy patrons. Inexorable logic of “externalization” due to the embeddedness of the wokou networks in local society, or in other words, the existing reliance of local society on overseas activities. The state was forced into the deep sea in its drive to follow mercantile networks. If the developing deep-sea economy made it necessary for Fujian to project force deep into the ocean, it also increased the risk posed to the entire system by predation from coastal pirates, what He Qiaoyuan called “our inland Japanese.”[^21] 
The intense struggle for resources in the densely populated but economically marginal coastal districts was intensified by the massive piratical raids of the 1550s-60s. One response to the traumatic late Jiajing _wokou_ crisis was a focus on training local militias (_baojia_) and constructing walls and moats around cities and villages alike. After the remnant pirates of the Wang Zhi network had been swept away, these rural military apparati were quickly turned against one another. The famous _xiedou_ inter-clan warfare, which was endemic across Fujian in the Qing dynasty, seems to have its origins in the universal militarization of these years. In the late Ming, before rural competition had taken such an elaborate form, it tended to overlap instead with banditry and piracy, which were themselves hard to distinguish (the same words, _kou_ and _zei_, were used for organized violence on both land and sea). 

( Chen Chunsheng on rural militarization; the newly militarized locales can easily turn towards raiding one another, especially when resources are scarce; per Xu Xiaodong this is where Lin Daoqian got his start, leading his colocals on raids against Zhao'an granaries during a famine. )

As this anecdote shows, no region was more intensely militarized than the border between Guangdong and Zhangzhou, above all the coastal border counties of Raoping and Zhao'an themselves. Another anecdote made famous by Fu Yiling emphasizes the deep embeddedness of "wokou" pirates in the local societies of these regions: in the 1550s, the Zhejiang magistrate Ruan E led his troops in pursuit of pirates who had attacked his jurisdiction, and tracked them all the way to the town of Meiling in Zhao’an. His men wished to massacre the denizens of this town, but Ruan stopped them, saying: “they are pirates in Zhejiang, but in Zhao’an they are our loyal subjects.”[^22] While they were rewarded for heads taken in pitched battle, there was little appetite in the Ming military for massacring civilians in their homes; but that is precisely how the Chao-Zhang pirates treated neighbors from across the provincial border, and how Quanzhou elites wished to see their “piratical” rivals treated in turn. 

The boundary between provinces was often porous for private individuals, who routinely circulated across it as traders, smugglers, itinerant laborers, and raiders, but highly problematic for state bureaucracy, as Fujian and Guangdong belonged to entirely separate military circuits with their own hierarchies and loyalties. The seam between Guangdong and Fujian provinces' spheres of authority was a recognized weak point in the empire's administrative geography, which pirate groups local to the area exploited with increasing sophistication. These pirates' freedom of movement along this nettlesome border was further increased by their deepening familiarity with the oceans beyond the Ming state's reach. Speaking broadly, the anti-wokou strategists of the mid-late Ming all focused on the utilization of geography (passes and chokepoints) and “encircling” tactics to break the bandits on land and force them into the seas, where it was assumed they would die without support from their homelands. 

While Yu Dayou had occasionally suggested naval setpiece battles as a tactic, most strategists assumed _wokou_ superiority on the waves and sought to force them into decisive land engagements. Major debates had to do with whether wokou should be “recruited” or “exterminated:” this has to do with whether maritime activities of given communities will be legitimated or suppressed, and unsurprisingly officials tend to support “recruiting” pirates from their own provinces and “exterminating” outsiders. Over time, this turned into a tendency towards co-optation of the wokou struggles for inter-clan, inter-village, and inter-regional contestation over trade. The loudest voice in favor of destroying whole communities connected to _wokou_ was Lin Xiyuan, who advocated tracking wokou to their home villages in Chaozhou and arresting their relatives; in so doing he may have been voicing his own anger as a victim of raiders from Chaozhou, but also as a competitor: as seen in Chapter One, Lin was himself among the most prominent maritime trading gentry of Quanzhou, specifically accused of smuggling by Zhu Wan before his suicide.

As the doyen of "local expertise" on Fujian coastal society, Lin Xiyuan articulated as a basic principle of _wokou_ policy that pirates relied on links to their supporters on land for survival: “海寇事宜，以元策之，乘風揚帆，往來上下，大兵一集，則望洋遠去追捕稍緩，則乘虚復來，此海寇之所長，難以樸滅者也。然亦謀事者未得其要耳。“ However, as the pirates have homes on land and cannot feed themselves forever at sea, they can be starved by cutting them from their coastal supporters, then surrounded and annihilated when forced to land: then, "even should they break free of their encirclement, their way home will be cut, and the blue waves are no place to live permanently; should they ride into the further oceans, they will be unprepared to enter foreign lands, and can be caught like fish in a pot."[^23] 

Land vs. Naval tactics (Qi vs Yu)
For the most part, Tan Lun's and Wang Daokun's narratives of the 1562-65 campaigns focus on major land battles, including crucial details of civilians rescued and enemies killed or captured. 

Wang appears to have had comparatively little appreciation for the naval side of anti-wokou warfare. In his poem "Ninth Month Peach Blossoms in the Courtyard," Wang describes his anxiety while waiting at camp for news from Yu Dayou's "tower ships": "We’ve waited long in the officers’ tents, bridled and haltered; / the news of the tower-boats’ victory comes late." [^2] Naval battles inherently followed a different rhythm from those onshore, with "victory" often ending in the foe scattering to the waves; the maneuvering of fortresses, passes, sieges, and ambushes did not apply at sea. While Wang yearned for the unlimited expanse of the open Eastern Ocean, it represented a largely hypothetical and misty vastness lacking concrete detail; his writings evince little concern with the actual geography explored by Fujian's sailors beyond the province's shores. At no point did the highest civilian officials in the Tan-Wang circle demonstrate a deep understanding of the overseas maritime networks they were setting free; rather than being far-sighted architects of maritime "shoots of capitalism," their aims appear to have been quite narrowly fiscal. Overseas trade was approved, licensed, and taxed because coastal suppression had simply become too costly. 
In general, Wang's focus appears to almost exclusively have been on supporting Qi's land-based tactical and organizational genius, while he evinced little understanding of Yu's naval campaigns. We see this for instance in Wang's poetry, where he complains about waiting for news from the naval front (幕府羈棲久，樓船戰勝遲). Later, when Yu was assigned to Guangdong under Wu Guifang, he was involved in disputes with Qi and Wang about the prosecution of campaigns against the pirate Wu Ping, who moved across the border between the two provinces: he pleaded with Wang to wait to attack Wu's troops in Zhangzhou until after Yu's navy could burn Wu's ships, but Wang and Qi refused to wait, with the result that Wu was able to escape to the sea and relocate to Nan'ao, where Yu and Qi defeated him only with difficulty and in acrimony, with Yu punished for allowing Wu to escape unharmed even though most of his men were killed or captured.

End Wang Daokun section on his impeachment after a weisuo soldier beat a degree-holder; failure to control the weisuo is the undoing of the mercenary leader.

The actual opening of Haicheng to private trade left curiously few traces in the historical record. While Zhang Xie credits a memorial of Tu Zemin's made in Longqing 1 with inspiring the decision to "open the sea ban," no such memorial is extant, nor have I found any contemporary reference to Tu playing an important role. Instead, the process began with Tan Lun's 1564 memorial initiating a review of trade regulations in Zhangzhou in connection with the creation of a new county there. The reformers who took control of the state with the Longqing accession, Xu Jie and Gao Gong, favored loosening trade restrictions, but did not issue clear orders to Fujian on the matter. The legal institutions which regulated trade out of Haicheng were established gradually, with the first "customs office" being merely a makeshift set of tolls on incoming and outgoing foreign goods set up in 1572.[^4] The more formal institutions which regulated the trade for the last decades of the Ming were promulgated in 1575 under Liu Yaohui. In the absence of clear evidence, it may well be that what happened in Longqing 1 was not so much a "legalization" of maritime trade out of Haicheng as a "decriminalization;" that is to say, a decision to cease enforcing restrictions, but without clear legal guidelines being put into place until later. 
There is real reason to doubt whether Haicheng trade was actually "opened" in any formal sense in 1567. Several other rough contemporaries credit the memorial which caused Haicheng to be opened not to Tu Zemin, but to the Zhangzhou prefect Luo Qingxiao. He Qiaoyuan: "以月港寇亂，因通販禁嚴，請當道弛之." ("Because Yuegang's pirate troubles arose from the severity of the restrictions on commerce, he requested that it be slackened.") Now, there is no trouble in envisioning Luo, as prefect,  petitioning his superior Tu, who then passed his idea on to the throne – except that Luo was in office in Zhangzhou only from 1570-72, and so did not overlap with Tu at all. Similarly, Li Weizhen states that "Prefect Luo Qingxiao asked to open the ban on ocean markets 守羅青霄請開海市禁."[^5] 
"Tu Zemin's three volumes in the _Ming Jingshi Wenbian_ — the only surviving corpus of his official writings — contain not a single reference to maritime trade, customs revenue, or the relaxation of the sea ban. Every text addresses military operations against the pirate Zeng Yiben and the fiscal strain of sustaining those operations. Even when writing to the Fujian gentry to plead for continued military funding, he makes no mention of trade revenue as an alternative to wartime surcharges. The man whom Zhang Xie would credit fifty years later with 'opening the sea ban' left behind a paper trail entirely devoted to closing the sea — to pirates."
- On how exactly Haicheng fit into the broader story of struggle to draw revenue from Fujianese elites: "The "recruiting local elites into producing revenue" formulation** from the outline has been replaced by the sharper "decriminalization" argument, which is good—but the elite dimension needs to remain visible. Who were the local elites who benefited from Haicheng's opening? How did their interests diverge from those of the agrarian gentry who would resist Pang Shangpeng's resurveys?"
- Note also that we really have two sets of voices: the high officials stationed in Fujian, who are almost universally pushed to their limits trying to scrounge resources for basic military efficacy; and the gazetteer writers, local Fujianese who unanimously decry taxation and praise those officials who reduce it. 

One of the earliest clear articulations of the strategy of accomodation with Yuegang's smugglers is a little-remarked-upon proposal by the Longxi native Xie Bin. The proposal survives in the Haicheng County Gazetteer, and while it is undated, contextual evidence suggests it was written around the time of Qi Jiguang's campaigns. What is extraordinary in this document is that Xie begins with an unstinting condemnation of the evils perpetrated by the smuggler-pirates of the Eighth and Ninth Wards, only for his proposed solution to their criminality to be absorption: "pacification" rather than "extermination." In particular, Xie implores his audience (the judge of Longxi county) not to burn the smugglers' fleet of 110 or 120 double-masted boats, but to absorb some of them into the provincial navy (as Xie avers had been done in Guangzhou), and to allow the others to trade abroad freely. The sole regulation Xie proposes is for the shipowners to form a guild -- which he terms a "baojia," using the term brought in vogue by Wang Yangming for rural self-defense organizations -- to ensure that none of their partners trade with Japan, the only country that should be kept off-limits. By absorbing Yuegang and allowing its people to trade in peace, the province can both directly add to its military strength and remove the root cause of piracy and banditry.

Contrast this to Lin Xiyuan's calls to track the pirates to their homes and wipe them out. For a native of Longxi, the smugglers gathered at Moon Harbor and Grass Board, while criminal, have comprehensible motives and are ultimately neighbors and fellow taxpayers, not barbarians, as Xie's consistent nomenclature of "the people of the Eighth and Ninth wards" underscores. Besides framing the smugglers as Chinese subjects rather than "wo," Xie also undermines the claim that maritime trade, even when illegal, is necessarily subversive: as he points out, there were decades when the sea ban had gone largely unenforced, and the people of Yuegang were able to rely on trade without piracy ever becoming a problem; only after the sea ban was tightened with the inspections of Zhu Wan in 1549-50 did these traders become violent. If local authorities judiciously suspended enforcing the letter of the law, and the traders were simply be allowed to resume their activities and keep their two-masted boats, the problem would go away -- even without the laws on the books being changed. Xie's proposal does not require Yuegang to be made a county, the establishment of a customs office, or the bans on large vessels and overseas trade to be rescinded at the central level; it hinges entirely on local officials coming to a tacit accord with the populace and ignoring the ancestral injunctions of the founding emperors.

We can tentatively sketch two phases: a "Longqing phase" from ~1567-72 in which prohibitions were lifted on a trial basis with the goal of reducing piracy by giving Haicheng smugglers an outlet; and a "Wanli phase" from ~1572/5-1620, in which the private trade was institutionalized and tapped as a major source of local government revenue. The key shift between these two phases was the recognition of the burgeoning South Seas trade as a positive solution to the problem of local military finance, rather than as a purely private affair which had become too burdensome to prevent. The institutions tapping Haicheng trade developed on a bottom-up basis, through a series of experiments beginning in the late Longqing years, before ossifying and finding official approval in 1575, which is arguably the best year to date the formal "opening" of the sea ban. The recognition of the potential for the state in taxing Haicheng rather than merely leaving it be would have to await a fiscal expert to replace the military experts who governed Fujian in the 1560s.
