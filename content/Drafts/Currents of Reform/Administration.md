The Role of the Xunfu 
- As the major protagonists of my narrative will include three Xunfus, it is prudent to define clearly how I understand that role. For simplicity's sake I will refer to this position as Governor, because it is a reasonable neat translation for the highest civil administrator of a province. Europeans of the time also called these men either "Viceroys" or "Visitors;" in either case, they precisely identified that these figures' awesome authority was delegated to them directly from the emperor himself (at least in theory). In the early Ming the role of surveillance commissioner had been an extraordinary position assigned only to deal with specific problems, but i came to be seen as a more regular position after 1500, particularly during the long reign of the disinterested Jiajing emperor, when the power struggles between high officials came to center on control over the organs of promotion and demotion, of which the surveillance commissioners were a powerful part. It is only after around 1550 that we see continuous appointment of Xunfus to Fujian, meaning it was in the mid-Jiajing years that this formerly discretionary position morphed into something like a governorship, with the expectation that this position at the top of the provincial administration should always be filled. The rise of the provincial governor was thus intimately linked with the development of the Grand Secretariat as the primary executive organ in government. The Governor was the most direct intermediary between local administrations and the central court. By the first year of Wanli these trends were already mature, but Zhang Juzheng was the first (perhaps only) figure to seize on the full potential of the surveillance commissioners to manage centrally-coordinated campaigns in individual provinces. 
- Besides linking the provincial administrations to the center, the xunfus also played the role of tying the smaller units within a province together. The "survey" (*xun*) in their title was originally quite literal, and like Carolingian royalty, the Governors were supposed to constantly roam their domains rather than sitting behind the walls of their capital. The imperative to constantly check and compare the workings of prefectural and county administrations obviously put the Governors in a position to judge locally-enacted reforms and champion those deemed successful. 
- Early in the Ming Dynasty, the court attempted to solve the problem of tax evasion in Nan Zhili mainly through periodic tax forgiveness, but as the situation continued to worsen, they began sending officials to investigate. Zhou Chen was the first such official to make thoroughgoing reform proposals. At the same time other officials were sent to Jiangxi, Huguang, Zhejiang, Henan/Shanxi, and Beizhili/Shandong. This was the beginning of the 巡撫 system (or at least of permanent assignment of xunfu to each province), and the initial remit of this set of officials was focused on the investigation and reform of the provincial tax situation.
- Ray Huang, Taxation:  “One of the functions of the surveillance commissioner was to check and rectify abuses in tax administration. As an extension of this, some surveillance commissioners set up ground rules for collecting silver payments as service levy. In so doing they were virtually acting as tax legislators. Two outstanding surveillance commisioners who did this in the sixteenth century were Pan Jixun (1521-95) in Guangdong and Pang Shangpeng (_jinshi_ 1553) in Zhejiang, both of whom were instrumental in devising the Single Whip Reform." 27

## The Zhang Reform Collective
Zhang Juzheng, Pang Shangpeng, Geng Dingxiang, and Liu Yaohui were contemporaries who knew one another well from early on in their careers; Zhang was the youngest by age but most senior in office, having passed his jinshi in Jiajing 26, while Pang and Liu both passed in JJ 32 (with Zhang as examiner!) and Geng in JJ 35. There are letters between the four of them from early days, but Liu, Geng, and Zhang each reference friendly face-to-face relations with one another as young officials in Beijing (and as fellow natives of Huguang), while Pang seems to have been more socially distant, and instead garnered respect for his achievements and writings while stationed in Zhejiang at the end of the Jiajing reign. When Zhang Juzheng took power as effective regent in Wanli 1, he immediately promoted Liu and Geng to high office, and called Pang back from exile in Wanli 5 to take on a sensitive and specialized mission, namely the extension of the Single Whip policy, which Pang had previously championed, to Fujian. When Pang criticized Zhang's choice to "夺情," he was ousted and his work in Fujian was continued by Geng, one of Zhang's most ardent loyalists (and, seemingly, one of few real friends).
