The development of a provincial military-fiscal system reliant on Haicheng customs meant that Pang's Single Whip reforms were not capable of fulfilling the basic requirement of increasing tax remittances to the central treasury, nor were they needed to. Fujian became capable of paying for its own defense, and this was enough to satisfy the center, especially as Fujian had never been a major source of tax income in the first place. The fact that it continued to bring in little revenue was not seen as a problem, despite the fact that -- as Liu Guanglin has extensively argued -- the Southern Song precedent shows that Fujian commercial taxes *could* have become a major money maker for the state if fully embraced. This doesn't mean that Pang's reforms had no importance; later sources frequently credit Pang for "making the peoples' lives easier" 便民. But they largely did so by *decreasing* the total tax burden and making it simpler to pay. They probably helped make Fujian a more stable and less fractious place, contributing to the economic upswing brought about by stabilization of foreign trade. What the Single Whip reforms in Fujian could *not* have done, however, is precisely what they're often credited with in the historiography, namely increasing China's overall demand for silver, because they did not actually increase the level of silver taxation demanded of Fujian at all! At best, everything from Tan Lun's reforms to Geng Dingxiang's can be seen as a process of central state accomodation with Fujian increasing dependence on its own resources, developing a military economy that could effectively defend itself and that was largely cut off from extraprovincial disbursements. This Fujian military became exceptionally effective, launching campaigns well outside Ming territory and successfully maintaining peace in Fujian's waters up until the rise of the VOC -- which, however, the Minnan state complex *also* succeeded in vanquishing, repeatedly, once the logic of local control had been pushed to its limit under the Zhengs. I believe therefore that everything which followed Zheng Zhilong's ascendance as merchant-admiral, including the conquest of Taiwan, can be traced back to the accomodation with private trade as the engine of the Fujianese military economy, and the institutional drift -- even compared to other maritime, silverized provinces -- with this compromise entailed. In this story, the traditional hallmarks of 1567 and the Single Whip fade to the status of *confirmations* of a unique arrangement which developed more gradually and from more local roots than has previously been suggested.
