## Chapter Four: Contradictions on the Coast

"In my home of Quanzhou on the sea, a boaster once told me: 'A huge fish entered this harbor and got trapped when the tide went out. We called together dozens of people, who all picked up knives and axes and sat on the fish's back, hacking to our hearts' content. We must have cut off a hundred tons, but the fish acted as if nothing had happened, and when the tide came in it simply left again.' This actually goes to show it was a rather small fish. If it was borne by the tide into harbor, that means it could fit in the harbor; such a fish isn't really very big at all." -  Li Zhi, _A Book To Burn[^1]_

Once the "Wanli Coup" was complete and Zhang Juzheng had consolidated his total control of the government, he set about appointing competent loyalists to key tasks throughout the empire. Zhang's first choice as Xunfu of Fujian was Liu Yaohui, a native of Linwu County in far southern Huguang who had taken his *jinshi* degree under Zhang and formed part of Beijing's "Chu clique" in the 1560s. Liu, stationed in Fujian from 1572-6, was the man tasked with laying the groundwork for Zhang's projected reforms in this troublesome province. It is therefore significant that his work there had nothing to do with the "Single Whip" as such, but was rather centered on the more fundamental problem of bringing lasting peace to the maritime frontier.

Liu Yaohui’s time in this office serves as a compressed window into the complex fiscal and military crises facing Fujian after the Jiajing years. The early Wanli years were a unique moment in Ming institutional history: the office of xunfu had become fully regularized as the highest administrative position at the provincial level, but still retained its original character as a censorial post with direct links to the emperor, while the role of “emperor” was played by a member of the civil official class with close personal ties to many of the officials he appointed. In some ways this makes the correspondence between Zhang Juzheng and his appointed xunfus comparable to the secret memorial system which historians of the Qing have exploited so successfully to investigate frank private communications between local officals and the emperors.[^2]

#### Biographical Preliminaries

Liu won his jinshi degree in the capital examinations of 1553, with Xu Jie and Zhang Juzheng as his examiners. As a young official in the capital Liu became close to his contemporaries Pang Shangpeng (jinshi ‘53) and Geng Dingxiang (‘56), forming something of a clique under the patronage of the prodigal Zhang (actually the youngest of the four men). Liu, Pang, and Geng served successively as xunfus of Fujian, administering the province for virtually the whole decade of Zhang’s regency (1572-82). It is no coincidence that these close confidantes, who began their careers in opposition to the venal government of Grand Secretary Yan Song, later cooperated in governing Fujian. The evidence suggests that Zhang chose to appoint his three proteges one after the other to that province precisely because conditions there required close, personalistic cooperation with the center, and he trusted the men whom he had bonded with during the battles of his early career above mere subordinates. Regardless of their common party origins, however, there were substantive differences in the three xunfus specialties and political orientations, as well as in their specific tasks in Fujian. Liu Yaohui, in particular, was tapped to clear the ground and make Fujian safe for reform, so to speak, by tackling the disastrous state of local finances and military readiness, and his time there was thus characterized by pragmatic exploration of possibilities rather than imposition of any particular policies. 

Liu’s first sally into factional politics was his participation in a 1558 mass impeachment of Hu Zongxian on the basis of “idling his troops and indulging the pirates,” and further attacked several of Hu’s subordinates for falsely claiming rewards for fictional battles. The censor sent to investigate these charges, none other than Pang Shangpeng, delivered a devastating report condemning Hu for corruption, luxury, and 'nurturing bandits.' The emperor vigorously rejected all charges, and Liu was shortly thereafter dismissed from office.[^3] Liu would spend 7 years in his home village of Linwu before being recalled to the bureaucracy around 1570 and serving in various largely ceremonial roles. 

When Zhang took charge of government after 1572, he fast-tracked the careers of several of his close associates. After Liu was rehabilitated, he spent just one year in a mid-ranking administrative position, as vice-prefect of Shuntian prefecture, before skipping several ranks in his sudden promotion to xunfu; he thus took up the highest provincial office with virtually no previous experience in military or fiscal affairs. This was a highly unusual rise and reflects that Liu must have been hand-picked for loyalty to Zhang as well as personal qualities – perhaps the very moral rigor which he had displayed in his attack on Hu Zongxian. Whatever his perceived personal merits (and Liu did prove himself an earnest and capable administrator), there could have been no doubt that he was Zhang Juzheng’s man, and throughout his time in Fujian Liu was in direct communication with Zhang through both official and private channels. While Liu was an independent-minded official with views of his own, he seems to have genuinely endeavored to implement Zhang’s reformist vision for the province as part of their shared vision for national renewal. In order to analyze the unique problems Fujianese coastal society posed within the broader picture of late Ming reform, we will look first at the problems Zhang commanded Liu to solve, and how Liu came to understand and explain the knot he was tasked with untying. 

While Tan Lun, Wang Daokun, and their associates had succeeded in guiding Fujian through the worst of the late Jiajing piracy crisis, they had still left much unfinished, and by 1572 the contradictions of their policies were readily apparent. In particular, remnant "wokou" continued to threaten the waters off Zhangzhou, while the emergency coastal defense measures put in place in the 1560s were too expensive and burdensome to continue indefinitely. Liu's core mandate as Fujian xunfu was to solve the wokou issue for good in coordination with his partners in Guangdong and Zhejiang, and to thereby place coastal defense on a firm fiscal footing that would allow Fujian to resume remittance of silver (earned, for example, from new maritime taxes at Haicheng) to Beijing rather than requiring subsidies. Liu inherited a very stark dilemma: in order to achieve fiscal stability, he would need bring lasting peace to the coast; but to do this required expensive and ambitious programs of defense and pursuit. The solutions to this dilemma, I will argue below, came largely from a measured embrace of the private coastal economy, which was routed through legible and taxable channels, and an unprecedented extension of state power into the "outer oceans" to ensure this newly licit activity stayed within accepted bounds. The most dramatic such extensions were new military and diplomatic missions to Penghu, Taiwan, and the Philippines, managed directly by Liu, which emerged in response to the extension of private commercial activities to these regions, and presaged their growing integration into the Hokkien world. 

## Part One: Diagnosing the Crisis

Our understanding of Liu's tenure as governor of Fujian is enhanced by the unusually detailed records of his official correspondence as xunfu reproduced in an 18th century reprint of Liu's collected writings. The collection includes not only numerous memorials submitted by Liu to the throne, but also the initial imperial edict appointing Liu to his position. This edict (certainly penned by Zhang Juzheng) sets a very clear scope of action for Liu's tenure

	"It is decreed: Fujian has lately been troubled by dwarf [Japanese] pirates and mountain bandits. Thus we now command you to go forth and oversee military affairs. You are charged with the extermination of the bandits of mountains and seas. You shall tour the prefectures and counties in turn, organizing militias (tuanlian) and community defense (baojia) units and preparing walls and moats. You shall not allow collusion between mountains and seas, nor allow the pirates to land on the shores. You may dispose all military matters involving preparation and adjustment of soldiers, horses, supplies, and salaries as seems fit. All relevant officers (the Regional Commander, Vice Regional Commander, Brigade Generals, and Defense Commanders, as well as all prefectural, sub-prefectural, county, garrison (wei), and battalion (suo) officials) shall be under your command. You shall recommend, audit, and evaluate them according to precedent. Should any civil officials of fifth rank or lower, or military officials of third rank or lower, dare to disobey or obstruct your orders, you have the authority to directly arrest, interrogate, and punish them. Furthermore, you are authorized to follow military law in investigating and punishing any *yamen* personnel, as well as soldiers or civilians, who dare to flee from battle or deliberately delay deployment. 
	In matters relating to the two prefectures of Chaozhou in Guangdong and Zhangzhou in Fujian which involve the Supreme Commander of Liangguang, you shall consult and deliberate with him. If the bandits are numerous and our troops insufficient, coordinate with the Supreme Commanders of Nan-Gan and Jiang-Zhe. If the momentum tips in the bandits' favor, you must work together with one heart and are forbidden to make distinctions between your jurisdictions. For any matters not discussed in this edict, you shall consult the archive of memorials approved by the Ministry of War and act as you consider the situation demands. Work to pacify the Japanese pirates and bring peace to the people; then you will have fulfilled this commission. If, however, your management is improper, the responsibility will rest with you. Take heed and respect these orders!"

Liu's responsibilities in Fujian were chiefly military, and specifically involved logistical and administrative reform rather than strategic oversight of a single campaign. He was tasked above all with inspecting and reorganizing Fujian's entire military hierarchy, from local militias to the high officers, to bring a lasting peace to the beleaguered coast. The language of the edict makes it clear that "exterminating" the bandits in pitched battle was a lesser priority compared to reorganizing local society to be capable of self-defense and immune to "collusion" with the pirates. In his memorial of thanks for his new appointment, Liu confirmed that he understood his core responsibilities to be the simultaneous overcoming of the wokou raids and fiscal shortages. “Now if we wish to wipe out the invading bandits, our military supplies are insufficient; yet we must also sympathize with the sufferings of the exhausted civilian victims.” 

In addition to the official edict promulgated in the emperor’s name, Liu also received private instructions in the form of a frank letter by Zhang Juzheng: 
"For several years Fujian has been at peace, so the responsible authorities have pursued the empty name of restraint and frugality, to the point that they've slackened in the face of real danger; their errors have been substantial. What I ask of you is to look at everything under your authority step by step. General Yu, that old scoundrel, has a weak will and is not fit to be re-employed. Only a young and vigorous general can handle these matters. Mr. Hu has been in Fujian for a long time, has displayed some merits in war, and is keen on making a name for himself; if you tie him to you with sincere trust and excite him with talk of loyalty and righteousness, he will surely be able to accomplish something. The deployment of soldiers depends on quickly-changing circumstance, and is hard to judge from afar; thus everything east of the Ou mountains, I entrust to you."[^4] 

By contrast to the impersonal and imperative tone of the edict of appointment, here Zhang displays his sympathy with Liu’s difficult task while laying blame on previous office holders in Fujian. Since Fujian has been relatively calm for a number of years, local officials have slacked defenses in the name of false “frugality;” thus Zhang frames Liu’s real task as personally inspecting each individual military installation to ensure their readiness. Zhang advises working around the famous kebing general Yu Dayou, an “old scoundrel” whose will has slackened, and instead entrusting key tasks to Hu Shouren, a veteran officer in the naval administration; this represents a call to return to normal military hierarchies in preference to personalized professional armies. Finally, Zhang admits that his position in Beijing is too distant to judge the military realities in Fujian, and ensures that he will trust everything in the region to Liu’s own judgement. 

In his personal response to Zhang’s letter, Liu identified the problems he was to solve as mostly having to do with the entrenched power structures in Fujian. His conception of his two-part task at this early stage was to pacify and manage Fujian's high officials, and to "sharpen blades" and defend against the exterior: 方求寧長吏，保元素以内克；更督備材官，厲鋒錂而外禦.[^5] 

From their correspondence, it is clear that Zhang and Liu agreed on seeing the moral slackness of both civil and military officials as the chief ill afflicting Fujian, with the fiscal shortages and outside invasions arising from that central weakness. To what extent did these early expectations match Liu’s diagnoses after actually inspecting Fujian?

The fiscal and military problems which Zhang identifies were the direct aftereffects of the solutions to the previous decade’s problems. Fujian’s coastal garrisons (weisuo), legacies of the demonetized corvée economy of the Ming founding which had shriveled to near-uselessness by the 16th century. The main current of military reform in the Wokou crisis of the 1550s-60s had tended to route around these derelict institutions by empowering professionalized mercenary armies led by charismatic reformers like Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou. These professional forces had convincingly triumphed in the Zhejiang anti-wokou campaigns of the 1560s, when multiple such forces spearheaded coordinated campaigns under the tenacious Supreme Commander Hu Zongxian. Following the destruction of the major pirate base at Shuangyu, an island in the Zhoushan archipelago, a portion of the professional armies had shifted to Minnan to pursue anti-piratical actions there, and became ensconced as the so-called “guest armies” (kebing 客兵). While effective in battle, these troops were problematic for several reasons: their foreign cultural background caused clashes with locals where they were stationed; because they existed outside normal Ming military structures, there was a danger of their being more loyal to their commanders than the dynasty as a whole; and most importantly, they demanded their pay in silver, meaning that keeping them in a state of permanent readiness would require the province to source the requisite funds for their pay. 

The “guest armies” led by Qi and Yu were originally brought in Fujian in 1563, during the worst period of “wokou” depredations in the province, and they proved their superiority over the local garrison troops in a series of brilliantly successful campaigns. At that time of need, the silver needed to pay the kebeing was extracted from local society via a series of creative levies. Tan Lun, who served as Xunfu from 1563-5, pioneered the use of extraordinary silver levies to pay his mercenaries’ salaries, normally requisitioning them in the form of surcharges to the ordinary annual taxes calculated as fractions of the main tax categories: the dinger misi ()…. And the taxes on monasteries. It was precisely these extraordinary silver levies which the previous xunfu administrations, in the relative peace of the Longqing years, had attempted to do away with in the name of “frugality.” However, doing away with the stipendiary professionals would put the hard-won pacification of the coast at risk. Liu's challenge was therefore to secure the coast in order to cut his silver expenses, while simultaneously needing to spend silver in order to secure the coast. 

Liu made his first comprehensive report from the ground in a memorial of autumn 1573, titled “Timely Considerations on Military Rations For the Protection of the Coasts.” The most pressing issue Liu identified was an immediate shortfall of close to a third of the military budget: he estimated that the province would be some 40,000 taels short of their 150,600 tael expenses for coastal defense even if all emergency surcharges continued to be collected in full. 

These were paltry sums compared to the several million taels spent annually on northern border defense, but they represented an abnormal gap in the expected accounting. As discussed in Chapter One, Fujian had long been a province which sent silver surpluses to the central treasury, and so a decades-long reversal of that flow, in the form of central subsidies to the provincial defense budget, would have seemed an irregular and easily patchable leak in the great vessel of imperial finances. 

Liu proposed to close the gap without new levies on an already exhausted populace by temporarily withholding certain grain and salt taxes (otherwise earmarked for the northern frontier) as well as requesting a direct transfer of 30,000 taels from the imperial treasury. He concurrently advised to avoid or reverse cost-cutting measures, restoring 3,000 recently demobilized kebing mercenaries along with five decommissioned warships per coastal squadron. These solutions were obviously stop-gap and unsatisfying, as Liu requested that Fujian’s defenses be maintained at the cost of the central treasury, exactly what he was supposed to foreclose, while temporarily reinstating the professional soldiers he intended to demobilize, but they were the only solutions available before Liu’s more comprehensive inspections and reforms could be carried out. Budget cuts were impossible because at the time of writing two pirate fleets were poised to strike Fujian from just outside its coastal waters: those of Lin Feng (林鳳) off "Ryukyu" (琉球; in this case actually designating Taiwan) and Zhu Liangbao across the Guangdong border. 

  

By late 1573 Liu had developed a clear diagnosis of the bind facing Fujian: as Xunfu, he needed to simultaneously reform the provinces’ fiscal and military structures without upsetting their overall balance, since both conditioned the other. The 40,000 tael shortfall was not catastrophic relative to the total tax income of the province, but it was large enough that it could not be dealt with either by “economizing” or by adding further surcharges to the already over-tapped land taxes. What Liu really needed was some new source of silver revenue outside the existing confines of the fiscal infrastructure. 

The remaining memorials sent by Liu to the court during his tenure in Fujian largely focus on two sets of issues: setting Fujian’s local defenses on a firm fiscal basis, and revising the military framework to ensure real readiness and self-sufficiency. In addition to these core concerns, however, Liu also looked for novel sources of revenue which could be collected without further burdening the exhausted taxpayers. 

  
  

## Part Two: Stabilization

In the course of his time in Fujian, Liu explored four distinct paths to escaping the fiscal-military bind: logistical de-silverization, minting of copper coin, centralizing collection of maritime customs, and a comprehensive reorganization of the military hierarchy. It should be noted that, despite being governor of the whole province, Liu split his time largely between Fuzhou and Zhangzhou, and his memorials deal almost exclusively with the four maritime prefectures. Each of his four reforms paths entailed a reckoning with the specific political economy of the coastal regions. In one way or another, Liu’s solutions would all carry further the logic of detente with Minnanese seafaring society which began with the end of the haijin. Rather than suppress coastal communities, Liu worked towards enlisting maritime society in its own defense. At the same time, Liu moved against some of the major policies pursed by the administrations of neighboring Guangdong and Zhejiang, in particular by committing to a careful and partial embrace of foreign trade with the silver-rich "western oceans," alongside policies designed to reduce his administration's reliance on silver. Liu's overall goals were a mapping out of legible boundaries for the maritime world as part of a process of selective and sustainable professionalization of provincial armies. 

#### The Limits of Silverization

Various of Liu’s memorials deal with the problems introduced by the extensive silverization of provincial finances, above all the constant temptation which silver flows offer for embezzlement in all directions. Quite against the seeming spirit of the times, Liu repeatedly called for de-silverizing critical parts of the provincial administration.This does not mean that Liu engaged in anti-silver polemics per se, but rather that he recognized that what was an effective mechanism of payments in certain settings caused problems in others. Nor does Liu seem to theorize silver as being itself morally corrupting: rather, it exposed the existing corruption prevailing in Fujian. It was due to that underlying corruption that Liu resisted relying on the market to source key material goods like horses, grain, and weapons; the most critical services also needed absolutely reliable sourcing. Silver, for all its convenience, required a moral foundation which was already hollow in the case of the weisuo, and revealed the soldiery themselves as a greater threat than the pirates: “I fear that the troubles of this place are not on the seas, but lie within the army and soldiers.” 

In one early memorial, Liu argued that a major reason for the poor performance of the weisuo soldiers was their constant state of near-starvation due to their chronically insufficient grain supplies. Given that these supplies are precisely what the provincial silver levies were supposed to pay for, Liu concludes that huge portions of these levies were diverted or embezzled, such that little grain ever actually reached the garrison troops. Liu breaks the main silver levies into two types: the universal ding er mi si surcharge on the normal land tax, which brings in 40,000 taels. Liu thus confirmed that, as of the early Wanli years, Tan Lun’s silver surcharges were still in operation and collecting the expected quantities of silver, but these “extraordinary” levies were actually dwarfed by various other silver-denominated taxes (the grain tax fee, the materials fee, the fishing tax, commercial tolls, granary commutation, militia archer commutation, and temple rents 糧剩料剩魚課商税倉折弓兵寺祖等), mostly dating to before the wokou crisis, which collectively valued 120,000 taels. These levies had mostly begun to be collected in peacetime, around the Zhengde and early Jiajing reigns; they were "not levied because of maritime troubles" (原不必海上有事而後加派者也). The implication here was that Fujian had long had the capacity to collect substantial silver revenues, and so there was no need to add new burdens on taxpayers. Instead, the core problem was that existing taxes were not being collected or distributed properly. 

Liu traced this collapse directly to the practice of silver commutation. "The coastal guards have, since the founding ancestors, maintained granaries where people delivered grain in kind, with regular annual stipends," he explained. "Recently, officials have casually followed gentry and commoner requests and proposed silver commutation (改折), causing all granaries to be completely emptied." The mechanism was straightforward: "Once silver commutation begins, it opens the door to embezzlement—all the personnel handling receipts freely engage in peculation and skimming" (且一經折價，便啟侵門，各該左右經收人員，恣其乾沒侵尅). Granary officials had become "just empty names in the registers" (徒寄空名於簿內). Grain could not be stolen without physically removing sacks from warehouses; silver vanished into ledgers.

The dynamics of lower officials' weakness in their local assignments compounded this problem. Local officials had learned to treat non-enforcement as a political asset, "using the pretense of 'maintaining calm' to indulge their laziness" (假安靜之名，以遂其怠荒之便). They were "not daring to take on resentment, concerned only with preserving their reputation" (事不敢於任怨，志惟在於全名). Some accepted bribes; others were "entangled with personal connections" or "bent the law for the powerful" (或牽情於近習，或屈法於勢豪). Silver commutation, beyond facilitating outright theft, restructured incentives so that enforcement became politically costly while non-enforcement was rewarded.

An example of this sort of malfeasance was reported by Chen Maoren, who served as 经理 of Quanzhou. Chen says that when he was ordered to audit Dehua's granary, he discovered both a large shortfall and evidence of unreported sales (for silver). The excuse was given that rice stored for long in Fujian was liable to be eaten by pests if not properly prepared, but Chen believed that this had long served as a cover for embezzlement; he thus suggested switching the granaries to early-harvest grain, which is less vulnerable to pests. 

Liu ordered that provincial granaries recently changed over to cash commutation must immediately revert to “tax paid in kind” (悉復本色徵納), while all surplus provincial silver already held in local treasuries is to be spent at once on restocking grain. The Ministry of Revenue duly authorized these reversions and ordered a thorough audit of military granaries. This measure was not a temporary stopgap, but seems to have lasted well into the Wanli reign: as late as 1610, a Magistrate Wang Silun of Fuqing (near Fuzhou) submitted a memorial running to “thousands of words” which requested that local grain taxes dedicated to military granaries be commuted to silver. The fact that Wang would need to expound on such length at this issue suggests that local weisuo supplies still relied on direct transfers of tax grain, and had done so for long enough for alternatives to be forgotten.

Besides the damage which excessive silverization did to the provisioning of basic supplies, it also proved a temptation for embezzlement which damaged military discipline. Reading Liu’s memorials, one gets the impression that the silver-hungry _kebing_ officers were the most vulnerable to this particular sin, though other sources frequently suggest that the underpaid _weisuo_ officers and their men were at least as venal. In any case, Liu used accusations of embezzlement to cashier and remove officers and even entire units of the Zhejiang forces, which aligned well with his overall goal of de-emphasizing their use along the Fujian coast. 

 In the Ming, postal relay stations were a standalone fiscal category with their own separate system of corvee requisitioning. Postal service was often considered especially onerous, due to both the consistently burdensome costs of provisioning horses or boats and the risk of more unexpected expenses when especially important or numerous visitors needed to be feasted and taken care of. Thus, the replacement of this hated levy by equalized silver surcharges spent on professional workers was among the most celebrated advances of the whole labor commutation policy. In Fujian, however, Liu found that this shift produced a system which was fundamentally inferior to what had come before. In a 1575 memorial titled “Investigating Failings in the Postal Service,” Liu complains that funds earmarked for workers or supplies are frequently embezzled or misspent, making basic mail service unreliable. Liu thus requested a reversion to full corvee management of the postal relays. One of the main benefits of the postal commutation was supposed to have been "equalization" of burden across the responsible counties, where beforehand the relay stations had been the special responsibility of only the largest landholders, but for Liu this was actually a benefit: he specifically requested to return this duty to wealthy households, returning to the logic of unequal burdens by wealth class, in order to lower the surcharge silver payments owed by poorer families.  

In numerous memorials submitted across Liu's whole time in office, he railed against the moral and practical limitations of the silver economy. Undergirding all of these critiques was not a thorough rejection of silver as such, but the basic dilemma we have already identified: Liu needed both to source sufficient silver to pay his professional soldiers and to reduce the overall tax burden on a populace perceived as exhausted and exploited. By attempting to reduce the _civilian_ administration's reliance on silver disbursements, Liu reserved the limited funds of silver for the most crucial military purposes (particularly wages for elite mercenaries). This selective de-silverization, intended to put the coastal armies on a sustainable fiscal basis, risked creating a “dual-track” economy, where sectors adjacent to the military were flush with silver while others remained tied to in-kind payments or labor requisitions. By the late Ming, it was common wisdom that these sorts of traditional tax structures tended to squeeze the civilian economy, while state use of money could actually stimulate it; the reversion to non-monetary fiscal means was by no means desirable in itself, either to Liu or to Zhang Juzheng. Thus, besides cost-cutting measures, Liu also turned to new sources of money income for his administration. 

#### Copper and Seigniorage

Late in Liu's tenure, he participated in a major push to give the state another monetary lever to pull: in this case, copper cash. Copper coins had been the main low-level money in the Song and Yuan dynasties, and still flowed quite abundantly in Fujian's private markets, but their use was discouraged for most of the Ming Dynasty as a legacy of Hongwu's suppression of agrarian contact with markets and botched attempt to enforce paper currency as the chief means of payment in his empire. Small quantities of bronze coinage were minted in various reigns, but never in sufficient quantities to meet domestic demand, and in any case they tended to flow abroad (often these coins were actually meant exclusively as payment for tributary gifts). By the mid-late 16th century, however, there were calls to expand state minting as part of an ambitious program to facilitate private commerce. In 1576, Zhang's court ordered widespread minting of new, high-quality bronze coins across the empire as part of an "expansionary monetary policy" intended to help shore of the treasury's deficits while "benefit[ting] the people."[^6] This policy dated back to proposals of Tan Lun's in the Longqing years, made as part of broader debates regarding the empire's conversion to a silver standard. 

In a memorial dated 6/19, 1576, Liu forwarded the provincial accounts for the first run of the new mint in Fuzhou.[^7] Using 1,265 jin of brass (bought, along with other materials, for 9.4 taels of "retained silver" 存留銀 held in county treasuries), the mint struck 10,400 coins worth 10.4 taels, yielding a clear profit of 0.97 taels; thus, according to Liu, a 100,000-coin run would net nearly 10 taels and a million coins almost 100 taels. The profit of almost 1 tael per 10,000 coins (or around 1/10 by face value) represented pure seignorage, suggesting that coinage could be a major source of local revenue as long as the people accepted the coins produced. (In order to gain that acceptance, Liu elected to cast coins slightly heavier than the Ministry of Revenue's model (1.5 _qian_ versus 1.4) in the hopes that this would make them heavy enough to deter private casting in Fujian's notoriously counterfeit-filled monetary climate.) Encouraged by these signs of success, Liu requested permission to continue casting larger and larger runs, with their seigniorage profits to be recycled as capital for still larger mintings. Liu imagines that this “mother-and-son” circulation will expand indefinitely, supplying both provincial expenses and the private needs of the market without resorting to extra levies. 

As beautiful as this vision must have seemed to Liu, it was clearly not a viable solution to Fujian's shortfalls. By contrast to the military budget, the seigniorage sums here are almost pitifully small: profits of 100 taels per million coins were clearly not sufficient to plug the gaps in the budget, even without considering the inevitable fact that mass-scale minting would quickly cause the imbalance between face and body value to shrink, if not reverse altogether. But perhaps the most important thing in this case was simply to prove that minting was not *unprofitable,* such that at least some existing expenses could be shifted from silver to coin without cost to the state. Had the program of coinage succeeded, it might well have helped increase Fujian's tax base and alleviate the fiscal scarcity it faced as long as silver remained the chief mean of payment. However, even in the best-case scenario this would be a fairly long-term solution, and did little to fill the immediate deficit. Doing so would require tapping an entirely new source of silver, outside the traditional tax base. 

#### Maritime Customs
If seignoriage from minting was insufficient to supply military expenses, and in any case began only at the end of Liu's tenure in Fujian, what was the most important measure which Liu oversaw to fill his immediate gap? The answer, and by some distance, was reorganized and stabilized maritime customs centered on the newly opened foreign trade center of Haicheng. While foreign trade had been legalized at that port in 1567, it was Liu Yaohui who implemented a stable set of maritime taxes there and oversaw their steady increase, helping plug the 40,000 tael gap in the military budget which it was his main goal to fix. 

In Wanli 3, after first curbing silver expenses to keep the immediate deficit under control, Liu elected to cut tolls on domestic coastal shipping (previously levied at Quanzhou and Fuzhou) and consolidate all maritime taxes at Haicheng. Thereby Liu shifted his administration's focus to tapping overseas shipping revenue as the main new source of silver while leaving the local shipping and fishing industries, crucial for basic livelihoods and food security in this grain-deficit region, untroubled. A 1575 memorial explains that he simultaneously removed the commercial customs at Fuzhou’s Nantai as well as Quanzhou’s boat tax, both of which were levies on domestic commerce. His memorial was approved, and the emperor (or rather, Zhang Juzheng) praised his diligence and loyalty in “clarifying merchant taxes and supplying military salaries,” recognizing that clarity and simplicity were important desiderata in revenue collection.[^8] 
The customs collection system which Liu institutionalized functioned on an entirely different basis than that of neighboring Guangdong. In a discussion of Liu's role establishing maritime customs as a provincial revenue source, Gu Yanwu clearly separates the systems prevailing in Guangdong and Fujian, which are designated as taxes on “market ships” (市舶)  and “merchant ships” (商舶) respectively:

 “In the ‘market ship’ system, the foreigners’ ships anchor close to our land, and set up markets there to trade with the locals, as in Haojing Bay [Macau] in Guangdong. ‘Merchant ships,’ by contrast, means that local people spend money building boats and filling them with local goods, then cross the Eastern and Western Oceans to trade with the various barbarians of the ocean islands.”[^9]

The crucial distinction between the two systems was that in Guangdong, foreigners were allowed to set up "markets" in Chinese territory (referring to the Portuguese at Macau), which were then taxed, while in Fujian domestic traders were permitted to outfit their own trading voyages abroad, paying customs on their cargoes. 
Early in his tenure, in 1573, Liu Yaohui explicitly proposed using maritime customs as a means of funding military salaries: "In Wanli 2, the _Xunfu_ Liu Yaohui requested that boat taxes be used to supply soldiers' wages, to the amount of 6,000 taels per year, and to entrust the Coastal Defense Deputy with exclusive oversight thereof."[^10] As the main purpose of the previous sea bans had been to cut off trade with Japan, Liu further proposed that his customs inspections would easily catch illicit trade: ships which had visited Japan were easy to distinguish from others both because of the monsoon schedule (returning in the fall) and because they arrived with no cargoes: the sole good they brought back was silver. 

The assumption that only ships returning from Japan carried exclusively silver in their holds was, of course, quickly proven wrong by the rapid growth of trade with Manila, another source of vast streams of bullion. Thus, while keeping Japan off-limits, Liu ultimately implemented a three-tiered customs policy: the "water tax" was levied on ships by size, the "land tax" was an ad valorem duty on the estimated value of ships' cargo on departure, while an "extra tax" of 150 taels per ship was levied exclusively on ships trading to Luzon, to make up for their lack of taxable cargo.[^11] The fact that the "extra tax" alone on a single Manila-bound vessel produced more revenue for the state than the production of over one million bronze coins demonstrates the extraordinary windfall which the opening of trade to the Philippines represented for the administration of Fujian. 

The rapid rise of In Wanli 4, the total maritime tax at Haicheng amounted to 10,000 liang, good for nearly 10% of total annual military expenses. By Wanli 11 (1585) this had grown to over 20,000 taels, and receipts continued to grow as Haicheng's trade with Manila expanded. In fact, this single lifeline was more responsible for ensuring the sustainability of Fujian's coastal defenses in the coming decades than any of Liu's intentional policies, though of course the savvy customs regulations which he implemented were themselves a well-crafted and, in the context of the Ming experience, entirely original system for making use of this good luck. In this respect, the Hokkien traders bringing in silver from abroad saved the provincial administration. They were repaid for this service, under Liu, by the utilization of the revenues they brought for an ambitious set of policies which expanded provincial state power deep into the oceans these traders "plowed as their fields."


#### Deep Sea navigation and the "forward base" strategy

Alongside his efforts, Liu also exerted himself in reorganizing Fujian's military hierarchy to maintain readiness against the unique challenges of coastal defense. Chief among his priorities were maintaining expert officers and civilian administrators who understood local conditions, repeatedly requesting to keep trusted subordinates in place without following the normal schedule of rotation. The only exception to this general preference were the Zhejiang armies, which he explicitly sought to keep in "rotation" rather than letting them settle in the province in order to avoid abuses, while he handed most of their responsibilities over to loyal, seasoned, locally-aware veterans belonging to the professional regiments under the provincial hierarchy, and who were to be kept in their stations as long as possible. His unorthodox approach to the military bureaucracy was, in turn, designed to fit his largest overall project: the development of forces capable of pursuing "_wokou_" fleets into the deep seas, and the establishment of a permanent network of forward bases on major coastal islands on which these specially-trained deep strike units would be garrisoned – precisely the same islands, in fact, where the _wokou_ themselves had previously built their "nests."

In one early memorial, Liu requests that the Assistant Military Commissioner (僉事) of the Xinghua-Quanzhou military circuit be retained a year past the end of his assigned term in order to finish training marine patrols. This official (not named in the memorial) is noted for "long experience… gathering navies to control the southern seas, known throughout the circuit for their heroism, and drilling locals to reduce the hiring of eastern soldiers, thus allowing the farmers to prosper and be settled."[^12] (The "eastern soldiers" here are, of course, the Zhejiang _kebing_ which Liu wished to reduce Fujian's reliance on.) Further, as "Xing-Quan is a crucial territory along the Min coast," threatened by an outpouring of "bandit groups" from Chaozhou, this official's experience is indispensible, due to the risks of replacing him with a succesor less familiar with local conditions.[^13] Shortly afterwards, in November 1574, Liu Yaohui made further requests to keep the County Magistrate (_zhixian_) Ye Chunji in Huizhou, and the Vice Prefect (_tongpan_) Deng Bangji in Xinghua, both of whom had been selected for promotion elsewhere. Though this highly irregular request was curtly denied by the Ministry of Personnel, it was part of a pattern of Liu requesting to retain experienced officials in place rather than letting them be reassigned.[^14] He was not always unsuccesful; a request from the summer of 1576 to keep several prefectural and county officials in place, which used the exigencies of ongoing campaigns against pirates as justification, was approved.[^15]

Even at the end of his own 4-year tenure (already unusually long for surveillance commissioners, who were more often rotated after 3), Liu applied the logic of local expertise to himself, making an anguished plea to be allowed to stay in Fujian at least long enough to oversee a few final adjustments to military readiness. He pointed to the example of a wokou raid which occurred in Wanli 1, after his predecessor had left office but before he himself had arrived, as an example of the dangers to which administrative delays exposed the province. Besides his attempts to solidify a corps of experienced and specialized officers to oversee coastal defense, Liu also adjusted the geographic structure of the military bureaucracy to better concentrate forces where they were most needed. In Wanli 3, he asked the throne to cast and issue an official seal for the Zhangzhou Prefectural Deputy Commissioner for Coastal Defense (_haifang tongzhi_ 海防同知) and, at the same time, to authorize moving that officer’s seat from the prefectural city to the fortress of Yunxiao. Yunxiao's county seat was located in a narrow river valley in far southeastern Zhangzhou – a choke-point one day’s march from the major coastal garrisons of Tongshan, Xuanzhong, Liu'ao and Zhao'an. Because the _haifang tongzhi_ was tasked with personally verifying soldiers' monthly rations, inspecting ships under construction, and countersign all pay and supply documents, the three-to-four-day journey overland to the southern seaboard invited delay, surcharging and fraud; local bandits also exploited the sluggish responsiveness of the state at the jurisdictional seams between counties. By moving the _haifang tongzhi_ closer to the most troubleseom district year-round, he would be able to pay troops on the spot, keep accounts under tight seal, and act concurrently as military-police intendant for the border zone, thus tightening control of the Guangdong–Fujian pirate corridor without raising new taxes or troops. 

Recall here that the Zhangzhou Deputy Commissioner was also the same official responsible for overseeing the Haicheng customs revenue. Those customs receipts, rathered than being routed through the standard revenue offices of the provincial administration in Fuzhou, were directly delivered to the far southwest and disbursed to the troops by an official integrated into the front. This fact alone should demonstrate the fundamental military logic which explained Fujian's handling of the Haicheng trade.

This was not the only attempt Liu made to increase oversight over the problematic border between Zhangzhou and Chaozhou. In a later memorial, Liu proposed to occupy the island of Nan'ao, technically part of Guangdong but located right off the coast of the Chao-Zhang border. The primary goal of establishing a permanent coastal garrison at Nan'ao was to deny it to pirates, as it had been a center for smuggling and piracy from the 1560s on. Eventually, a joint Guangdong-Fujian military administration was established to shut off access to the island, with each province supplying two garrisons of 500 men each, but this complicated bureaucratic maneuver was not actually completed until after Liu had left Fujian. It is worth noting here the differential treatment given to Guangdong’s vs. Zhangzhou’s troublesome smuggling centers: whereas the old Zhangzhou pirate's cove of Yuegang was opened for legal trade as Haicheng county, the Nan'ao smugglers were eradicated and their home base placed under permanent surveillance to block smuggling out of the Chaoyang delta. We might read the difference in results as an institutionalization of provincial-level differences in sea trade policy, with Guangdong officials vigorously blocking their own residents from taking to the seas, but it was also driven by the real military emergency of the 1560s-70s in the waters around Nan'ao, of which more below. 

Liu's calls to move the military command structure closer towards the frontier, at Yunxiao and Nan'ao, show him departing from the logic of defensive "garrisoning" and groping towards the strategy of establishing forward bases to actively confront the _wokou_, which would eventually culminate in the Ming occupation of Penghu (after the Dutch arrival) and, decades later, the Kangxi Emperor's decision to permanently annex Taiwan. Before this time, the provincial navies of Fujian (like those of other provinces) were largely limited to defensive patrols in coastal waters, and staffed by hereditary _weisuo_ reserve troops who were theoretically supposed to make their livings from farming, and generally lacked experience sailing the open oceans. As seen in Chapter 3, the extraordinary successes of Yu Dayou as admiral stemmed in part from his successful mobilization of private populations which did have experience with sailing: the fishing and saltern households. As Lin Xiyuan had noted in 1550s, the _wokou_ and fishermen came from the same regions and were equally matched in sailing skill, while the _weisuo_ navies utterly lacked it. The facility of local pirates in moving around the waters of their home region, gained by decades of experience in navigation for trade and fishing, eventually forced the Ming military to gain similar capacities. This is precisely what Liu's reforms were intended to accomplish, hence the frequent references to "coastal patrols" and naval training in his memorials; it is also what made the island garrisons crucial (including Tongshan and Xuanzhong on Dongshan Island, and Zhongzuosuo on modern Xiamen). 

By the end of Liu's tenure, he had successfulyl established several forward bases staffed by permanent professional troops, mostly belonging to the categories "mobile strike forces" (游擊) or "shock troopers" (奇兵). These professional forces, while superior to their weisuo predecessors in skill and to their _kebing_ rivals in oversight and affordability, nevertheless had to be paid in silver and fed with _weisuo_ granary supplies. At an average annual silver salary of 7.5 taels per recruit, single garrisons could cost the state hundreds or thousands of taels per year, and the whole system of elite naval guards took up a substantial portion of the provincial military budget, probably more than making up for the reduction in _kebing_ numbers.[^16] Their location on rocky coastal islands short on fertile land only worsened the difficulties of supply, making them more reliant on private grain shipments paid for by the private commercial activities of the civilian populations connected to the _weisuo_ garrisons nearby; the coexistence of silver-rich soldier populations and commercially-minded, _weisuo_-registered civilian populations would be of great relevance to the eventual development of some of these, like Tongshan and especially Xiamen, into commercial centers and urban cores.[^17] 

#### Conclusion

By early 1574 Liu reported that, after years of borrowing and juggling every available Fujian tax item to finance coastal warfare, the province finally held its own store of salt commutation silver and standing quotas. He therefore requested that, instead of forcing these modest tolls on ports, mines, temples and market boats to keep flowing to Beijing, they be permanently reserved in the provincial treasury as a special “seaward contingent fund,” to be drawn on for soldiers’ pay and hull repairs whenever fleets sail, but otherwise left in local circulation so that the domestic economy be given space to recover before the next round of warfare. At the midway point of his tenure, Liu had largely succeeded in regulating expenses and keeping Fujian self-sufficient, but this fiscal security remained tenuous: it required a continued reprieve from having to actually send funds to the capital, or even to repay "loans" from the neighboring provinces.[^18] Behind these requests lay the awareness that, while the worst of the _wokou_ crisis had passed, there were still undefeated threats lurking on the horizon. 

Regarding the purely military side of Liu's mission, his reorganization of the local military structure to emphasize the professional regulars under the _zongbing_ Hu Shouren, and to train a hardened core of expert sailors stationed along the coast for quick action in blue-water battles, fundamentally altered the nature of Fujian's conflicts with the _wokou_ networks. The embrace of long-standing illicit maritime trade networks, by both tapping them for revenues with customs and tapping them for expertise with sailors recruited from among the merchants and fishermen, represented early steps in the Ming state's re-entry into the naval sphere after a long period of dormancy. Since the early 16th century, the steady growth of foreign maritime trade pulled Ming society outwards, and after 1570 it brought the state's administrative structures with it. A paradigmatic, if extreme, example of how private maritime activities forced the state to engage with more and more distant spaces came with the young Spanish colony on Luzon. What in the earliest years of Liu's tenure was still a poorly-understood source of windfall revenue would come to be a close diplomatic partner and battleground for domestic conflicts, as warfare across the Chao-Zhang provincial boundary spilled across the oceans and brought about the first diplomatic contacts between the Ming Empire and Spain. 

  

## III. Leaving the Harbor: The Campaigns Against Lin Feng

The historical geographer Li Zhijun has proposed that Fujian went through a continuous process of "exteriorization" (_waixianghua_ 外向化) over the course of late imperial history, as the center of economic gravity shifted from the inland agrarian economy outwards, towards coastal production and maritime trade, and eventually outside the boundaries of the Chinese state.[^19] The dynamics of "exteriorization" also favored the concentration of capital in large organizations for a variety of purposes.[^20] As Yang Peina has argued, the very nature of coastal life demanded a highly mobile, monetized society, particularly as the numerous registered fishing households, with their produce being unsuited for shipment inland, were among the first groups to be taxed in silver instead (the "fish tax" 渔课); soon the right to extract profit from coastal fishing was passed over to local elite clans as intermediaries, with fishing households themselves forced to either be adopted into, hired by, or flee from these elites. Deep-sea fishing itself required large ships, large crews, and long-term investments which further entrenched fishermen further into relationships with wealthy patrons.  Inexorable logic of “externalization” due to the embeddedness of the wokou networks in local society, or in other words, the existing reliance of local society on overseas activities. The state was forced into the deep sea in its drive to follow mercantile networks. Articulated by both Liu ("今日海上之賊非外番島夷也乃該省土著之民也所乘首舟楫所食者穀粟所利用者大呉三者皆不産於海而賊人不能缺一以爲生舟行於海内兩年必敝欣而改作兩月而後成") and Lin Xiyuan (“萬一突圍一去，則彼歸路已絕，滄波非久駐之所，萬一望洋遠去，則彼生計就窮，倉卒無入番之備，度彼釜中之魚“ ). If the developing deep-sea economy made it necessary for Fujian to project force deep into the ocean, it also increased the risk posed to the entire system by predation from coastal pirates, what He Qiaoyuan called “our inland Japanese.”[^21] 

The intense struggle for resources in the densely populated but economically marginal coastal districts was intensified by the massive piratical raids of the 1550s-60s. One response to the traumatic late Jiajing _wokou_ crisis was a focus on training local militias (_baojia_) and constructing walls and moats around cities and villages alike. After the remnant pirates of the Wang Zhi network had been swept away, these rural military apparati were quickly turned against one another. The famous _xiedou_ inter-clan warfare, which was endemic across Fujian in the Qing dynasty, seems to have its origins in the universal militarization of these years. In the late Ming, before rural competition had taken such an elaborate form, it tended to overlap instead with banditry and piracy, which were themselves hard to distinguish (the same words, _kou_ and _zei_, were used for organized violence on both land and sea). Chen Chunsheng on rural militarization; the newly militarized locales can easily turn towards raiding one another, especially when resources are scarce; per Xu Xiaodong this is where Lin Daoqian got his start, leading his colocals on raids against Zhao'an granaries during a famine. 

As this anecdote shows, no region was more intensely militarized than the border between Guangdong and Zhangzhou, above all the coastal border counties of Raoping and Zhao'an themselves. Another anecdote made famous by Fu Yiling emphasizes the deep embeddedness of "wokou" pirates in the local societies of these regions: in the 1550s, the Zhejiang magistrate Ruan Ji led his troops in pursuit of pirates who had attacked his jurisdiction, and tracked them all the way to the town of Meiling in Zhao’an. His men wished to massacre the denizens of this town, but Ruan stopped them, saying: “they are pirates in Zhejiang, but in Zhao’an they are our loyal subjects.”[^22] While they were rewarded for heads taken in pitched battle, there was little appetite in the Ming military for massacring civilians in their homes; but that is precisely how the Chao-Zhang pirates treated neighbors from across the provincial border, and how Quanzhou elites wished to see their “piratical” rivals treated in turn.

The edict appointing Liu to Fujian laid special emphasis on the prefectures of Chaozhou and Zhangzhou, on either side of the border between Guangdong and Fujian provinces. In doing so Zhang Juzheng highlighted the most militarily troublesome region along the southeast coast, and also recognized the serious administrative barriers in the way of pacifying it. The boundary between provinces was often porous for private individuals, who routinely circulated across it as traders, smugglers, itinerant laborers, and raiders, but highly problematic for state bureaucracy, as Fujian and Guangdong belonged to entirely separate military circuits with their own hierarchies and loyalties. The tendency for provincial militaries to butt heads was clearly anticipated by Zhang, but no amount of cajoling from the center could actually overcome the competition and mutual distrust between provincial generals and governors in practice. As it happened, the major "wokou" campaigns of Liu's tenure floundered on the shoals of constant disagreements with the Liangguang army and navy. By the time of Liu's tenure, the seam between Guangdong and Fujian provinces' spheres of authority was a recognized weak point in the empire's administrative geography, which pirate groups local to the area exploited with increasing sophistication. These pirates' freedom of movement along this nettlesome border was further increased by their deepening familiarity with the oceans beyond the Ming state's reach. 

Speaking broadly, the anti-wokou strategists of the mid-late Ming all focused on the utilization of geography (passes and chokepoints) and “encircling” tactics to break the bandits on land and force them into the seas, where it was assumed they would die without support from their homelands. While Yu Dayou had occasionally suggested naval setpiece battles as a tactic, most strategists assumed _wokou_ superiority on the waves and sought to force them into decisive land engagements. Major debates had to do with whether wokou should be “recruited” or “exterminated:” this has to do with whether maritime activities of given communities will be legitimated or suppressed, and unsurprisingly officials tend to support “recruiting” pirates from their own provinces and “exterminating” outsiders. Over time, this turned into a tendency towards co-optation of the wokou struggles for inter-clan, inter-village, and inter-regional contestation over trade. The loudest voice in favor of destroying whole communities connected to _wokou_ was Lin Xiyuan, who advocated tracking wokou to their home villages in Chaozhou and arresting their relatives; in so doing he may have been voicing his own anger as a victim of raiders from Chaozhou, but also as a competitor: as seen in Chapter One, Lin was himself among the most prominent maritime trading gentry of Quanzhou, specifically accused of smuggling by Zhu Wan before his suicide.

The doyen of "local expertise" on Fujian coastal society, Lin Xiyuan, had articulated a basic principle of _wokou_ policy: “海寇事宜，以元策之，乘風揚帆，往來上下，大兵一集，則望洋遠去追捕稍緩，則乘虚復來，此海寇之所長，難以樸滅者也。然亦謀事者未得其要耳。“ However, as the pirates have homes on land and cannot feed themselves forever at sea, they can be starved by cutting them from their coastal supporters, then surrounded and annihilated when forced to land: then, "even should they break free of their encirclement, their way home will be cut, and the blue waves are no place to live permanently; should they ride into the further oceans, they will be unprepared to enter foreign lands, and can be caught like fish in a pot."[^23] When Liu Yaohui first arrived in Fujian, he shared this basic set of assumptions about how to combat piracy: "The oceangoing bandits of today are not barbarians of foreign islands (外番島夷), but rather native civilians of this province (該省土著之民). Their navigators and oarsmen, the grain they eat, the goods the profit from: none of these come from the seas, and the bandits cannot live without a single one."[^24] A similar logic also animated Zhang Juzheng's language in Liu's appointment edict of “preventing collusion between mountains and seas.” However, this shared understanding would prove naive in one important way: it failed to account for the wokou’s growing ability to find bases over the seas, building on the experience of Fujianese mariners in ranging further and further afield. Far from being “lost in the waves,” _wokou_ groups would begin building new settlements on the islands around the rim of the Nanyang. Eventually, Liu would be forced to abandon the notion that “cutting off” pirates at home was sufficient, and look for ways to accomodate the military to coastal society while gaining the power to pursue enemies even into the islands of the "outer seas." The catalyst for this change in strategy would come from the long campaigns against the decade's most notorious pirate, the Chaozhou-born corsair Lin Feng. Ultimately, Liu would rise to this challenge by developing his "forward base" strategy and successfully coordinating strikes deep into the outer seas, but Fujianese success at pursuing pirates abroad was undermined by continual conflict with his cross-provincial partners. In the end, Liu would find it easier to cooperate with foreign rulers than the neighboring Guangdong military. 


## Origins of Lin Feng
Lin Feng was born in Raoping, the easternmost coastal county in Guangdong, right on the border with Zhao'an and Nan'ao island. He first emerged as a part of the "charter empire" of the Nan'ao pirate netorks, the conglomeration led by Wu Ping before his death at the hands of Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou (recorded in Chapter 3).[^25] Thereafter Wu's forces had split under the leadership of the pirates Lin Guoxian, Lin Daoqian, and Zeng Yiben. Zeng Yiben had been a major threat to the Chao-Zhang region during the Longqing years, who pioneered a strategy of poising his troops directly on the boundary between Fujian and Guangdong in order to take advantage of delays in coordination between the armies of the two provinces. Lin Feng fought as a subordinate of Zeng Yiben until the latter was killed in Longqing 3, whereupon Lin seems to have inherited some of his forces. Lin was further said to be a "great-nephew" of Lin Guoxian and a "son-in-law" of Wu Ping, underscoring the close personal relations among prominent figures in this milieu.[^26] While reliable biographical information on these smuggler-pirates is hard to find, it seems clear from the existing evidence that the activites of the Nan'ao smuggling network which was prominent for over a decade in the 1560s and 70s were dominated by a relatively small number of close-knit figures, if not all literal blood relatives, then at least probably long-standing associates from a small number of home places and clans. All of these figures hailed from the narrow strip of coastal land between the Chaoyang River delta and the county seat of Zhao'an county in Fujian. 
Lin Feng first emerged in historical sources around Longqing 6, when he raided the town of Shenquan (神泉鎮) in Chaozhou prefecture. A report by General Yu Dayou at this time assessed Lin’s fleet at 43 ships, and noted his crew included Japanese (_wo_ 倭) and men from Leizhou, Lianzhou, and Hainan, all regions in southwest Guangdong, where Lin had supposedly been "stealing pearls."[^27] We might infer from this report that Lin spent the years between Longqing 3 and 5 in the waters near Hainan, gathering men around the small core he inherited from Zeng Yiben, and making profits from the region's trade in pearls (whether as harvester, smuggler, or pirate).[^28] The fact that Yu could provide such detailed commercial intelligence about Lin Feng's operations might be a result of mere military intelligence gathering, but it also suggests Yu's own networks overlapped with maritime commercial circles – another reminder that the most influential local lobbyists of Minnan naval society were socially quite close to the "wokou" they fought against, and were by no means disinterested parties in the suppression of their commercial rivals.
In April–May 1573, anticipating a reprisal by the Guangdong navy, Lin fled north into the Taiwan Strait with, occupying the island of Penghu (澎湖). He was pursued there by Fujian naval forces under Yu Dayou (then still the province's acting Regional Commander), and so fled further to the island of Taiwan, where Yu was unable to pursue him. Lin then reorganized his forces and settled at a place called Wanggang (魍港) at the mouth of the Xingang River (新港溪) in modern Tainan. (This was the situation Liu Yaohui referred to in his first report from Fujian, when he mentioned Lin Feng being "anchored at Penghu.")
For his failure to capture Lin, Yu Dayou was forced into retirement and replaced as Regional Commander of the Fujian regular armies by his former subordinate Hu Shouren. This humiliation did not prevent Yu from offering his services to the newly appointed _xunfu_ Liu Yaohui (who, perhaps armed with Zhang's advice to sideline Yu, is not recorded as providing any response to these letters). Yu’s unsolicited advice to Liu included a detailed assessment of ship types and crew quality available to rebuild the provincial navy: of the various provincial ship varieties, Yu recommended relying on Fujianese "white junk" ships (_baicao chuan_ 白艚船), which had a good mix of size and mobility and were manned by sailors recruited from saltern and merchant communities; these sailors could be motivated fighters so long as they were well-compensated.[^29] In his initial report on Lin Feng, meanwhile, Yu confirmed that most of Lin's fleet consisted of these same "white junks," with a small contingent of Dongguan-style "crow's tail ships" (_wuwei chuan_ 乌尾船). Does the identification of Lin's ships as "Fujian" rather than "Guangdong" style might imply that some of his manpower was also Fujianese, perhaps of the same Zhang-Quan saltern or merchant origin as the professional sailors of the Fujian navy? While it is impossible to confirm the actual composition of Lin's followers, the evidence would at least suggest that the Chaozhou pirate networks operated in a culturally “Minnan” space, more than a “Pearl River Delta” one, whether or not they actually recruited from the neighboring province. We might thus see Lin Feng and the Nan’ao network which spawned him as a mirror image of the Zhang-Quan trading world, denied the same opportunities for productive rapproachment with the state, and thus driven instead to open conflict with it. 
In certain respects Lin's successes can be explained precisely by the Chaozhou network making better use of the trails blazed by the expansion of private Minnan and Chaozhou traders, whose effective horizons had clearly expanded by this point well beyond their home regions. At this time, Taiwan as a whole was virtually uncharted in official Ming sources, but it was clearly not unknown to the Chaozhou and Minnan mariners. In fact, Lin Feng was not the only _wokou_ inhabiting the island: shortly after arriving there he encountered his former compatriot Lin Daoqian, who himself had an active raiding and smuggling career in the late Longqing years. The two clashed, and Lin Feng defeated his rival and seized most of his fleet, though Daoqian himself survived with a few ships and fled to Cambodia. Following the merger of the two Lins' fleets, Lin Feng controlled 95 ships, the peak of his naval power, and returned to raid Chaozhou in the winter fall of 1573-4, after spending the full summer and fall at Wanggang. 

Yu Dayou's failure to pursue Lin to Taiwan is partially explained by the actual inability of his forces to operate so far from the mainland, in part by Yu's discomfort navigating the uncharted coasts of Taiwan, and in part by Yu sharing the assumption that Lin relied on natives of his home in Raoping for supplies; he thus predicted that Lin's ambitions and constraints would lead him to return to Guangdong, and proposed that preparations should be made to catch him there, rather than pursuing him in the waters off Fujian. Yu was partially correct in this assessment: Lin did in fact return to Nan'ao, and was clearly in contact with associates there and in mainland Chaozhou. However, as events would shortly demonstrate, Lin was not entirely dependent on these contacts, and proved himself comfortable operating very far from his home. 
In the 10th month of Wanli 1 (~November 1573), Lin burst back onto the scene with a substantial force, but his ambitious attempt to sack the county seat of Chenghai was stymied by the locals' dogged defense of their city walls.[^30] Thereafter Lin seems to have withdrawn for the winter back to his old haunt of Nan'ao, from which base he raided shipping along the coast while delaying reprisals by negotiating with the provincial authorities about potential terms of surrender.[^31] He spent several months there before Liu Yaohui prepared a report to the throne requesting permission for a joint attack on Lin's position from both Guangdong and Fujian. Liu insisted that Lin had to be killed, not "pacified," because his negotations were part of a known delaying tactic: by pretending to beg for amnesty, then withdrawing to the deep sea whenever faced with real military force, Lin forced Fujian into an exhausting state of permanent mobilization to guard against his raids. This was the core reason Liu requested to participate in a matter which technically should have been under Guangdong's jurisdiction: prolonged mobilization put Fujian at risk of fiscal exhaustion and mutiny (師老而財匱…兵久而變生).[^32] 
In March 1574 the Ministry of War made the necessary preparations for a joint Fujian-Guangdong campaign against Lin's forces at Nan'ao, and approved Liu's request to pursue a "pincer-and-elimination" strategy, in which the Guangdong armies under Yin Zhengmao would approach by land and Fujian's forces under Regional Commander Hu Shouren would surround the island by sea.[^33] This was the specific strategic maneuver which Liu moved to Zhangzhou to oversee in early 1574.
Unfortunately for Liu, the joint attack with Guangdong moved too slowly to trap Lin Feng, who fled to the "outer seas" (外洋) on the 2nd day of the 4th month (~May 1574).[^34] This was the point at which Liu proposed to occupy Nan'ao as a permanent coastal garrison, in order to deny it to Lin Feng should he return. At this time, Liu continued to expound Lin Xiyuan's argument: pursuing the bandits at sea would be too draining on state resources, but precisely since they were local to Chaozhou, they would to return there for food, ships, and merchandise, at which point a trap could be sprung.[^35] Liu further proposed to fund the new garrisons through ad valorem customs on coastal trade (without going so far as to propose opening the island to _foreign_ trade).[^36] To a certain extent this represents an extension of the principles Liu put in place for Fujian, but it should be noted that the choice to tax local trade while continuing to block Nan'ao's locals from engaging in business overseas, while perfectly in keeping with Guangdong provincial policy, was also obviously advantageous to the maritime traders of Minnan, who thereby gained stricter prohibitions on the activities of their key rivals in the Nanyang, even as coasting trade among Fujian's harbors remained duty-free. 
Regardless of Liu's assurances that Lin Feng would invariably return to Guangdong, the court ordered that Fujian pursue and capture Lin, and Hu Shouren duly spent the summer chasing Lin to Penghu and, again, to Wanggang in Taiwan. On this occasion, compared to Yu's pursuit of Lin the previous year, Hu showed himself considerably more flexible in adapting to the foreign circumstances: not only did he recruit fishermen to lead his troops to Taiwan, but once there he negotiated with "eastern foreigners" to prepare a joint attack on Lin.[^37] This incident may well be the first recorded case of a Ming official interacting with the indigenous people of Taiwan, though as the intercession of "fishermen" shows, Hu's Taiwanese partners were probably already familiar with Ming subjects even besides _wokou_. The "pincer" raid on Lin succeeded in dislodging him from Wanggang that November, whereupon he disappeared into the deep seas.[^38] If Liu and Hu expected that Lin would thereby be forced back to Guangdong, however, they were mistaken. 
When Lin Feng repeatedly fled to Taiwan for stays of several months at a time, during which periods he clearly succeeded in supplying several thousand people, he undermined the core premise shared by Lin Xiyuan and Liu Yaohui: the Chaozhou "fish" actually were capable of escaping the "pot" and surviving on the resources of the outer oceans. The onus thus fell on the state to not only sever them from their partners on the Chinese mainland, but to pursue them abroad. In certain cases, like Penghu (and, eventually, Taiwan), this would oblige the state to actually annex and settle territories outside the previous remit of the Ming state; in others, it would require negotiating with established Southeast Asian powers, which by this time included European colonial administrations.

## Manila and Pangasinan: Limahong Among The Fan
Following his November 3rd retreat from Chaozhou, Lin Feng intercepted a Fujianese merchant vessel returning to Haicheng from Manila, founded as a Spanish colony just three years previous. From the traders on this ship he learned of the young city, said to be inhabited by a small group of soldiers with extraordinary wealth in silver. There is no sign that Lin knew of Manila before this time, but he apparently decided to turn his fleet south and make a play to become king over this unusual new country. His fleet arrived in Manila Bay at dawn on November 30, 1574, and immediately launched an assault, which killed the Spanish sentries and sacked the outer town, but failed to penetrate the palisades of the inner city. Two days later, a second assault was repelled by the Spanish, making use of their fortifications and cannons, and Lin decided to withdraw to elsewhere in Luzon, ultimately settling in Pangasinan, slightly upstream from the mouth of the Agno River, where he began constructing his own fortified settlement. 

Past historians have treated the episode of Lin Feng’s attack on Manila in great detail. It became a foundational experience in Spanish memory and historiography regarding relations between their Philippine colony and the Chinese; in Chen Jinghe’s words, this raid “taught the Spanish to fear the Chinese,” and thus presaged future episodes of Spanish preventative violence.[^39] In the words of the Jesuit historian Francisco Colin, writing from Manila in the 1680s, the Devil "brought Limahong from China, on the coasts of which he was a famous corsair, with seventy ships which pushed Manila to the limits of endurance."[^40] (2-3 sentences lit review here: Loarca, Gaspar de San Agustin, Morga, Chirino, Colin, Chen Jinghe, Dai Yixuan, Ng Chin-keong, Macauley, Tremml-Werner, Shutz.) There is no need for our purposes to reconstruct the full Spanish experience with "Limahong." What is crucial is the fact that the Spanish did not drive Lin away from Luzon on their own: they did so with assistance from Liu Yaohui's newly trained navy, and the joint campaign to capture Lin thus became the occasion for the earliest diplomatic contacts between Spain and China, in which Liu acted as the Spaniards' main counterparty. 

The earliest contact between Fujianese and Spanish troops was established in April, 1575, when the squadron commander (_bazong_ 把总) Wang Wanggao arrived at the Lingayen Gulf and contacted the Spanish commander Juan de Salcedo, who was then besieging Lin Feng's settlement with some 3,000 men (mostly indigenous Luzonese auxiliaries). Wang had been dispatched to Luzon at the head of the Shock Trooper (奇兵) detachments of the Tongshan and Wuyu camps. (In theory, Wang as Squadron Commander should have brought 500 men from his own Tongshan battalion, while the Wuyu troops were reserves and perhaps added another 200-500 men to that tally.) By this time, the Spanish troops had already burned the majority of Lin's ships, but were unable to penetrate his defenses or prevent his remaining ships from leaving at will.  Wang thereupon agreed with Salcedo to engage in yet another pincer attack, where the Fujian navy would block the river mouth, while the Spanish armies besieged Lin from land. This siege lasted from April of 1575 through August; during this time Lin’s personal secretary is supposed to have defected to the Spanish, revealing that the pirates held sufficient supplies for several months but faced deteriorating conditions. 

During the months of siege, a pair of Spanish priests, Martin de Rada and Geronimo Martin, took the opportunity afforded by their fortuitous contact with the Ming navy to request an embassy to China. Wang Wanggao agreed, and in July took the padres alongside a small contingent of Spanish military ambassadors to confer with Liu Yaohui at Fuzhou. This was the first instance of any Europeans other than Portuguese being granted an official audience with a Ming official. Spanish sources on the embassy record two main requests: first, that Catholic missionaries be allowed to freely preach Christianity; and second, that Spain be granted a small island of the Chinese coast for the purposes of trade, just as the Portuguese had with Macao. However, much to their disappointment, de Rada and Martin were shipped back to Manila without being allowed to proceed to Beijing, with Liu's promise that their gifts and letters would be forwarded to the Emperor. (The entries on this embassy in the _Veritable Records_ mention "tribute," but there is no sign that Liu reported either of the actual Spanish requests to Beijing).[^41] 

This early diplomatic contact between Manila and Fuzhou would be virtually the last, as events in the Philippines quickly soured Liu on the performance of his foreign auxiliaries. In August, entirely against the expectations of Wang and his Spanish allies, Lin succeeded in breaking through the blockade and escaping Pangasinan with 33 newly built (or perhaps merely repaired) ships. He escaped with close to 2,000 of his men in the direction of Taiwan, successfully evading Wang's pursuing navy.[^42] There, Lin seems to have spent a short time recuperating at the old base of Xingang, once again making use of Taiwan as a resupply hub and safe harbor.[^43] 

The Pangasinan siege and Lin's daring escape reveal the capacities of the parties involved. On the one hand, Lin's ability to survive for nearly a year in an environment completely unknown to him, and even in siege conditions, demonstrates the high level of organization and preparedness of his organization. Their ability to rebuild a fully functioning navy in just a few months from their camp, in particular, makes it clear that they could likely have operated abroad indefinitely, without strictly needing to return to China for supplies. On the other hand, Wang's forces also succeeded in maintaining their supplies and readiness for 4 months far away from the logistical networks of the _weisuo_ granaries and civilian paymasters. Though there is no direct evidence of how this strike force handled its logistics, it seems unlikely that they relied entirely on materiel dispatched by the Fujianese bureacracy; Wang's forces must then either have been supplied by their Spanish allies, or purchased supplies from private – Spanish or Hokkien – traders. 

In September, Lin's remaining fleet straggled back to the coast of Haimen, where Liu once again dispatched Hu Shouren to coordinate with Guangdong forces for another attempt at a joint elimination campaign. In November Liu Yaohui reported that Lin’s forces had been scattered and reduced to remnants in a series of zig-zagging pursuits between Chaozhou and Taiwan (or rather the "seas of Danshui" – an ambiguous placename but perhaps referring to northern Taiwan). That month, two subordinate commanders under Lin Feng, Ma Zhishan and Zhou Xiao, approached the Guangdong authorities (represented by Jin Zhe) to surrender. Despite Liu's vigorous protests against pausing hostilities, the Guangdong Military Vice Commissioner Jin Zhe ultimately accepted the surrender of 1,712 of Lin's men – but Lin himself was not among them, and in fact was never found again. As late as 1578 Zhang Juzheng would lament that Lin had escaped justice to an entirely "uncertain fate." [^44]

#### Aftermath: Struggle With Guangdong, Surrender of the 1,712

For a comprehensive assessment of the Lin Feng campaign and its role in Liu's tenure as a whole, we can turn to the final memorial which Liu submitted on the campaign, titled "Memorial on the Not-Yet-Exterminated Raoping Bandit, Requesting Dedicated Suppression, And Further Requesting Verification of Merit Records to Illuminate Rewards and Punishments."[^45] From this point onwards, with Lin's strength effectively broken, the remaining sources shift focus to the bitter struggle between Guangdong and Fujian factions over the strategy employed against Lin. Signs of inter-provincial rivalry begin with the memorial's very title: Lin Feng is identified as a "Rao thief," explicitly connecting him not just with "our people" or "locals of the coast" as before, but with the specific, vilifed Guangdong county of Raoping. Throughout the rest of this very long memorial, Liu bemoans conflicts with his partners in Guangdong which have given Lin an opportunity to slip the net around him. Guangdong side orders a stop to hostilities to negotiate with surrendering bandits. Liu urges the court to order Guangdong to resume fighting, on the basis that Lin has repeatedly escaped from exactly such situations before; Liu cites Lin's speedy reconstruction of a full fleet while besieged on Luzon as proof that he can quickly recover from temporary setbacks.

It was indeed only once the main bureaucratic struggles for credit in the Lin Feng case were over that Liu forwarded news of the Spanish embassy to the throne. His first report on the matter was recorded in the Veritable Records on September 28, 1576, when reported the completion of the Luzon campaign and, additionally, forwarded the letter and gifts presented by the Luzon tributaries.[^46] Shortly thereafter the court announced promotions to Liu, Hu, and various subordinates for defeating Lin, with the Luzon barbarians to be rewarded as well.[^47]  Shortly thereafter, however, Guangdong's officials were also heard out and praised. 

A final assessment of the campaign was provided by the Ministry of War in January 1576, in the last days of Wanli 4. This report emphasizes the balance of both provinces' military contributions: the Fujian army drove Lin's forces to desperation, but it was Jin Zhe's final offer of amnesty which induced Lin's fellowers to surrender themselves en masse (prostrating themselves with their hands tied), thus allowing 1,712 men to be captured and rehabilitated. Despite Liu’s denouncements of Guangdong obstructionism, Tan Lun, as Minister of War, considered both the "extermination" (_jiao_) and "recruitment" (_fu_) methods to be valid (“one exterminates, the other recruits, and their merit is the same 一勦一撫，其功則一").[^48] 

The debate between _jiao_ vs. _fu_ had long-standing precedents in _wokou_ policy circles, but these debates were in very few cases undertaken from a neutral standpoint. It is likely no coincidence that, in the case of Lin Feng, it was the authorities of his native province which were so anxious to rehabilitate his followers. When Guangdong “recruited” 1,712 of Lin's followers, these men were almost certainly absorbed into the provincial navy; thus Guangdong gained a corps of experienced sailors familiar with overseas routes, most of whom presumably were natives of Chaozhou prefecture (and thus of Guangdong). The ferocity of the conflict between Fujian and Guangdong the Lin Feng operation is thus explicable as more than a mere wrangle for bureaucratic rewards.[^49] Something more fundamental was at issue: possibly competition over which province would control the maritime commercial networks these 'pirates' represented. Liu clearly held the principle of his mission to be wiping out illegal smugglers altogether, something which would benefit the legally sanctioned traders, while the Guangdong authorities wished to protect their own subjects and perhaps make future use of their skills. This fundamental conflict of interests recurred in bitter bureaucratic disputes between the two provinces throughout the late Ming.

While Liu failed to fully enforce his will on the troublesome Guang-Fu border region, his patron Zhang Juzheng ultimately approved of his performance, and in particular of his successful diplomatic use of the "eastern foreigners": “若非遣谍行间，彼番人安肯歼乎？” The rewards showered on him for this success were to mark be the pinnacle of Liu’s career in Fujian. In early 1577 he was accused on corruption charges and recalled from office.[^50] Although he was quickly cleared of the charges and took up high posts in Jiangxi and Liangguang, he never returned to Fujian.  The final sign that Liu was favored in the Guang-Min conflict is that the was rewarded by taking up the very post of Liangguang Zongdu which had obstructed him before, in order to finish the work he had started.

### Conclusion

By the time Liu Yaohui arrived in Fujian in 1572, the coast was nominally pacified: Qi Jiguang's campaigns had broken the great wokou confederations, and the opening of Haicheng had channeled some maritime trade into legal forms. But the fiscal and military structures that had achieved this pacification were themselves becoming problematic. The kebing consumed silver that came from surcharges the populace resented. The weisuo remained hollow shells. The pirates had not been fully exterminated, but rather displaced: to Nan'ao, to Taiwan, to the burgeoning routes of the East and West Seas. The shining lights of this system had been transferred north to face the Mongols, leaving their troops in the hands of successors who had not recruited them and could not command their complete loyalty. Liu's task was not to build a new system but to make an inherited one work.

Liu undermined Tan Lun’s silverization policies, and shifted critical tasks from the guest armies they introduced to a new regular army made of Fujian locals, and drawing on regional specialties (like deep-sea navigation). At the same time, he embraced and normalized the most radical of the Tan group’s reforms, namely the acceptance of private coastal shipping and centralization of maritime customs at Haicheng, and he took seriously the attendant extension of the state’s scope of responsibility deep into the outer oceans. Why did he embrace one set of reforms and not the other? 

To understand this we must discard our expectation for a neat teleological narrative of steady “silverization,” or of a progressive opening to the market and the seas, in favor of deeply understanding the real pressures and possibilities facing the key official actors along the southeast coast. Ultimately, policies at the provincial level were subordinate to the fiscal demands of the center, and hence, in the early Wanli years, to Zhang Juzheng. Those policies were retained which served the needs of making Fujian self-sustaining and ultimately capable of resuming the supply of funds to the central treasury; those which might impede that goal, such as excessive use of mercenaries or those reforms which undermined the core subsistence basis of the weisuo garrisons (and thus threatened to lead to silver bailouts), were discarded. While it is true that the architects of late Ming policy, Zhang above all, appreciated silver for its fluidity and simplicity, they were not drawn to white gold’s inherent charisma; it was a tool which suited certain purposes, but not all. 

While Liu was unable to decisively resolve some of the thorniest problems he encountered, particularly the dereliction of the weisuo and the difficulties of coordinating military campaigns across provincial and international boundaries, certain of the solutions he hit on were to become important parts of the local political economy. Most importantly, Liu’s cultivation of maritime customs at Haicheng as a chief source of provincial military revenues came to define institutional arrangements in Minnan for decades to come, and largely solved the problem of sourcing silver for the coastal garrisons. Those coastal garrisons themselves were in turn brought to a much higher standard of readiness under Liu, with elite, professional naval corps capable of operating almost as far abroad as the Hokkien mercantile web itself. As regards the growing Fujianese trade with Manila, there is surprisingly little direct evidence from Liu’s time that authorities recognized they were receiving vast new shipments of silver from just overseas, but purposefully or not, the system Liu put in place was well-positioned to take advantage. 

Liu advocated "extermination" of the illegible traders at the interstitial points between provincial regulatory regimes, and an embrace of fully visible and regulated trade at a central, controllable node (Haicheng). Those who wish to participate in the commerce of the coasts must do so within clear, visible limits, and anyone who wishes to break free of those limits must flee the Ming space entirely; even then, the agents of the state can track them.[^51] Liu's proposal of putting Nan'ao under joint Guangdong-Fujian military jurisdiction was a way to deny smugglers an important interstice, while allowing authorities of both provinces to exercise some oversight over one another. 

Liu’s partial de-silverization, silver-based foreign customs regime, and silver-hungry "forward base" strategy were not contradictory, but rather complementary attempts to map and tax maritime space. Faced with the severe fiscal-military dilemma posed by coastal security, Liu succeeded in finding a way to make the maritime economy pay for its own active defense through careful drawing of boundaries and routing of activity through His successors in the office, Pang Shangpeng and Geng Dingxiang, inherited his successes and would attempt to apply this insight to the land economy in their efforts to implement the Single Whip policy. These efforts would prove far less successful. 

Efforts to draw revenue out of Fujian's land-based economy were stymied in large part because of dynamics created by the very exteriorization and reliance on deep-sea trade which created the conditions for Liu's success. The solutions which Liu adopted tended to confirm the existing particularities of Minnan society and make it more, rather than less, resistant to conforming with the general fiscal policy of the empire, including under the Single Whip reforms. The specific combination of silverized of public finance integrated with legalized maritime trade and a continuing dependence on local professional troops would set Fujian further along its distinctive path of institutional evolution, eventually causing the defining events of late Ming Fujian history to revolve around control of shipping in Xiamen Bay. The true benificiaries of Liu's reforms, more than his partners among the Zhang Juzheng reform collective, would be the heirs of admirals and "recruited" _wokou_.
