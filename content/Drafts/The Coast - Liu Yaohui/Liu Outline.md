# Section III: The Limits of Stabilization

## Analytical Frame

Liu Yaohui's tenure as xunfu was defined by a fundamental tension: his mandate to bring "lasting peace" to Fujian's maritime frontier required not only reforming the province's own military apparatus, but coordinating action across jurisdictional boundaries that the Ming administrative system made nearly impossible to cross. The campaign against Lin Feng, which consumed much of Liu's energy from 1574 to 1576, exposed these structural limits with particular clarity. Liu could diagnose Fujian's fiscal-military problems, implement targeted reforms, and even achieve tactical successes against pirate fleets—but he could not compel cooperation from Guangdong authorities who pursued fundamentally different strategies for managing the same maritime networks. The result was not failure, exactly, but a kind of exhausted accommodation: Liu's reforms created a more stable framework for Fujian's coastal defense, but one that remained dependent on arrangements he could not fully control or standardize.

## The Manila Expedition and Its Aftermath (1574-1575)

Lin Feng's attack on the nascent Spanish settlement at Manila in late 1574 marked a turning point in his career—and, indirectly, in the strategic situation Liu Yaohui faced. The expedition itself was a characteristic gamble: Lin Feng committed a substantial portion of his forces (estimates range from 2,000 to 4,000 men across 60+ vessels) to an assault on a poorly defended colonial outpost, apparently hoping to establish a permanent base beyond the reach of Ming authorities.

The Spanish sources, filtered through Jesuit historiography, present the Manila attack as a dramatic trial overcome through divine favor and Spanish valor. As Liu would later report, the pirates had been "defeated by the Luzon barbarians" (為呂宋番人攻破) and lost most of their ships, but Lin Feng himself escaped with approximately a thousand followers and immediately set about rebuilding his forces through raids on coastal settlements.

The return of Lin Feng's fleet to Chaozhou triggered the inter-provincial conflict that would consume the final year of Liu's tenure. Liu's response was immediate and aggressive: he dispatched the Fujian Regional Commander (總兵官) with a naval squadron to pursue Lin Feng into Guangdong waters, coordinating with local garrison forces to trap the pirates against the coast. The initial results were promising. In the ninth and tenth months of 1575, Fujian forces engaged Lin Feng's fleet repeatedly, reportedly sinking or burning over twenty vessels and capturing more than a hundred pirates and their dependents.

## 剿 vs. 招撫: The Fujian-Guangdong Conflict

It was at precisely this moment of apparent success that Liu's campaign ran into an obstacle more intractable than Lin Feng himself: the Guangdong provincial administration. On the sixteenth day of the tenth month, just as Fujian forces were pressing their advantage, the Guangdong 惠潮道 (the circuit intendant responsible for the Huizhou-Chaozhou region) sent word to the Fujian commander requesting that he "slow his advance" (且緩進攻) to allow for negotiations with the pirates. The intendant, a man surnamed Jin (金參政), had evidently concluded that pacification (招撫) offered a more promising path than continued military pressure.

Liu's memorial preserves his furious response to this intervention. The request to halt operations, he argued, was not merely strategically unwise but fundamentally misconceived. The pirates who had returned from Luzon were not "coerced followers" (脅從) amenable to amnesty; they were hardened criminals who had burned villages, killed soldiers, and murdered the very officials sent to negotiate with them. 招撫, Liu insisted, was a strategy appropriate only when military victory was impossible: "招撫者特力不能勝計不能剿耳—誠能何故撫之?" (Pacification is for when you lack the strength to win or the means to exterminate. If you actually can [defeat them], why pacify?)

The deeper issue, which Liu addressed with remarkable directness, was the question of competing provincial interests. Why was the Guangdong intendant so eager to claim credit for "pacifying" pirates that Fujian forces had already defeated in battle? Liu accused the Guangdong officials of inflating their reported successes—claiming to have "dispersed" (撫散) pirates who had in fact merely regrouped—while simultaneously denying Fujian forces their rightful recognition:

> 若將閩船丹留在廣似有競功之意

"If Fujian ships remain in Guangdong, it will seem as though we are competing for credit."

The accusation of "competing for credit" (競功) points toward a structural dynamic that transcended the particular personalities involved. The 剿 vs. 招撫 conflict wasn't just about military strategy—it was about which province would control the terms on which maritime networks were incorporated into quasi-legal commerce.]

Liu's memorial documents the immediate consequences of Guangdong's intervention. With Fujian forces ordered to withdraw, the surviving pirates rapidly regrouped:

> 今周孝等迯去初報賊二十餘船未數日報四十船未效日又報五十船賊愈聚而其心愈長

"Now Zhou Xiao and the others have fled. The initial report said twenty-odd pirate ships; within days it was forty; within days more it was fifty. The more the pirates gather, the bolder they become."

The pirates, far from submitting to pacification, grew increasingly demanding—requesting hostages, seeking guarantees, maneuvering for advantage. Liu's bitter conclusion was that Guangdong's strategy had not only failed to eliminate the threat but had actively strengthened it.

## Yu Dayou and the Charismatic Alternative

[This subsection develops the Liu vs. Yu Dayou comparison from your outline. Suggested structure:]

The contrast between Liu Yaohui's bureaucratic approach and the alternative represented by Yu Dayou crystallizes the structural dilemma facing Ming maritime policy in these years. Both men recognized that the "wokou" were not foreign invaders but local populations whose livelihoods depended on maritime commerce; both understood that the old garrison system (衛所) was incapable of effective coastal defense without substantial reform. But they drew fundamentally different conclusions from this shared diagnosis.

Liu, the outsider appointed by Zhang Juzheng to impose central discipline on a recalcitrant province, sought to reform existing structures through bureaucratic oversight: auditing garrisons, regulating finances, ensuring that funds reached their intended purposes. His approach assumed that the Ming state's administrative machinery could be made to function properly if only corruption were rooted out and procedures enforced. The maritime customs system he established at Haicheng exemplified this logic—bringing informal commerce under official supervision, channeling its revenues into the provincial treasury, making the illegible legible.

Yu Dayou represented a radically different model. A native of Jinjiang (晉江) in Quanzhou prefecture, Yu had spent decades building personal networks among the coastal populations he nominally commanded. His military effectiveness derived not from bureaucratic reform but from charismatic authority embedded in local society—the same gentry networks, kinship structures, and commercial relationships that sustained the "pirates" he fought against. ## Coda: The Manila Connection

Lin Feng's career traced an arc that connected Fujian's provincial politics to a genuinely global transformation: the emergence of Manila as a node in the trans-Pacific silver trade. His attack on the Spanish settlement, however unsuccessful, marked an early moment of direct encounter between Ming subjects and the expanding Hapsburg maritime network. The diplomatic aftermath—Liu's dispatch of envoys to Luzon, the Spanish mission's abortive visit to Fujian—established patterns of contact that would deepen over subsequent decades as the "Manila galleon" trade drew ever-larger quantities of American silver into the South China Sea economy.

For Liu Yaohui, the Luzon dimension of the Lin Feng affair remained peripheral—a complication in his primary struggle with Guangdong rather than a matter of central concern. But the fact that a Chaozhou pirate could sail to the Philippines, attack a European colonial settlement, and return to raid the China coast within a single year demonstrated how thoroughly the maritime world had outgrown the administrative frameworks designed to contain it. The "forward base" strategy that Liu tentatively advocated—pushing Ming military presence outward to island garrisons, developing deep-water naval capacity—was in part a response to this new reality. But it was a response that the Ming state would never fully implement, leaving the integration of Fujian into trans-Pacific commercial networks to be managed through precisely the kinds of informal, privatized arrangements that Liu had sought to reform.
