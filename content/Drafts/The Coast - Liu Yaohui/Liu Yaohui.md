
The core of this section -- Liu's mandate as Fujian xunfu was to solve the wokou issue for good in coordination with his partners in Guangdong and Zhejiang, and to thereby place coastal defense on a firm fiscal footing that would allow for remittance of silver (earned, for example, from new maritime taxes at Haicheng) back to Beijing rather than continued subsidies. Focus on the thorny situation he inherited, the basic fiscal-military deadlock he tried to break, and reasons why his purely *military* solutions failed to satisfy Zhang, setting the state for attempted *fiscal-economic* solutions under Pang. (Finally, Geng's mandate of leading qingzhang surveys pushes the problem back one step deeper, in the intractable and illegible local society itself.)

The imperial mission: Liu is instructed to inspect all coastal defenses and ensure coordination to defeat the remaining wokou. "以时巡礼郡邑团锁保甲（备/修？）辩城池，不许山海勾引毋容倭奴登岸。"

據時酌議兵食以保固海防疏
- Long memorial outlining Liu's immediate recommendations after first fill year in Fujian.
- Autumn 1573: estimates the 1574 coastal-defense budget at 150,600 taels, but even if every scheduled surtax (丁二米四, temple rents, salt surcharge, tax-deed, etc.) is collected the province will still be 40,000 taels short. Proposes to close the gap—without new levies on an already exhausted populace—by (1) temporarily earmarking the current “tenth-year” tax-deed proceeds, (2) borrowing one year of the 22,200-tael salt surcharge normally sent to the northern frontier, and (3) drawing 30,000 taels from the imperial treasury “in peace to avert future peril,” just as the court earlier advanced 400,000 taels after the 1562 fall of Putian. Concurrently asks to fill the three recently demobilized “guest-army” battalions with 3,000 Zhejiang mercenaries, restore five decommissioned warships per coastal squadron, and defer further cost-cutting so that troops “do not fight on empty stomachs.” Warning that the pirate fleets of Lin Feng (off Taiwan/琉球) and Zhu Liangbao (Guangdong border) are both poised to strike Fujian, Liu requests discretionary authority to mobilize additional reserves once revenue is secured.

查泰怠議軍餉有司以彰法典疏
- Proposals to deal with a lack of grain in the military granaries. Main issue seems to be that, of 142,000 taels levied from taxes earmarked for the provincial military, huge amounts are diverted or embezzled, such that the garrisons go unfed.
		- Digress here on the basic issue, noted as grotesque by various commentators at the time (such as Ye Chunji), that garrisons occupy prime land but cannot get enough food imports to feed their troops. The difficulties of ensuring supply either via silver or direct grain transfers. 
- b) Granaries ordered changed-over to cash commutation must immediately revert to “tax paid in kind.”  
- c) Surplus provincial silver already in the treasuries is to be spent at once on advance grain, and the Ministry ordered to authorise it.
		-Important: As of Wanli 2/3, the Fujianese authorities are *stripping back* local efforts at cash commutation of taxes; the overriding concern is to ensure enough grain in official granaries to fully supply the coastal troops, who (with their reduced numbers) cannot be expected to provide for themselves. In a way Liu's policies seem to suggests a distrust of the *kebing* (or perhaps a mandate to cut the costs of hiring them) and preference to return to local control. This goal is undermined, however, by the difficulties of ensuring compliance, especially in Zhangzhou. 

^ Similar points in 審漏驛傳疏
- Complains that the commutation to silver of the postal service requirements has ruined the system, as the funds are frequently embezzled or misspent. Requests a reversion to full corvee management of the postal relays! He specifically wants this burden to fall on large landholders, returning to the logic of unequal burdens by wealth class. 

奉旨建言民情疏
- Liu Yaohui’s memorial of early 1574 observes that after years of borrowing and juggling every available Fujian tax item to finance coastal warfare the province finally holds its own store of salt commutation silver and standing quotas; he therefore asks that, instead of forcing these modest tolls on ports, mines, temples and market boats to keep flowing to Beijing, they be permanently reserved in the provincial treasury as a special “seaward contingent fund,” to be drawn on for soldiers’ pay and hull repairs whenever fleets sail, but otherwise left in local circulation so that merchants and villagers are not bled anew each time the drums beat.
		-Attempt to regulate taxes and keep Fujian self-sufficient, but this requires reprieve from having to actually send funds to the capital.

疏通法欽以裕經用疏
- Wanli 4.5.23. Liu Yaohui forwards the provincial accounts for the new bronze-cash mint ordered by the throne in 1576. Using 1,265 jin of brass (bought with 9.4 taels of silver from each county’s “retained” revenue), Fujian’s new Fuzhou mint has already struck 10,400 coins worth 10.4 taels, yielding a clear profit of 0.97 tael; scaled up, a 100,000-coin run would net nearly 10 taels and a million coins almost 100 taels. The coins average 1.5 qian weight (slightly above the ministry’s 1.4-qian model), making them heavy enough to deter private casting yet still interchangeable with older cash. Liu therefore asks that the sample 10,000 coins now enclosed be approved and that full production proceed, its seigniorage to be recycled as capital for still larger mintings so that “mother-and-son” circulation will expand indefinitely, supplying both provincial expenses and popular currency without extra levies.
	- So Liu was also tasked with implementing Zhang's scheme for setting up brass-coin mints in Fujian, as detailed by Richard in FoF...

Liu's struggles with Lin Feng, and the development of ties with Luzon.
- Chen Jinghe on Limahong case as the moment when the Spaniards learned to "fear the Chinese;" “如此西人之親華態度固出於鼓勵華商來島以促進中菲貿易籍便獲得天主教士進入中國機會之本意，但由此次林風事件，西人始知華人之可怕；又由王望高一行之來菲，西人始知華人之狡猾；致使菲島當局親華之心裡全失." Struggles to maintain security in the waters between Fujian and Manila play an important role in shaping the policy of the nascent Manila colony towards its Chinese residents.
