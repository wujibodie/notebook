Once the "Wanli Coup" was over and Zhang Juzheng had consolidated his total control of the government, he set about appointing competent loyalists to key tasks throughout the empire. Zhang's first choice as Xunfu of Fujian was Liu Yaohui, a native of Linwu County in far southern Huguang who had taken his *jinshi* degree under Zhang and formed part of Beijing's "Chu clique" in the 1560s. Liu, stationed in Fujian from 1572-6, was the man tasked with laying the groundwork for Zhang's projected reforms in this troublesome province. It is therefore significant that his work there had nothing to do with the "Single Whip" as such, but was rather centered on the more fundamental problem of bringing lasting peace to the maritime frontier.

Our understanding of Liu's tenure as governor of Fujian is enhanced by the unusually detailed records of his official correspondence as *xunfu* reproduced in an 18th century reprint of Liu's collected writings. The collection includes not only numerous memorials submitted by Liu to the throne, but also the initial imperial edict appointing Liu to his position. This edict (certainly penned by Zhang Juzheng) sets a very clear scope of action for Liu's tenure:

詔曰：福建邇亦倭奴擾犯，加以山賊來梯，亦時命爾前去提督軍務，著授剿殺山海諸寇。以時巡歷郡邑，團練保甲，備辦城池。不許山海勾引，毋容佞奴登岸。事干軍機，預備、調度各項兵馬、糧餉，悉從宜便。應該總兵官、副總兵、游擊、守備，各府、州、縣、衛、所等官，俱聽節制。照例舉核考察施行。敢有抗違阻撓者，文官五品以下、武職三品以下，徑自拿問發落。其各衙門幷到軍民人等，敢有臨陣退縮、逗留方士者，悉許軍法從重究問。

廣潮、福漳二府事宜，有與兩廣軍門相關者，會同計議。若寇多兵少，于南贛軍門及江浙地方海道相通，若賊勢延蔓，務布同心共濟，不許區分彼此。敕內開列未盡事宜，查照兵部題准案卷，酌量時宜施行。此。敕內開列未盡事宜，查照兵部題准案卷，酌量時宜施行。此。敕內開列未盡事宜，查照兵部題准案卷，酌量時宜施行。務俾倭息民安，斯副委任。如或措處乖方，責有所歸。爾其欽哉！

"It is decreed: Fujian has lately been troubled by dwarf [Japanese] pirates and mountain bandits. Thus we now command you to go forth and oversee military affairs. You are charged with the extermination of the bandits of mountains and seas. You shall tour the prefectures and counties in turn, organizing militias (tuanlian) and community defense (*baojia*) units and preparing walls and moats. You shall not allow collusion between mountains and seas, nor allow the pirates to land on the shores. You may dispose all military matters involving preparation and adjustment of soldiers, horses, supplies, and salaries as seems fit. All relevant officers (the Regional Commander, Vice Regional Commander, Brigade Generals, and Defense Commanders, as well as all prefectural, sub-prefectural, county, garrison (*wei*), and battalion (*suo*) officials) shall be under your command. You shall recommend, audit, and evaluate them according to precedent. Should any civil officials of fifth rank or lower, or military officials of third rank or lower, dare to disobey or obstruct your orders, you have the authority to directly arrest, interrogate, and punish them. Furthermore you are authorized to follow military law in investigating and punishing any *yamen* personnel, as well as soldiers or civilians, who dare to flee from battle or deliberately delay deployment. 

In matters relating to the two prefectures of Chaozhou in Guangdong and Zhangzhou in Fujian which involve the Supreme Commander of Liangguang, you shall consult and deliberate with him. If the bandits are numerous and our troops insufficient, coordinate with the Supreme Commanders of Nan-Gan and Jiang-Zhe. If the momentum tips in the bandits' favor, you must work together with one heart and are forbidden to make distinctions between your jurisdictions. For any matters not discussed in this edict, you shall consult the archive of memorials approved by the Ministry of War and act as you consider the situation demands. Work to pacify the Japanese pirates and bring peace to the people; then you will have fulfilled this commission. If, however, your management is improper, the responsibility will rest with you. Take heed and respect these orders!"

Liu's responsibilities in Fujian were chiefly military, and specifically involved logistical and administrative reform rather than strategic oversight of a single campaign. He was tasked above all with inspecting and reorganizing Fujian's entire military hierarchy, from local militias to the high officers, to bring a lasting peace to the beleaguered coast. The language of the edict makes it clear that "exterminating" the bandits in pitched battle was a lesser priority compared to reorganizing local society to be capable of self-defense and immune to "collusion" with the pirates. 

The edict's special emphasis on the prefectures of Chaozhou and Zhangzhou, on either side of the border between Guangdong and Fujian provinces, evinces an awareness of the most problematic region in the 16th century maritime framework. The boundary between provinces was often porous for private individuals, who routinely circulated across it as traders, smugglers, itinerant laborers, and raiders, but highly problematic for the Ming military, as Fujian and Guangdong belonged to entirely separate military circuits with their own hierarchies and loyalties. The tendency for provincial militaries to butt heads was clearly anticipated by Zhang, but no amount of cajoling from the center could actually overcome the competition and mutual distrust between provincial generals in practice. The major campaign of Liu's tenure, against the "wokou" native of Chaozhou, Lin Feng, floundered against consistent disagreements with the Liangguang army and navy. 

In addition to the official edict promulgated in the emperor’s name, Liu also received private instructions in the form of a frank letter by Zhang Juzheng: 
闽中数年无警，当事者务沽节省虚名，以致缓急无措，误事非小。公所请者，已属该部一一覆行。俞帅老奸，志意隳，难以复用，非新壮将军，不能办此。胡君旧在闽中，颇著战功，锐于功名，惟公结以诚信，激以忠义，必能有所建也。用兵机宜，难以遥度，瓯岭以东，一付于公。
This letter goes beyond the broad proclamations of the edict to find fault in previous office holders in Fujian. Since Fujian has been relatively calm for a number of years, local officials have slacked defenses in the name of false “frugality;” thus Zhang frames Liu’s real task as personally inspecting each individual military installation to ensure their readiness. Zhang then makes a crucial personnel recommendation: he advises working around the famous Yu Dayou, an “old scoundrel” whose will has slackened, and instead entrusting key tasks to Hu Shouren, a veteran officer in the naval administration. Finally, Zhang admits that his position in Beijing is too distant to judge the military realities in Fujian, and ensures that he will trust everything in the region to Liu’s own judgement. 

Liu made his first comprehensive report from the ground in a memorial of autumn 1573, the 據時酌議兵食以保固海防疏. The most pressing issue Liu identified was an immediate budget shortfall: he estimated that the province would be some 40,000 taels short of their 150,600 tael budget for coastal defense even if all emergency surcharges continued to be collected in full. Liu proposed to close the gap without new levies on an already exhausted populace by temporarily withholding certain grain and salt taxes (otherwise earmarked for the northern frontier) as well as requesting a direct transfer of 30,000 taels from the imperial treasury. He concurrently advised to avoid or reverse cost-cutting measures, restoring 3,000 recently demobilized "guest-soldier" mercenaries along with five decommissioned warships per coastal squadron. Budget cuts were impossible because at the time of writing two pirate fleets were poised to strike Fujian from just outside its coastal waters: that of Lin Feng off "Ryukyu" (琉球; in fact, probably Taiwan) and Zhu Liangbao (across the Guangdong border). 

The remaining memorials sent by Liu to the court during his tenure in Fujian all focus on two sets of issues: setting Fujian’s local defenses on a firm fiscal basis, and revising the military framework to ensure real readiness. The two issues came together in Fujian’s coastal garrisons (*weisuo*), legacies of the demonetized corvée economy of the Ming founding which had shriveled to near-uselessness by the 16th century. The main current of military reform in the Wokou crisis of the 1550s-60s had tended to route around these derelict institutions by empowering professionalized mercenary armies led by charismatic reformers like Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou. These professional forces had convincingly triumphed in the Zhejiang anti-wokou campaigns of the 1560s, when multiple such forces spearheaded coordinated campaigns under the tenacious Supreme Commander Hu Zongxian. Following the destruction of the major pirate base at Shuangyu, an island in the Zhoushan archipelago, a portion of the professional armies had shifted to Minnan to pursue anti-piratical actions there, and became ensconced as the so-called “guest armies” (*kebing* 客兵). While effective in battle, these troops were problematic for several reasons: their foreign cultural background caused clashes with locals where they were stationed; because they existed outside normal Ming military structures, there was a danger of their being more loyal to their commanders than the dynasty as a whole; and most importantly, they demanded their pay in silver, meaning that keeping them in a state of permanent readiness would require the province to source the requisite funds for their pay. 

In the 1560s, when wokou raids in Minnan were at their worst, there could be no question of dispensing of the “guest armies,” and the silver needed to pay them was extracted from local society via a series of creative levies. Tan Lun, a veteran of the Zhejiang campaigns who served as Xunfu from 1563-5, pioneered the use of extraordinary silver levies to pay his mercenaries’ salaries, normally requisitioning them in the form of surcharges to the ordinary annual taxes calculated as fractions of the main tax categories: the *dingsimiba* ()…. And the taxes on monasteries. It was precisely these extraordinary silver levies which the previous Xunfu administrations, in the relative peace of the Longqing years, had attempted to do away with in the name of “frugality.” However, doing away with the stipendiary professionals would put the hard-won pacification of the coast at risk. Liu's challenge was therefore to secure the coast in order to cut his silver expenses, while simultaneously needing to spend silver in order to secure the coast.
