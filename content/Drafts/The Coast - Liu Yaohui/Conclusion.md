Note on exteriorization, customs, and extermination: Liu advocates "extermination" of the illegible traders at the interstitial points, and an embrace of fully visible and regulated trade at a central, controllable node (Haicheng). Those who wish to participate in the commerce of the coasts must do so within clear, visible limits, and anyone who wishes to break free of those limits must flee the Ming space entirely; even then, the agents of the state can track them. Note, though, that the outer garrisons are expensive: maintaining the youbing drains 10s of 1000s of silver per year. He Qiaoyuan credits the Wuyu commander Shen Yourong with implementing the most logical extension of this logic: recruiting merchants to spy on their fellows abroad. Splitting Nan'ao in half is a way to deny smugglers an important interstice, while allowing authorities of both provinces to exercise some oversight over one another.

Liu’s customs system, forward bases, and de-silverization weren't contradictory but complementary attempts to map and tax maritime space. He discovered that the maritime economy could fund its own defense if the state could register it. Pang and Geng would later apply this insight to land through the Single Whip.
