

Chen Boyi discusses the migration of the core trading posts around the rim of the Xiamen Bay in his article "From Yuegang to Anhai." Chen lists 7 reasons given in the literature for the shift from Yuegang to Anhai, all of which he dismisses either for not having correct timing or for being general phenomena that can’t explain changes between individual ports: 1. Inconsistent maritime closure policies; 2. Excessive state taxation; 3. Qing invasion and maritime evictions -> rise of Xiamen; 4. Collapse of the tribute trade system; 5. Geographic factors, particularly silting of the Jiulongjiang river mouth (but Chen sees over-silting as a result of commercial decline rather than a cause); 6. Western aggression; 7. Devastation by pirate/wokou raids. Rather than any of these relatively "structural" explanations, Chen favors an explanation based on the contingent social relationships within the broader interlocking structures of Chinese-Western commercial competition on one hand, and the anti-piratical strategies of the Ming court on the other. Yuegang happened to have the largest surviving concentration of overseas traders when the trade was legalized in 1567, and so was chosen as site of the customs post; from that point on it grew, above all because of the relationship its traders established with Manila, until the arrival of the Dutch after 1620 closed this important route to all but Dutch-affiliated traders, of whom the most successful was Zheng Zhilong of Anhai. Once Zheng established his political dominance over the Minnan coast and its radiating trade routes, capital also concentrated in his headquarters of Anhai (as well as his secondary base on Xiamen). 
What is important for our purposes in Chen's explanation is its emphasis on the socio-political arrangements that explain the concentration of traders and capital at given sites in the broader region across periods of institutional transition. Birgit Tremml-Werner has noted that former "wokou" bases which survived decades of suppression became centers of legalized trade once maritime policy liberalized. 

The shifting boundaries of the coastal lands caused endless disputes among claimants, leading to headaches for local officials, as in this weary anecdote by the Quanzhou magistrate Chen Maoren: "鷺鶿洲田，本海沙漲出，因莖戶欺隱，沒為學田，民佃輸賦，以資膏燭，頗為有力所侵，幾致激變。監司檄餘監，以綏農人。因思不日歸耕，如淵明於下潠田舍獲釋鑱詠歌，翛然自適，無復作風塵面目，看人眉頰也."


In a 1541 letter to an unnamed coastal defense commissioner (*haidao*), Lin Xiyuan inadvertently provides an illuminating survey of the Xiamen Bay economy. He describes the pirates ransacking the island of Jiahe and raiding along the rim of the bay, "killing residents, defiling women, and demanding silver for ransom." Besides these direct harms, piracy has brought about famine: the Anping marketplace is empty, and garrison troops have been forced to travel far away to buy grain. In these straitened circumstances, the bandits are able to recruit soldiers by paying their families one tael of silver and a shi of rice, plus half a tael for equipment. Lin thus implores the haidao to offer more generous pay for state soldiers, including 10 taels in funeral expenses for soldiers killed in battle. Only by outmatching the bandits in generous silver payments can the state wrest back control over coastal populations. Lin's arguments at this time foreshadow the professionalization of Minnan's major coastal defense forces which was to follow in later decades.


Paragraph on the establishment of permanent xunfus for greater central oversight, but also creating a more cohesive "provincial" administration:
Zhu Wan's failure in Fujian proved the difficulty of managing this province without extensive understanding of local conditions. As a result, the joint Min-Zhe surveillance commission was largely abolished, and Fujian instead began to be governed by a *xunfu* appointed exclusively to that province. The first in what was to be an unbroken line of *xunfus* was Ruan E, appointed in 1557. (Fn: Officials had been appointed *xunfu* of Fujian in the past, but this had been an extraordinary rather than a routine post.) The office of *xunfu,* often translated as "viceroy" or "governor," was unique in that it combined oversight (backed by powers of impeachment) over all other civil and military officials in the province, but was itself a part of the court censorial bureaucracy. While most *xunfus* operated more-or-less as governors of their given provinces, responsible for ensuring the promulgation of overall policies and effective day-to-day administration, they were also in direct contact with the throne and derived their authority from that contact, having few clearly-defined duties in the province. (Fn: This contrasted to the other high officials with province-wide powers, the *buzhengsi shi* and *zongbing*, each of whom headed specific bureaucracies and was bound by clear routines.) Observers interested in the institutional development of Fujian as a political unit did not fail to note the importance of the *xunfu*-ship in creating a cohesive "provincial" administration under central oversight. (Fn: He Qiaoyuan, Zheng Zaoqing, Gu Yanwu all commented on this. Cf. 鄭造卿, 閩中經略議, reproduced in Gu, Tianxia Junguo Libing Shu, Fujian.) 

Though the role of *xunfu* as "governor" became institutionalized after the 1550s, it retained a personalistic and improvisational character. Each *xunfu* was basically responsible for carrying out the priorities of the Grand Secretariat, which assigned them to their post, and the court's expectations would be spelled out both in initial edicts of appointment and in correspondence throughout the *xunfu*'s tenure. Despite their lofty rank, *xunfu*s were vulnerable to recall at any time if they disappointed Beijing's expectations, and each *xunfu* thus strove to satisfy those expectations as he understood them, often in idiosyncratic ways. It is the highly personal nature of *xunfu* administration which makes them excellent subjects for an institutional history, since -- while they were not all-powerful, and as Xiong's quote opening this chapter demonstrates, were normally outsiders learning about Fujian even as they governed it -- their various tenures tended to mark genuinely distinct periods of policy-making in the province. Periodization by *xunfu* rather than by emperor is thus a natural way of marking time in Fujian. Further, because *xunfus* were generally drawn from the most prominent ranks of officialdom, their collected writings tend to survive in published form, making each individual *xunfu* a node for correspondence relating to Fujian in their time period -- most often this includes correspondence with the Grand Secretaries who set their agendas in office, allowing a glimpse at the back-channels of Ming governance. 
The channel of communication between Secretariat and provincial censoriate was particularly important during the career of Zhang Juzheng, member of the *Neige* from 1567 and Chief Grand Secretary from 1572-82. Zhang governed the empire as much through private correspondence as through official edicts, and in the case of Fujian, his communications with the governors he appointed do much to shed light on how Fujian was perceived by Beijing, and where it fit into Zhang's broader reformist program. In many cases looking at the figures of the *xunfu*, far from confirming the impression of Zhang's authoritarian control over the empire, reveal the severe limitations of his -- and by extension, the central court's -- grasp on provincial policy. 
Zhang's time in power, the famous "Long-Wan Reform Era," is best associated with two policies: the opening of the sea ban after 1567 and the empire-wide enactment of the Single Whip taxation reform. Both policies are typically connected with the sea change occurring in Fujian's political economy during this same period.  However, as will be seen in the succeeding chapters, the implementation of these policies in Fujian was neither smooth nor complete; in fact, it is misleading to speak of the Single Whip being "enacted" in Fujian at all, at least in the form it took in other provinces. Similarly, while Fujian certainly did "open" to the sea, this opening cannot be easily dated to 1567, nor can it be attributed primarily to directives from the center. 

The chapters which follow trace the policy changes of the critical years between 1557 and 1583, in order to revise our understanding of Fujian's place in the late Ming empire. The core emphasis of these chapters is on the distinctiveness of Fujian's political economy within the broader Ming world, and how this led to a fundamentally different institutional arrangement than was found in other comparable regions. 

Chapter Two lays out the comparative case by providing a synoptic view of "silverizing" fiscal reform across the Ming empire. Over several decades a set of policy experiments at the local level in the wealthy coastal regions coalesced into a coherent "reform package" which was first extended to cover the whole provinces where it originated and then exported wholesale to other provinces. This "reform package" became known as the "Single Whip," and it was by this name that it was eventually promoted across the entire Ming empire. The key provinces involved in this "bottom-up" development were Fujian's neighbors of Guangdong and Zhejiang, which in many ways had comparable economic and social situations to Fujian. However, Fujian -- which, as we have seen, did experience commercial and fiscal "silverization" of its own -- was alone in remaining uninvolved in these experiments. 

Why and how Fujian's experience of silverization followed a different path from that of its neighbors is the focus of the core middle chapters of the dissertation, covering the tenures of several of the most important *xunfus* in this era. Chapter Three will focus on the tenures of Tan Lun, Wang Daokun, and Tu Zeming, anti-pirate experts appointed to deal with a disastrous incursion of "*wokou*" in the  1560s. As part of their comprehensive overhaul of Fujian's coastal defenses, Tan and Wang committed the province to substantial silver outlays for professional mercenary forces (the Zhejiang "guest troops" led by Qi Jiguang). These outlays were largely paid for by silver-denominated surcharges on the standard land and capitation taxes. In part due to the burden these professional forces represented, and in part for genuine ideological sympathy for private commerce, the governors of the 1560s largely pursued a policy of accommodation with the private smugglers of Xiamen Bay -- the same cliques which caused the death of Zhu Wan -- ending in the elevation of Yuegang to a county seat under the new name of Haicheng in 1566. While Haicheng's creation was a watershed moment, the specific institutional arrangements under which Haicheng's traders were allowed to seek profits overseas, and by which the provincial administration tapped a portion of these profits, were more complex than preceding historiography has acknowledged, and were only developed after years of experimentation, largely at a very local level. 

Chapter Four moves to the tenure of Liu Yaohui, Zhang Juzheng's first hand-picked appointment to the post of Fujian *xunfu*. Liu's tenure (1573-6) corresponded with the last wave of major anti-piracy campaigns, which he pursued while working to undo many of Tan and Wang's previous policies. Appointed initially with a mandate to reduce budget deficits, Liu ultimately embraced a flexible overseas policy which relied on deep-sea naval actions and muscular foreign diplomacy, paid for not by land taxes, but by customs on the overseas shipping of recently-established Haicheng province. Thus, Liu moved Fujian towards institutional reliance on maritime trade rather than land-based taxation -- a policy which would be partially reversed by his successors. 

Chapter Five traces the careers of two of Liu's successors, Pang Shangpeng and Geng Dingxiang, who were tasked by Zhang Juzheng with actually implementing the Single Whip in Fujian. Pang, among the initial champions of the Single Whip, is universally credited with instituting that reform in Fujian -- but what the "Single Whip" meant in Fujian was, I argue, something far simpler and more limited than elsewhere in the empire. Among other limitations, Fujian's registered landed tax base was simply too small to supply the province's needs according to the standard Single Whip schedule. Geng Dingxiang's chief task as *xunfu* was to rectify this by implementing a comprehensive resurvey of land and population; but the survey was resisted by the local population and was only partially complete when the death of Zhang Juzheng put an end to the project. Fujian was left, after 1583, with a fairly minimal  burden of taxation bound for the center, and a provincial military economy which depended on the substantial but uneven revenues supplied by Haicheng's overseas private trade.

The Conclusion will trace the consequences of this arrangement forward to the end of the Ming dynasty. In the decades after Zhang's death, real power in Fujian would increasingly come to rest on the shores of Xiamen Bay, and control over shipping through that bay would be the main object of contestation between "state" and "society." A number of episodes of violent confrontation over that shipping -- from the eunuch Gao Cai's attempts to route customs to the capital and intervene in the Manila trade, to the challenge posed by the Dutch East India Company in the 1620s, to the clash between Admiral Yu Zigao and the pirate-adventurer Zheng Zhilong -- would end with the consolidation of military authority and private shipping under the Zheng clan of Anhai. Ultimately, the Zheng clan's evolution into a proto-state "regime" capable of rivaling the Qing Dynasty and the VOC occurred within the institutional niche opened up by Fujian's checkered reform process in the time of Zhang Juzheng.
