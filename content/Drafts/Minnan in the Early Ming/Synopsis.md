**枯槎燒盡有根在，春雨一洗皆萌芽。- Su Shi** （Re： sprouts and transition.)

Pre-Ming centrality of Quanzhou was disrupted in the transition, with the persecution of the economically dominant Muslim community and designation of Fuzhou as tributary port breaking main overseas connections. Thereafter Quanzhou city, while remaining an important center, slowly "diffused" into the countryside, particularly along the rim of Xiamen Bay and the Jiulong Delta, which became the most dynamic part of the region. Late and quick assimilation into the Ming means Fujian is an institutional afterthought: little guantian, no imperial princes, a unique weisuo system mainly enrolling the local tanka population. Thus, Fujian remains somewhat outside the Jiangnan reformist discourses attacking tax evasion on guantian and abuses by great princely and private (gentry) estates.

Buddhist monasteries: dominant in the Song, restricted heavily in the early Ming, then attacked by Confucian polemicists in Zhengde and Jiajing reigns and exploited for silver in the wokou crisis. By 1570 the monasteries have almost been extirpated, but the 寺田 land is an important (because newer) source of local government revenues (silver and grain). There is also substantial dispute and litigation over who should own the confiscated lands: former small tenants or gentry landlords. 

De-urbanization and commercial decay through 15th century; clear uptick in commercialization, especially overseas trade, after ~1500. Hokkiens in Malacca, Pattani, and Japan as well as across China - contacts with Portuguese. 

Rise of Zhangzhou: problematic "malarial" border region becomes urbanized center; Jiulongjiang delta as major site of land reclamation, fishing, and overseas trade (European accounts of horizon full of sails). Security challenges and proliferation of *xian* in Zhangzhou as well as Chaozhou. Difficulty of separating the two prefectures. 

(Ending point: 1560s wokou crisis, urban wall-building, the kebing under Tan Lun/Qi Jiguang, solution found in creating Haicheng County and regulating legal Nanyang trade. Think of the first section as covering "**Fujian in the Ming**" which means both "**the history of Fujian during ~1368-1644**", but also how Fujian fit into the broader Ming empire as a whole. This entails understanding Ming institutions and how these changed, as well as how Fujian fit into these empire-wide institutions distinctly from other regions, how it was shaped by them and how they adapted to local conditions.

**3 key pillars of the Ming system to focus on:**

1. **land use** (**官** vs **民田**; princely estates + temples);

2. **defense and trade** (the **卫所** \[Weisuo] + their commercialization budgets, connection to the policy of the "**coasts**")

3. **taxation and silverization**. These 3 points are intimately tied – see **Gu Cheng**, **Liu Zhiwei**.

***

First section should carry us from **Song-Yuan-Ming transition**, through mid-Ming, to **1563-7** the ascendance of **Tan Lun**, the piracy, silverization, and the opening of **Haicheng**.

> "Scholars often take the **1567** lifting of the sea ban, and opening of **Haicheng** to legal trade — or the transition from "**Moon Harbor**" to "**Tide City**" — as a dramatic turning point, but I argue it should be seen as one moment in a slow process."
