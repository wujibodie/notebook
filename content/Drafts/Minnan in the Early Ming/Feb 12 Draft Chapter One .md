# Chapter One: Minnan in the Early Ming

**枯槎燒盡有根在，春雨一洗皆萌芽**。  
Burn withered twigs to ash, and there will still be roots; /  
just one wash of spring rain, and all will sprout anew.   
\- Su Shi[^1]

One Surveillance Commissioner of Fujian, Xiong Wencan, described the province he governed as follows: 

“Min leans against mountains and is hemmed in by the sea. Its upland districts brush against Cantonese soil: bandits gather in hidden marshes and maraud from pond to pond, and alarms are often sounded. The districts south \[downhill\] of the mountain ranges are arranged like scattered stars along the rim of the roiling, azure waves: in the past they were tormented by the Japanese wretches, while lately their tormentors have been red-haired foreigners. Further, as their lands are squeezed and their people dense, their grain is insufficient, such that they are now less worried about the savages on their frontier than about their own population. Foreign money flashes within while food and goods stream abroad; the ocean ships thereby give the people their lease on life, and so Zhongzuo has become the critical chokepoint.” [^2]

Xiong was writing in 1619, in a preface which he provided for the most monumental late-Ming encyclopedia of Fujian, the *Min Shu* by He Qiaoyuan. As the highest state official in the province, Xiong would have been approached to give his blessing to the project of writing a comprehensive provincial gazetteer, even though the publication itself was privately conceived and funded, and it was only natural that his stamp of approval was placed at the very start of the final printed edition.[^3] Unlike He Qiaoyuan himself, and the book’s other prestigious preface writers (including the Grand Secretary Ye Xianggao, who actually outranked Xiong in the imperial bureaucracy, but not the provincial hierarchy of honors), Xiong was no native of Fujian, being stationed there for a tenure of just a few years. His introduction to the work thus provides an outsider’s view of the region, or more precisely, the view of an agent responsible to the central court. (This dissertation will rely heavily on the views of Surveillance Commissioners, or *xunfus*, like Xiong, since by the late Ming they exercised the powers of a viceroy or governor and were the most direct link between local administrations and the imperial court.) What, then, did Xiong, speaking in the emperor’s name, see in Fujian? 

The immediate feature which distinguishes Fujian from elsewhere is its location between mountains and sea. Its highland districts are intractable and prone to internal turmoil (especially near the provincial border with Guangdong), while the constellation of fertile coastal plains, home to the densest nodes of population, suffer from the twin perils of foreign attack and scarcity of food. Xiong’s point that the “lands are squeezed and the people dense” is a stereotyped formula which appears with slight variations in virtually every description of Fujian from the whole late imperial era. This constant of Fujianese life had, however, transformed within Xiong’s own lifetime: following the legalization of maritime trade in 1567, “foreign money” and “ocean-going” ships had become vital parts of Fujianese life, providing a “lifeline” for its inhabitants, and shifting the province’s economic center towards the region around Xiamen Bay. 

The settlement of Zhongzuo which Xiong called the whole province’s “critical chokepoint” began as a military garrison on an otherwise unpopulated island then known as Jiahe, a subordinate part of the Wuyu garrison, located about halfway between the important ports of Haicheng and Anping. As the waters around it became ever more thickly populated with merchants, fishermen, and pirates, this military base slowly civilianized and transformed into an important market. In Xiong’s time it merely commanded the most strategic portion of the Fujianese coast, but in the following decades more and more of the commerce of the region came to be concentrated there. This metamorphosis of a state garrison into a commercial hub encapsulates the broader transformation of the province. While Zhongzuo would later become famous, under its modern name of Xiamen, as the base of the warlord Zheng Chenggong, in the period of this study it was merely the most visible symptom of a deeper shift as the geography of coastal defense blended with the geography of international commerce. 

The long story of Fujian’s transformation which eventually gave rise to Xiamen, and the interrelation in that story of the elements of land and sea, of the military, fiscal, and commercial constraints and opportunities which that geographic situation afforded the world of Minnan, and of how this unique region fit into the broader Ming empire, forms the core focus of this dissertation. The Ming was wracked almost from birth by a series of crises resulting from the basic mismatch of its economic, fiscal, and military policies – based on the founding emperor’s vision of a landlocked, agrarian, demonetized society – with the realities of life in the lands the empire inherited. Nowhere were these contradictions more apparent than Fujian, which was structurally dependent in a way no other part of China was on overseas trade, maritime products, and widespread use of monetary metals. Fujian is thus a key site to analyze the transition of the whole economy to a silver basis and the mobilization of money for military purposes which swept all of China in the 16th century. To tell this story as clearly as possible, however, we first need to specify what we mean by the “Fujian” at the center of it. 

**Geography**

The political borders of Fujian province were largely static in the period under question: it was bounded by Zhejiang to the northeast, Jiangxi to the northwest, Guangdong to the southwest, and the ocean to the southeast. Although political power within that space was centralized in the civilian administration at Fuzhou, important cultural, economic, and logistical cleavages existed between portions of the province. I will largely follow Wu Xiu’an’s basic division between the coast and interior as two regions with distinct courses of economic development. The “Three Ranges” of central Fujian, while not completely impenetrable, were effective enough impediments to communication between the mountainous interior and coastal plains that we can safely restrict our study to the four coastal prefectures: Fuzhou, Xinghua, Quanzhou, and Zhangzhou. In the Ming Dynasty, these four prefectures were sometimes collectively referred to as “Minnan,” “south Min,” while at other times that term referred only to Quanzhou and Zhangzhou.[^4] Quanzhou and Zhangzhou shared a relatively contiguous coastal plain stretching from the easternmost outcroppings of Jinjiang and Hui’an across the rim of Xiamen Bay to the delta of the Jiulong River, and a certain distance upstream, until roughly the point where the confluence of several tributaries creates a relatively broad and navigable river with a sizable floodplain. This whole space was one of just two really sizable portions of arable land along the coast, with Fuzhou controlling the other. While Fuzhou was the political capital of the province, and after 1488 home to the province’s only official tributary customs office, Quanzhou and Zhangzhou formed a larger cultural and demographic center, and were far more deeply enmeshed in oceangoing trade. For that reason, most of this dissertation will focus on those two prefectures, and the definition of “Minnan” used here will be the modern one, limited to Zhang-Quan. “Fujian” will refer to the whole province as a fiscal-political unity, but even then the weight will normally be on the coast. 

Even our scope within these prefectures can be defined more narrowly. Both Zhangzhou and Quanzhou had political jurisdiction over several mountainous counties which, while economically integrated into the coastal markets, still followed quite distinct paths. The phenomena which really concern us are those of the coastal portions of these prefectures, roughly ten counties running from Hui’an to Zhao’an. These counties ran up against counterparts in Xinghua and Chaozhou (Guangdong), and in certain respects were closely connected to them, but the political boundaries (particularly between provinces) were as consequential as they were arguably arbitrary. Within coastal Minnan, the key economic corridor ran between the two prefectural capitals, through the counties of Jinjiang, Nan’an, Tong’an, Haicheng, and Longxi, of which all but Longxi bordered Xiamen Bay.

The city of Quanzhou, extolled as one of the world's largest and wealthiest by 13th century travelers like Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta, looms large in the history of Minnan, but by the mid-Ming was in deep decline.

The maritime economy was important to Minnan from its earliest appearances in Chinese historical records, but Quanzhou first became an important port in the market system of China as a whole during the Tang-Yuan transition, when the small independent kingdom of Min relied on trade with Southeast Asia to provide necessary revenues. After Min was absorbed into the Song, Quanzhou remained an important entrepot, but it was a decidedly secondary halfway point between the great centers at Guangzhou and Mingzhou. With the fall of Kaifeng to the Jurchens and the Song court's move south of the Yangtze, Quanzhou gained two important advantages: first, its traders' pre-existing ties with Zhejiang meant that it benefitted from the stimulus provided by the new court at Hangzhou, and second, Quanzhou itself became home to a large contingent of imperial clansmen, stationed in the far south for safety in case of further invasions. These clansmen, who numbered over 1,000 by the end of the Southern Song, received substantial state stipends, and thus constituted an important source of demand for local and exotic luxuries; even more importantly, they tended to invest their fortunes directly in maritime ventures, such that a substantial portion of the cargo recovered from Southern Song shipwrecks with identifiable ownership belonged to Zhao clansmen. Another group crucial to the city's mature trade was the population settled foreign sojourners, who numbered in the tens of thousands and frequently cooperated with Chinese partners on joint voyages.

Rise of Quanzhou city from the Min Kingdom to the Southern Song; basis in local crafts, institutions for pooling of risk and distribution of capital, imperial clansmen and monasteries as major players, foreign quarters and question of quantitative weight of Fujianese voyages vs. foreign sojourners (though both were important). By the eleventh century Quanzhou's narrow hinterland could not provide enough food for both its own producers and the swelling city, and so the region became structurally dependent on import of staple foods – a situation not fundamentally changed by the upheavals of subsequent centuries.[^5] (Minnan was likely one of the earliest regions of the world to import a majority of its grain without being a major political center or resource colony.) I tend to agree with So that while silk was certainly a major export from Quanzhou, it is doubtful that much of the _zaytuni_ referenced in the Cairo Geniza as circulating around the Eastern Mediterranean was actually from Quanzhou; likelier this was a name given to Western imitations ("satin" as a broad variety of silk textile).[^6]


When Gaozong's fears were realized and Hangzhou finally fell to northern invaders, Quanzhou turned out not to be a safe refuge after all: before the Mongols had even reached the city, a faction of the populace led by the Persian Pu Shougeng surrendered the city without a fight, and slaughtered the Zhao clansmen living there as a show of loyalty. The new Yuan Dynasty rewarded Pu with overlordship over Minnan, which his family retained until the fall of that dynasty 80 years later. Under the Yuan, Quanzhou remained the most important Chinese port city, but its trade was dominated by foreign sojourners, above all the Persian or Turkic clients of the emperor under the _ortoq_ system. 
Fall of the Yuan, slaughter of the Pu clan, civil war and pogroms against Muslims in Quanzhou, Chen Youding’s warlord regime and general movement towards the interior; Ming conquest takes a depleted Quanzhou as an afterthought, one of the last regions to be unified, and integrated in relatively haphazard way, separate from the “standard” process in central/northern China, but similar to Guangdong – the founding doesn't really suppress Minnan's agrarian economy, but it does rip the heart out of the region's former economic role, which has to be slowly reconstituted. The whole infrastructure of Quanzhou's golden age as perhaps the world's greatest port city was ripped out by the destruction of its foreign communities, and the subsequent years of strict coastal surveillance left little room for its indigenous families to rebuild that glory. When Minnan again rose to become an important center of international trade, that trade was protagonized by fundamentally rural, corporate lineage organizations anchored to relatively small and competitive port towns, who exclusively sojourned and settled and conducted their business abroad, rather than by domestic networks welcoming and cooperating with seaborne merchants from abroad.[^7] This makes the later Ming-Qing era of Fujianese trade an entirely different beast from Marco Polo's Zaytoun. 

For Ming intellectuals, Zhu Xi's passage through the region, rather than Quanzhou's era of prosperity, was the key turning point in Minnan's history. His presence elevated this remote and barbarous region to the very center of the Chinese cultural universe. The apellation of "Zou-Lu by the sea," referring to the home states of Mencius and Confucius, was equally as shopworn as the cliche about Fujian being "squeezed and densely peopled." Every county or major settlement had its altar to "Zhu, Master of Literature," normally in pride of place near the local academy or examination hall.[^8] Zhu Xi was credited with having essentially founded the region's proud tradition of learning, and his reputation was linked with the status of Minnan itself.Therefore, it is hardly surprising that local intellectuals tended to defend Zhu Xi's teachings quite fiercely against all criticism, and were highly resistant to the wave of reformist thought radiating from the followers of Wang Yangming after about 1520. The first generation of local intellectuals after Wang Yangming included three leading lights, Chen Chen, Zhang Yue, and Lin Xiyuan, each of whom "specialized in Cheng-Zhu (thought) and rejected Wang (Yangming)."[^9] These three men were the vanguard of what would be a torrent of successful degree-takers from south Fujian in the late Ming, and they left their mark on the culture of their successors by a variety of means, from exam primers to inscriptions on the walls of the academies.[^10] This loyalty to the venerable local orthodoxy in a time when that tradition was being challenged by reformists would have more than purely abstract consequences, as it meant that men educated locally were, with a few notable exceptions, resistant to the language in which the major policy reforms of the period were couched.
 
## Land and People

The basic principle of the fiscal system implemented by the Hongwu Emperor was for taxes to be assessed in agrarian commodities, above all grain, but this was a singularly poor fit for Fujian: the province simply did not possess enough agricultural land for tax payment in the form of cultivated products to be feasible. During the Southern Song and Yuan, heavy commercial taxes had made Quanzhou among the most fiscally important commanderies in the empire, but Zhu Yuanzhang's aversion to such taxes meant that Fujian's was almost invisible in the broader tax structure of the empire. In the Hongwu era land surveys Fujian had the smallest amount of cultivable land per household (17 mu) of any province. Correspondingly, Fujian’s overall land tax quotas were among the lightest in the empire, representing a mere 1.5% of the total.[^11] In part, this was a result of Fujian's late incorporation into the Ming, which meant that it was not a vital part of the early dynastic war machine and could thus be tolerated as a revenue-neutral, rather than revenue-raising, province. One consequence of this was that virtually all land was registered to private owners; the great tracts of "state land" (*guantian*) and the princely fiefs which dotted the core regions of the empire were nearly absent. While the Ming emperors may have been little concerned with drawing funds from Fujian, their neglect of the province's taxation allowed considerable confusion to take root, which later administrators had to contend with.
\[Section on the lijia and its failure to take root in Fujian.\]  
To understand the basic situation of the land tax in mid-late Ming Fujian, we can take the Jiajing-reign county gazetteer of Yongchun as our sample. This gazetteer, for a riverine, hilly county of Quanzhou prefecture, is a good sample for two reasons: first, the county itself, located a short ways inland from the prefectural capital of Quanzhou, was relatively average within the broader region: neither exceptionally poor nor especially rich, connected to coastal markets but not itself home to any ports, and without a major city to distort its land-to-people ratios. The second reason is the compiler: Lin Xiyuan, the chief editor of this gazetteer, was among the most prominent gentry figures in the nearby coastal county of Tong’an, indeed perhaps the single most famous and representative of his generation’s breed of rising rural lineage heads who made their names and fortunes outside the official bureaucracy. His perspective can thus stand in for the emerging worldview of the coastal gentry which was at just this time coming to dominate life in Fujian.   
Lin’s account of Ming-era taxes in Yongchun begins by explaining that land is divided into two categories: privately-owned “civilian land” (*mintian* 民田) which is all legally identical, and “state land” (*guantian* 官田), which is divided several varieties: *guan* 官 (long-standing government land), *zhi* 職 (land earmarked for bureaucratic salaries), *moguan* 沒官 (land seized at some point by the state, e.g. from executed criminals), *feisi* 廢寺 (only example given is Song precedent, the closing of 大理寺), *fuxue* 府學 (land belonging to the prefectural academies), *xianxue* 縣學 (ditto the county schools). Civilian land was grouped into various quality grades and then assessed a tax of so much grain per *mu* at consistent rates for each grade; in general, the tax grain from this category was all destined to be shipped to official state granaries at taxpayer expense. State land, by contrast, had wildly varying schedules of taxation and was earmarked to cover the needs of a variety of different bodies; the basic practice on this land was to rent it at high rates to private tenants, whose rent would cover the assessed value of the tax; whether this was an attractive option for renters varied, naturally, with tax rates and rental conditions in the county’s private lands. Because state land was the chief source of land tax revenue destined for local (county, prefectural, or provincial) uses, and because it was so flexible and diverse in its categorizations, it tended to be the main site of fiscal reform by local administrators seeking to expand their available revenues.   
Yongchun’s very first land survey, conducted in Hongwu 24 (1392), registered farmland (*tian* 田) in the amount of 2437 *qing*, 80 *mu*. In the following resurvey of Yongle 10 (1412), however, the tally dropped by a third to 1631 *qing*. This figure was then virtually constant in every single census up to Lin’s own time, almost a century and a half later, at which time there were still counted 1631 *qing*. Rather than being the product of a disaster which really swallowed up a third of the county’s cultivated land, the steep drop in taxable property reflects a polite consensus reached between state and subjects, which neither saw fit to revise with more realistic surveys in the later course of the Ming. The tendency for tax rolls to drop in the Yongle reign and then “freeze” thereafter has been noted for virtually the entire Ming domain, but the undercounting was particularly extreme in Fujian, where both population and area of farmland probably increased steadily from the Hongwu reign on.   
The reasons for the state’s lack of interest or ability in resurveying Yongchun’s tax base to increase its revenues can be better understood by looking at the nature of that tax base. In Jiajing 1, of 1631 *qing* of land on the tax rolls, 1,488 were civilian farmlands, leaving less than 10% of arable land (111 *qing*) in the hands of the state.[^12]   
	If the under-registration of land by at least 1/3rd was extreme, it was actually conservative compared to the undercounting of population. The foundational 1392 census counted 4041 households (*hu* 戶) and 21,017 people (*kou* 口) in Yongchun, which was slashed to just 2093 households and 6670 people by the census of 1412: a ludicrous drop of two-thirds of the county’s population in the course of just 30 peaceful years. While the population registers showed more variation than the land registers, by Lin’s time Yongchun’s official population was a mere 6,376 people in 2,017 households.   
	In Lin’s commentary to the land tax section of the Yongchun gazetteer, he explains the drop in registered land as a consequence of population decrease, which caused land to be left fallow. He further relates that the perception of Yongchun having empty land led the court to establish military farm colonies (*tuntian*) there, only to cause strife and lawsuits with the locals, as it turned out the “fallow” land was in fact occupied. It is striking that Lin, even speaking as a county magistrate, chooses to defend the obviously inaccurate tax registers. It is clear that he shared a worldview in which maximizing the state’s tax revenues was less important than leaving the civilian populace to its own devices. Ye Chunji, writing slightly later as magistrate of neighboring Hui’an County, was even more frank in acknowledging the defects of the Ming system in this region: the tax burdens associated with registration of land have led people to flee the census takers and made the numbers for Hui’an an undercount by at least two thirds, and understandably so: “How can one blame the people?”[^13]   
	Lin and Ye’s defense of tax-dodging and under-registration were representative of the broad attitude of the bureaucratic class of their time, and reflected the deeper truth that Ming tax and population registers were never really intended to serve as accurate censuses. Instead, they crystallized a sort of minimum obligation to the state and parcelled that obligation out in return for legal recognition, bringing with it various benefits, particularly the right to sit for civil service exams. Legally registered, tax-paying landholders also tended to have an advantage in conflict over land or resources. Over time, the *lijia* “households” crystallized into artificial associations characterized by patronage relationships between the legal household head, their family dependents and tenants below them, and in many cases legally invisible outside investors above them.[^14]  
The relatively *laissez faire* position of the state’s agents towards its own main tax base does not, by any means, imply that rural dwellers thereby lived free from exploitation. One major reason not to credit the state’s figures for cultivated land and rural population is that Fujian was widely understood to suffer from land scarcity throughout the entire dynasty. By the mid-fifteenth century, the scarcity of land and the ongoing subjection of peasants to landlords broke it in a number of agrarian rebellions, which became a topic of intensive discussion in 20th century historiography. The most famous of these was the 1448-9 rebellion led by one Deng Maoqi, which Tanaka Masatoshi called “the earliest clear-cut example of tenants rising to oppose the payment of rent and to consciously assert a peasant-like claim to the land they cultivated.”[^15]   
Given the intense scarcity of land which in fact existed over land in Fujian, and the fact that the frozen tax registers meant the state reliably turned a blind eye to most land acquisition, the competition for any new sources of farmable land drove much of the ferment of society in these decades. There were two basic sources of new land in land-starved Minnan: claiming from others (particularly Buddhist temples) and claiming from the sea. Both of these strategies would be pioneered and protagonized above all by the corporate, single-surname lineage associations which first emerged in the Song Dynasty, but only came to truly dominate the Fujianese landscape in the course of the Ming. 

### **Dissolution of the Monasteries** 

In the Min Kingdom period and throughout the Song Dynasty, Buddhist (and to a lesser extent Daoist) monastic establishments had been among the dominant landholders in Fujian.   
The Hongwu emperor, himself a former Buddhist monk, sharply restricted the number, landholdings, and attached populations of the Buddhist temples, allowing just two temples and 40 monks in most prefectures, such that the earliest Ming censuses record monasteries holding less than 5% of Fujian’s land. Even this reduced patrimony came under attack by Confucian polemicists in the Zhengde and Jiajing reigns, and further large tracts of temple land were seized by the government to help pay for military expenses during the piracy crisis of the 1560s. Thus, by 1570 Fujian’s monasteries had almost been extirpated from the countryside, but the category of “temple land” remained an important (because newer, and thus not channeled to Beijing) source of local government revenues (both silver and grain). There is also substantial dispute and litigation over who should own the confiscated lands: former small tenants or gentry landlords.   
	Gu Yanwu summarized the Jiajing phase of temple land appropriation as follows: “First, in the Jiajing years, there were great public works projects which lacked funding, and so the people were encouraged to buy up temple land. In following years, Fujian was dealing with *wo*, and the military was short of funds, so the censors repeatedly memorialized “there are temples and monks,” by which they meant to give four-tenths of the land to monks while taking the other six-tenths for soldiers’ pay; this has been in place for a long time.”[^16]   
The Chongzhen-era gazetteer of Kaiyuan Temple in Quanzhou city narrates the decline of the *sangha* in dramatic turns: “By the Chenghua-Hongzhi years, the monks had again scattered to the four directions, and the institutions of the community slid into ruin. By the Longqing and Wanli reigns, the elders had passed away, and more than half of the abodes of the Buddha and monastic shelters were inhabited by lay people. Matters even reached the point where the ordination platform was occupied by gunpowder artisans, who slowly encroached even upon the Hall of Dharma, which they partitioned and possessed for some thirty years.”[^17]    
The decline in temple lands was particularly severe in Zhangzhou prefecture. This district had long been known as a “Kingdom of the Buddha,” with some 600 temples founded there between the Tang and Yuan; but in the Ming these were consolidated into just five temples holding lands assessed at an annual tax value of 2320 *shi*. Earlier in the dynasty monks had certain tax exemptions which made it attractive for people to surreptitiously register their lands as belonging to monasteries, often paying fees to the monks, but this loophole was eventually closed. After the Chenghua reign, active monastic lands were taxed at the same rates as normal civilian lands (though the *feisi* confiscated temple lands remained a separate category). After Jiajing 27 (1538) their tax status was actually worse than normal farmland, as a punitive silver surcharge was levied on all temple lands, and thereafter monks were frequently recorded as selling their *sangha*’s land to commoners.[^18]   
After this point legal disputes between monks and commoners became commonplace, as the complex nature of land rights in Fujian made it difficult to establish who precisely held rights to land which had been transferred. Gu notes that many transfers of land occurred when wealthy notables took land as collateral for predatory loans to cash-strapped monks, then moved some of their tenants onto the land thus usurped. Decades later, after these tenants had been long established on this land, conniving monks requested the lands back from the government, using various means to pressure the new tenants away.[^19] The potential for monastic lawfare existed because the various rounds of exactions never fully wiped out the monasteries as institutions, nor did they erase their long-standing claim to some of their former temple lands; so there was room for dispute around the boundaries of those lands.[^20]  
Among the most prominent critics of Buddhism in Fujian was Lin Xiyuan, whom we met previously as the compiler of the Yongchun gazetteer. Lin, in his official career, had devoted himself to the “destruction of heterodox temples,” and wrote several polemics against Buddhism in particular. For example, in his preface to the portion of his Yongchun gazetteer devoted to monasteries, Lin wrote that of the 68 Buddhist establishments which had existed in that county in the Song, only 16 remained: while “as for their land and grain, some went to the people. As for Daoist abbeys, shrines to local gods, and other heterodox temples, these are now being gradually destroyed by local worthies: some have been turned to academies, others to community schools. Here we see \[the difference between\] evil and righteousness, between flourishing and decline\! I’ve recorded their rise and destruction in this register while awaiting the gentlemen who will set matters right.”[^21] Lin was no disinterested observer in the carving-up of the : when he returned to Tong’an after being dismissed from office, he himself was granted land from an abolished temple by an admiring local magistrate. This land became the kernel of what became Lin’s lineage estate, granting him a firm foundation befitting his role as clan patriarch, and the rent income from it served as the principal for his substantial investments in business, including overseas smuggling. ### Lineages

Over the long term the monasteries were outcompeted, as were urban merchant-landlords, by rural lineages as the dominant form of organizing labor in the countryside. The functional similarity between lineages and monasteries as quasi-corporate institutions has been remarked on before. For example, Patricia Ebrey notes that Fan Zhongyan’s lineage estate, which was a charter model cited by many of the lineages which followed, was partially modeled on Buddhist monastic organization in its allotment of communal lands sufficient for maintenance of basic livelihood of all members along with subsidies for rituals and students, while allowing individual households to retain their own property. Lineage estates as survival and modification of the centralized labor organization model of the monasteries. Zheng Zhenman suggests that Song-era lineages were mostly built around temples and monasteries, and first split away from these in the Yuan and early Ming.[^22]   
While there were earlier antecedents, the lineage structure as it ultimately developed was tied to the specifics of the Ming legal and fiscal order. The rise of the corporate lineage was, as Liu Zhiwei has argued for Guangdong, a specific institutional adaptation to the conditions created by the mid-Ming fiscal regime. The *lijia* system, by binding tax obligation to registered household status, made that status a burdensome obligation, but also a resource providing recognition by state officials. This resource could be monopolized, traded, and deployed to claim legal recognition of landholding. The lineage, organized around a corporate estate and a shared ancestral hall, provided a mechanism for consolidating scattered families under a single registered “households,” pooling the tax obligations of numerous people under a single "household name," and thereby preserving access to the legal and social privileges that registration conferred. Selection of men to fill the quota of *ding* belonging to a given “household” occurred at the discretion of the lineage leader, giving that patriarchal figure an important tool to influence his subordinate clansmen.

The basic issue facing Zhangzhou was the poor fit between its economic structure (dependent on fishing and imported grain) and the agrarian fiscal base enforced by the Hongwu emperor. 

The *South Quanzhou Miscellany,* a record by the late Ming administrator Chen Maoren, describes the disputes surrounding a case of land reclamation in the Jiulong delta:   
“Egret Islet emerged from the ocean sands. Because it was seized on by local households as tax-free land, it was turned into school fields, with the tenants' rent funding oil and candles. Even so it was encroached on by powerful locals, almost causing an uprising. I was sent to inspect and pacify the farmers. It made me fantasize about retiring to the land, like Tao Yuanming in his country home, singing after throwing down my spade, carelessly following my own desires, never again participating in this realm of blowing dust where one watches the expressions of others.”[^24]   
	Clearly, Chen found the matter of ownership disputes in newly-reclaimed farmland mundane to the point of weariness. There is no sense that such cases were exceptional. What is most interesting here is the importance of the newly emerged land’s tax status: when it first appeared, it was “tax-free” (literally “hidden” *yin* 隱), making it attractive to local farmers, but it was seized by the state (*mo* 沒) in the category of “school fields,” which would be rented to tenants at high rates to fund local academies.

## Popular Silverization up to 1550

Eventually, the maritime economy would bring enormous quantities of silver into Zhangzhou and Quanzhou (and through them, to the rest of the empire), but this occurred only after silver became crucial to the economy of these places in a gradual process following the Ming founding. Silver had probably taken root as the preferred store and measure of value in the private economy of southern Chinese provinces by around 1450\. How exactly silver became so dominant, however, is not an easy process to track, as it mostly occurred in defiance of official restrictions of private use of non-sanctioned monetary media. Zhu Yuanzhang had attempted to enforce a  Qiu Yongzhi argues, following Kuroda Akinobu, that the real situation of the Ming economy was not defined by official policy, but by the conflict between this policy and the very diverse circulation of different currencies in the real markets. In the early Ming, the “shriveling” of markets and restriction of commerce due to the dislocation of the Yuan collapse and the harsh policies of Ming Taizu caused the currency system to become very unstable.  
	By contrast to the Southern Song, when private circulation of state-issued paper money became commonplace in conditions of robust commercial growth and ever-increasing demand for currency, the early Ming saw massive emission of paper notes into shrunken and uncertain markets where stability, security, and durability, rather than convenience of circulation, were at a premium. The use of *baochao* was primarily limited to circuits which had direct contact with the state apparati in Nanjing, Beijing, the Grand Canal, and the northern frontier. In distant Fujian, however, it appears that paper money never even temporarily displaced copper coin, and copper coins were an important export overseas; indeed, Minnan can be seen as part of a larger copper economy including Japan, Java, mainland Southeast Asia, and Guangdong.[^25] 

In the 1930s and 50s Fu Yiling published a series of preserved mid-Qing tenancy contracts from Fujian, which have served as the basis for speculations about Ming-era tenancy in Fujian as well. Rawski takes it that the basic structure of tenancy did not change much between the Ming and Qing. While this may be true of the standard contract structures governing tenancy, we are unfortunately unable to use the data from surviving Qing contracts to make inferences about price levels or even the actual currencies used to track or complete transactions. In theory, one might expect the flood of silver into the local economy of Zhangzhou and Quanzhou would increase the general price level in the region, perhaps particularly in land, where we are told that urban merchants preferentially poured their commercial profits, and there are some anecdotal statements indicating that land prices in southern Fujian, especially Zhangzhou, rose in silver terms over the 16th century. Unfortunately, the existing data on land prices in late Ming Fujian are too incomplete to responsibly estimate changes in price levels during our period of study. In the case of Minnan we simply lack anything like the voluminous data of the Huizhou land contracts analyzed by McDermott, Wan Ming, Kishimoto Mio, and many others to track the steady dominance of silver in large transactions like land sales in that heavily commercialized county, and can thus only make the broad generalization that silver appears to have been the dominant measure of value as well as actual means of payment in high-value commerce by no later than 1550\. This means that we have little hope of quantitatively defining clear stages in the development of Minnan’s silver economy, let alone of collating the sorts of price series which were once important in writing the history of Europe’s “commercial revolution.”   
Evelyn Rawski has written that by the 1570s Zhangzhou land prices were inflated to 2/3 times those elsewhere: “Land prices in Chang-chou reflected the competition for land. According to a 1572 report, land was selling for 8 liang of silver per mou, and the rich temple lands in Hai-ch'eng brought from 7 to 10 liang per mou. Land prices in Chienning during the same period ranged from 2 to 4 liang per mou, about a third of the Chang-chou prices.” However, the fact that Zhangzhou land prices were high by itself does not permit us to posit monetary "inflation;" among other reasons, the alluvial coastal lands around Haicheng were unusually productive once they had been sufficiently diked, certainly more so than the mountainous interior land Rawski cites (Jianning). We have also seen that tax categories dramatically impacted the market value of land, and that temple land was often particularly attractive. Densely populated and productive land near major urban centers, which enjoyed special tax exemptions, would be expected to be more expensive than land in impoverished rural uplands regardless of monetary conditions, so this comparison on its own does not clarify questions of whether silver caused any kind of “price revolution” in Zhangzhou. There has yet to be research conclusively moving beyond the qualitative impression that land was more expensive in terms of silver in Minnan than elsewhere.  
One more tractable means of estimating the penetration of commerce and money into Fujianese society is the number of markets noted in local gazetteers. Based on this data, Rawski shows that periodic markets in Zhangzhou increased from 11 in 1491, to 65 to 1628.[^26]

## Fiscal Transformations of the Mid-Ming 

A crucial watershed in Fujian’s silver policies occurred in 1520, with the reforms of the visiting censor (*xun’an yushi*) Shen Zhuo. At the time of Shen’s arrival in Fujian, only 3/10ths of tax rice were normally commuted into silver, but Shen memorialized to the throne that, since the taxes on state lands were higher, these should be permitted to send half of their tax as silver, while newly added and "floating" rice quotas should be commuted in full. Shen further introduced the “eight-*fen* policy” (*bafen fa* 八分法), which was entirely unique to Fujian, and involved levying all taxpayers surcharges at a fixed rate of 8 *fen* of silver per *shi* of rice or adult male. This silver would be delivered to government offices, where officials would use it to purchase the goods of the *chaogong* tribute demanded by the capital (including silks, tea, spices, minerals, animal skins, and so on) on the market; after 1548 the transportation of those materials was also undertaken by government employees rather than being arranged via corvee as it had in the past.[^29] In 1528 the *yushi* Nie Bao reduced this levy to 6 *fen*, but it otherwise seems to have been kept intact as a full replacement for the previous corvee duty of procuring court tribute.   
In fact, Shen Zhuo was not the first to replace in-kind tribute with silver payments in Fujian: according to the *Bamin Tongzhi* (1489), it was already common practice in some parts of the province to levy silver to pay for military equipment that the *weisuo* garrisons no longer produced themselves (軍器料銀), but this practice seems to have been restricted to the garrison context before Shen’s efforts. In line with this, various Fujianese counties had already drawn up silver-denominated budgetary regulations beginning in the Chenghua reign, primarily to avoid excessive spending. These budgets were combined and extended under Shen Zhuo, again in his comprehensive reforms of 1520, in what became known as the “regulated silver policy” (*gangyin fa* 綱銀法), though these budgets were repeatedly revised to incorporate new silver charges, iron out deficiencies, and solidify faithful accounting in practice, as miscellaneous expenses came increasingly to be merged into a single silver-denominated levy.[^30]  
Further reforms involved the transformations of the corvee levies themselves, particularly those belonging to the category of “equalized corvee,” which had originally been introduced around the empire in the 1440s-80s as an attempt to split up the originally highly irregular demands for corvee to ensure greater “equality.”[^31] Around 1537 the *xun’an yushi* Li Yuanyang 李元阳 implemented the “ten-part policy” (*shiduan fa*十段法), which involved calculating the proper land and labor tax quota for an entire county, dividing it by 10, and charging that year's taxpaying *jia* for their share in silver, with which the government would hire laborers and buy materials. This was a way to equalize taxes while giving local government greater oversight and control over the collection and distribution of taxes.   
As Li Bao notes, however, local government oversight over funds was not necessarily an untrammeled positive, at least from the perspective of the central treasuries. Once tributes had been converted to silver, officials were tempted to shift funds from one basket to another (or simply steal them); it was often the reformers themselves who gave in to such temptations (for example, the *xun’an yushi* Nie Bao began the practice of using *bafen* silver for local government expenses instead of allocating it solely to the capital tribute.[^32] This dynamic, of silver streams being relatively vulnerable to embezzlement or diversion for local purposes, made “corruption” into a constant theme of anti-silverization polemics well into the Qing Dynasty. While pure individual venality was certainly a problem, however, “corruption” of this sort was just as often a measure for under-funded local administrations to use the resources at hand to handle their various urgent responsibilities, a problem which would become particularly acute in periods of military unrest, such as with the tenant revolts and “mining banditry” of the 1440s and again with the major pirate incursions of the 1560s.[^33] Thus, the fungibility of silver and the ease with which it could be diverted along numerous channels would become a major problem for the reformers who sought to complete Fujian’s system of fiscal silverization, especially after the establishment of the substantial new revenue stream of legalized maritime customs in 1567\.   
By the 1540s, then, Fujian already possessed many of the traits associated with the later Single Whip reforms: the land tax, labor corvee, and in-kind tributes had been substantially amalgamated into silver payments, which were divided equally and levied on individual taxpaying households based on their assessed land value and number of adult males, and these payments passed through local silver treasuries before being forwarded to the imperial treasuries or disbursed locally. However, this system had not yet been regularized, and there remained a great deal of uncertainty about the permanence of these measures, as well as their applicability to different government budgets. Further, the amounts of silver collected after this round of reforms remained very small, being tied to the Yongle-era land and population registers, which as we have seen were hopelessly out of date and left almost nothing for the provincial administrators. The system nonetheless appears to have been adequate for the provisioning of basic government needs up until the explosion of provincial budgets occasioned by warfare against coastal raiders beginning in the late 1550s, which inaugurated a new round of thoroughgoing reforms. Both the impetus for these reforms and the major solutions would come in large part from the sea.

## **The Sea** 

While the preceding sections have focused on the land economy and its relationship to taxation, the most distinctive feature of Minnan's society was its closeness to the sea. The crowded coastal plains around Xiamen Bay could not feed themselves; the weisuo garrisons which were supposed to defend them could not maintain themselves; the fiscal system which governed them could not collect enough revenue in the products of the land alone. Each of these structural deficiencies pushed the people and institutions of Minnan towards the ocean, where fishing, salt production, coastal shipping, and eventually long-distance overseas trade provided resources that the land could not. By the mid-sixteenth century, the maritime economy had become so thoroughly entangled with every aspect of Minnanese life that the state's attempts to regulate or suppress it were not merely ineffective but actively destabilizing. Understanding the development of this maritime world, and the institutional structures which both constrained and enabled it, is essential to grasping why the wokou crisis of the 1560s proved so transformative.
The limited sources we have from the 15th century generally give the impression that the interior counties of Minnan were growing and thriving, while the coast stagnated in a sort of genteel poverty. By the mid-16th century, however, we see a flurry of activity all along the coasts, expressed in land reclamation (the Jiulong delta), competition for prime land, newly rising towns, an increase in periodic markets, and a rising number of successful exam candidates. There is little doubt that this revitalization of the coasts was connected towards the region's embrace of overseas trade after 1500. 

### The Weisuo and the Coast

The Ming state's primary institutional presence along the Fujianese coast was the network of military garrisons known as the weisuo. Established in the Hongwu reign as part of the founding emperor's vision of a self-sustaining military order, the weisuo were intended to serve simultaneously as defensive installations and self-sufficient agrarian communities: garrison troops would farm allocated lands in peacetime, producing their own food and supplies without burdening the civilian tax base, while remaining ready for mobilization in emergencies. In practice, the system was poorly suited to Fujian's coastal geography from the start. The narrow, sandy strips of land available near most coastal garrison sites were insufficient to support the assigned troop complements, and the proximity of the sea offered more attractive livelihoods than coaxing rice from marginal soils.[^34]

Fujian's weisuo were distinctive in one important respect: unlike the garrisons of the northern frontier or the Yangtze valley, which were largely staffed by soldiers transferred from other regions, Fujian's coastal garrisons drew heavily on local populations. While some effort was made to station soldiers outside their home counties in accordance with the "avoidance" principle, the practical result was that Fujianese weisuo were deeply embedded in local society from their inception. This had consequences in both directions. On one hand, it meant that garrison troops maintained family and commercial ties to the surrounding civilian population, facilitating the gradual blurring of military and civilian identities that historians have termed "civilianization" (民化). On the other, it meant that the decline of the weisuo as military institutions was simultaneously a transformation of their social and economic roles: as soldiers ceased to function as soldiers, they did not simply disappear, but became fishermen, traders, and tenants, while the garrison installations themselves became nuclei around which commercial settlements grew. \[Cite Yang Peina's discussion of weisuo civilianization and commercialization.\]

Among the most important populations enrolled into the weisuo system were the fishing and saltern households of the coast. These specialized households, registered in separate categories from ordinary civilian taxpayers, were obligated to deliver fixed quotas of fish and salt to the state in lieu of the grain and labor owed by agricultural households. In the early Ming, this arrangement placed fishing and saltern communities under direct state supervision, tying them to specific coastal locations and restricting their mobility. Over time, however, the state's capacity to manage these populations eroded, and control over their labor and output shifted to private intermediaries. Local gentry figures — often heads of large lineages who held registered household status and thus formal standing within the fiscal system — inserted themselves as middlemen between the fishing and saltern populations and the officials who nominally governed them. These intermediaries collected the quotas owed to the state while extracting additional rents and fees for themselves, effectively converting a public obligation into a private revenue stream. The fishing and saltern households thus found themselves doubly burdened: still formally liable for their state quotas, but now also subordinated to gentry patrons whose "protection" was as coercive as it was indispensable.

The extension of the junyao equalized labor levy system to weisuo garrisons marked a critical turning point in this process. When garrison populations lost their separate tax status and were assessed alongside civilian households, the institutional distinction between military and civilian identities effectively collapsed. Weisuo troops who had long since ceased to drill or patrol were now counted as ordinary taxpayers, and the garrisons themselves became administrative fictions overlaid on thriving commercial communities. The irony was that the very populations which the state had originally enrolled to defend the coast had become, by the mid-sixteenth century, among its most active maritime traders — and, in some cases, its most troublesome smugglers.

### Grain, Fish, and the Freedom of Shipping

The fundamental constraint shaping life around Xiamen Bay was the region's chronic inability to feed itself. The narrow coastal plain, however densely cultivated, could not produce enough grain for its growing population, and Minnan depended on imports — above all rice shipped from Guangdong, Guangxi, and the Jiangnan region — to make up the difference. This dependence on imported grain made freedom of coastal shipping not merely an economic convenience but an existential necessity. Any serious disruption to the flow of grain ships into Xiamen Bay's harbors threatened famine, and the populations which crewed these ships, maintained the harbor infrastructure, and brokered the grain trade wielded a corresponding degree of informal power.

At the same time, the products of the sea — salt and dried fish above all — were among Minnan's most valuable exports, traded coastwise and inland in exchange for the grain the region could not grow. Salt smuggling, in particular, was endemic along the Fujianese coast, where the official salt monopoly was both difficult to enforce and widely resented. The geography of the coast itself militated against effective control: as Lin Renchuan has observed, the waters near the market town of Yuegang, nestled among the inlets and islets of the Jiulong River's seaward reach, were extraordinarily difficult for the government to monitor, and this topographic advantage was one reason Yuegang emerged as a major center of smuggling from the Xuande reign onwards. The same coastal geography which made smuggling easy also supported a thriving fishing industry, and the line between fishing, coastal trading, salt smuggling, and outright piracy was blurred in practice: the same boats, the same crews, and often the same voyages combined elements of all four activities depending on opportunity and season.

### Into the Outer Seas

If domestic coastal shipping was vital for Minnan's survival, the growth of long-distance overseas trade in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries transformed the region's economic horizons entirely. The trajectory of Fujianese maritime expansion in the early-to-mid Ming is difficult to trace in detail, precisely because it was almost entirely illegal under the dynasty's prohibition on private overseas voyaging. Nevertheless, the evidence that Hokkien merchants and sailors were active throughout Southeast Asia by the late fifteenth century is substantial. Fu Yiling dates the expansion of Fujianese merchants from domestic to overseas trade to the Chenghua-Hongzhi transition (circa 1465-1505), preceding by decades any significant European stimulus; by the time Portuguese ships reached the waters of Southeast Asia in the 1510s, they found well-established communities of Fujianese traders in Malacca, Pattani, and elsewhere.

One revealing piece of evidence for the extent of Minnan mariners' knowledge comes from the account of Chen Kan, a Ming official dispatched on an embassy to the Ryukyu Kingdom. Chen noted that Zhangzhou sailors were far preferred for the voyage over the Fuzhou-based mariners who were theoretically responsible for managing the tributary relationship, because the Zhangzhou men understood the sea routes far better. This preference speaks to a straightforward reality: private Minnan sailors had been regularly navigating the waters between the Chinese coast, Taiwan, and the Ryukyu islands for years, acquiring an expertise in deep-sea sailing that the state's own naval personnel lacked. That even representatives of the imperial court were willing to overlook the legal implications of employing these experienced — and therefore necessarily lawbreaking — sailors testifies to how thoroughly the *haijin* had been hollowed out in practice by the early sixteenth century.

The earliest definite records of Fujianese trading missions to Japan date to the 1530s, apparently originating as extensions of the Ryukyu route, and by the 1540s such voyages appear to have been routine. Meanwhile, the southern routes connecting Minnan to Vietnam, the Malay world, and the spice islands continued to develop, with Fujianese merchants trading local products — ceramics from the kilns of Dehua and Anxi, silk, sugar, and ironware — for the aromatics, pepper, tin, and other tropical commodities which had been the staples of Quanzhou's commerce since the Song dynasty. By mid-century, the northeastern route via Ryukyu to Japan and the southwestern route via the South China Sea to Southeast Asia were both vitally important to the livelihoods of coastal households.

### The Shadow of Piracy

The very success of Minnan's maritime expansion carried within it the seeds of violent conflict. Long-distance trade in the absence of legal protection required armed escorts, fortified bases, and willingness to use force against competitors — requirements which made the line between "merchant" and "pirate" functionally meaningless. The same gentry lineages which financed trading voyages also maintained the armed retinues necessary to protect them, and the same sailors who crewed merchant vessels in one season might join a raiding party in the next. Organized maritime violence was not new to the Fujianese coast — piracy had been a recurring feature of life in the Taiwan Strait since at least the Jin dynasty — but the scale and sophistication of armed maritime organizations grew in tandem with the expansion of overseas trade itself.

By the mid-Jiajing reign, the accumulated pressures of illegal but indispensable maritime commerce, decayed coastal garrisons, assertive gentry networks, and a central government increasingly aware of and alarmed by conditions in the southeast were converging towards crisis. The immediate trigger came from outside Fujian, when the suppression of major pirate-merchant networks based in the Zhoushan islands of Zhejiang (most notably that of Wang Zhi) displaced large numbers of armed maritime operators southwards along the coast. But the ground on which these displaced "wokou" landed had been prepared by decades of institutional decay and commercial growth in Minnan itself. The state's first major attempt to reassert control over these waters — the ill-fated campaign of the commissioner Zhu Wan in the late 1540s — ended in Zhu's political destruction and suicide in 1550, a stark demonstration that the local interests aligned behind maritime trade were powerful enough to bring down a determined official backed by the full authority of the central government. Zhu Wan's fate proved that the Ming state could not simply suppress Fujian's maritime economy; it would have to find some way to accommodate it. How that accommodation was eventually achieved, under the extreme pressures of the wokou crisis of the following decade, is the subject of the next chapter.

### Conclusion

The first two centuries of the Ming Dynasty were outwardly quiet ones for Minnan, but over that time a number of slow transformations prepared the way for the region's explosion to prominence thereafter. The reconsolidation of most land under tightly-controlled lineages operating with complex networks of layered ownership, the subordination of powerful competing groups like Buddhist monasteries, and likewise the assertion of rural rights to land use against urban moneyed classes, set the stage for the dominance of a traditionalist, educated half-rural gentry combining in commerce, agriculture, and study. The decline of Quanzhou city furthered this dynamic by causing population to diffuse more evenly throughout the coastal lowlands and weakening dependence on a central metropole. Population was pulled towards the coast by burgeoning exploitation of the ocean for salt, fish, grain, and foreign trade, again organized under capitalistic lineages. By 1500 the economic center of the region had shifted definitively towards the rim of Xiamen Bay, with Zhangzhou playing a substantially greater role than in the Song and Yuan. The private economy had become thoroughly monetized, using a dizzying variety of bronze coins as well as silver, while the local state had also taken steps towards fully silverizing its finances. The combination of lineage power, private monetization, the commercial boom on the coasts, the death of the weisuo, and a mobile and growing rural population caused the reality of Minnan life to slip almost entirely out of the grasp of the state, which remained reliant on badly outdated tax registers. The inability of the state to adequately assess or manage local life was revealed in the pirate crisis of the 1550s and 60s, which revealed how closely imbricated the whole area had become with international maritime trade. 

The end of the first phase of localized anti-piracy activities in Minnan with the death of Zhu Wan left Fujian poised in an ambiguous position. Its fiscal structure was partially silverized but its tax burdens fell unevenly on a population and economy which bore almost no resemblance to official tax registers. Men like Lin Xiyuan represented a coalescing gentry elite whose power was based on a combination of landed wealth, control over the labor of their lineage organizations, degree- and office-holding, and substantial liquid (silver) capital deployed in overseas trade. This elite was especially prominent along the Xiamen Bay, where its members could engage in coastal smuggling, large-scale salt and fishing enterprises, and land reclamation. This was the landscape which would be ripe for reform after the irruption of “wokou” fleets into the region after 1560 forced the central state to more closely scrutinize and adapt itself to local conditions in what was just then emerging, for the first time in the Ming, as an area of paramount importance in the empire as a whole.   

My second chapter will cover the emergence after 1540 of an empire-wide rhetoric of silverizing reform which the Fujian case in some respects paralleled, but in others diverged from, in order to better understand the distinctiveness of Fujian’s fiscal system by the final years of the Jiajing reign (1521-66). Thereafter my core chapters will focus on three phases of state accommodation with the silver economy of Minnan, in each case departing from the perspective of the *xunfu* as the highest provincial official: first the 1560s, when Tan Lun, Wang Daokun, and Tu Zemin (alongside Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou) battled "wokou" pirates, enacted comprehensive silver taxation to fund a professionalized military structure, and successfully petitioned for Haicheng to be made the Ming empire's only legal port of private export trade; then Liu Yaohui's tenure and his combined pursuit of a "forward defense" strategy and resolution of the thorniest fiscal problems through retrenchment and embrace of centralized maritime customs; and finally Pang Shangpeng's and Geng Dingxiang's only partially successful attempts to implement the Single Whip and "qingzhang" land resurveys which are normally seen as the core components of Zhang Juzheng's reform agenda. Finally, I will conclude by surveying developments in Minnan’s political economy after 1585, focusing on the stabilization of a system of trade centered on exchange between Haicheng (Zhangzhou) and Anping (Quanzhou) with Manila and Japan, before studying the challenges facing that system after 1620, when it was slowly consolidated under the rule of a single especially powerful coastal clan, the “Zheng regime” of Zheng Zhilong, which in many respects resolved the thorniest contradictions between Ming state and Minnan society by absorbing the key functions of both into one corporate quasi-state.
