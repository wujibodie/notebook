
“Fujian’s land is sparse, while its people are many;” “just a strip of terrace between mountain and sea;” “narrow and dense;” such evaluations are commonplace in writings about Fujian from any time in the past millennium. Just as frequent a truism is the claim that due to this shortage of land, Min people must “plow the sea;” they “take foreign countries as their fields;” talented sons are forced to “abandon scholarship and pursue trade.” The connection between Fujian’s environmental conditions and its culture of seafaring has been made time and again, and I don’t intend here to reinvent this particular wheel. That said, if Minnan was always a crowded stretch of coastal plain, the details of the relationship between its numerous people and its ever-insufficient land nevertheless changed greatly over time, with wide-ranging consequences for Minnan society. 

It may seem obvious to center a study of agrarian China on the matter of arable land. But Minnan was exceptional within the broader Chinese world precisely because it went hundreds of years living beyond the means of its self-sustaining agrarian economy, and was nevertheless able to flourish as one of the leading regions of the empire in trade, culture, and education. The uniqueness of a region so thoroughly devoted to the “branch” occupations (crafts and trade) over the “root” (farming) helps explain many peculiarities in the region’s institutional and political history, not to say some of the mutual incomprehension between Minnan’s natives and the court and officials ruling over them. Central to this incomprehension was the misfit between the Ming’s basic fiscal system, which demanded tribute largely in the form of rice and cloth, and the realities of Minnan’s economy, which imported both. Not only did state exactions actually force Minnan’s people to engage more deeply with the commercial economy which Hongwu’s system sought to suppress in order to pay their dues, but the obvious absurdity and hardships imposed on the region made it into one of the most important laboratories for fiscal reform, and particularly for the commutation of taxes into silver, an area in which Minnan took a highly distinctive path from other commercialized regions. 

The fiscal problems facing Zhang-Quan were rather different from those of neighboring provinces, in part for reasons dating to its relatively late and quick incorporation into the Ming dynasty. South Fujian was something of an afterthought in the early Ming; far from the centers of power, not the site of any of founding emperor Zhu Yuanzhang’s personal campaigns, left out of the narrowly-channeled maritime system (which privileged Guangzhou, Fuzhou, and Ningbo as shipbuilding arsenals and sites for tribute trade), there is little sign that the first Ming emperors cared to adjust their grand policies for the sake of this far-flung rocky cove. The fall of the Yuan was a wrenching period for urban Quanzhou in particular (as will be discussed in detail below), and there is every sign that Minnan around 1400 was still a rather desolate place far from recovering its former glories. However, Fujian was spared the worst of the fighting in the Yuan collapse, and did not experience the large-scale requisition of land that characterized the develop Jiangnan region, Zhu Yuanzhang’s initial base, or the Yellow River provinces, where the conquering dynasty took direct control over what had been large Mongol noble estates. 


One of the most pressing questions for this study is that of how precisely the rapid influx of silver into Minnan after about 1530 changed the balance of power in local society, particularly as regards the price ratios between land and labor, the composition and material interests of landholders (it would be misleading in these conditions to talk of a "landlord class”), the strategies of local elites for social advancement, and the role of cities in the broader economy. In a society as dense and commercialized as late Ming Minnan, land tended to change hands rapidly, and by the standards of even other parts of southern China estates were very small; a family which owned 5 or 10 *qing* was regarded as wealthy. 

From as early as the 11th century Minnan had been a net importer of grain, and reliance on rice imports increased as commercial prosperity recovered after 1500; Minnan was thus a region where almost no families existed in the rustic self-sufficiency envisioned by the Ming founder, with most farming being done for the market. 

The period of internal unrest misleadingly labeled under the “wokou invasions” (in reality, Minnan was largely predated upon by Minnanese, along with neighbors from Guangdong) brought about deep-seated changes in Minnan’s culture and political economy. Perhaps most important was the reorganization of local fiscal and military structures after the weisuo garrisons proved entirely moribund and incapable of defending anything beyond their own walls. The organization of lineage and place-based militias under gentry leadership strengthened the organizing role of prominent families and their paternal heads, while the lean, professionalized strike corps led by new military men like Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou were paid in silver which local officials were forced to levy from the agrarian economy, producing lasting changes in the region’s fiscal structure and solidifying silver’s place as dominant monetary medium. Along with these changes came a wide-ranging reorganization of land tenure, as disfavored or insolvent landholders (such as Buddhist temples and garrisons) had their properties redistributed to tax-paying farmers, though the majority of land probably remained insulated from taxation by the layers of private ownership involved in the multiple tenureship system typical of Minnan.
